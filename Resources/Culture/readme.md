# Culture, History, Geography and Society

> **TL;DR**
> - This is the repo's reference chapter on Japan itself - history, geography, social structure, etiquette, work, religion, food, pop culture, and what it is actually like to live there as a foreigner.
> - It matters for language study because Japanese grammatically encodes social relationships. You cannot pick a verb form without knowing who is senior, who is inside your group, and who is listening. Register, honorifics and implicature are cultural facts before they are grammatical ones.
> - Most comprehension failures at intermediate level are cultural, not lexical. You know every word in the sentence and still miss the point.
> - Start with the [history](#chapter-1---history) and [geography](#chapter-2---geography) chapters once, for a map. Then read [social structure](#chapter-4---social-structure-in-the-language) properly, because it is the chapter that changes how you hear dialogue.
> - This folder holds a substantial offline library, organised into sub-folders: scholarly history (MIT Visualizing Cultures), religion as actually practised (the *Japanese Journal of Religious Studies* archive), society and contested history, anime and manga studies, otaku subculture and doujin/fandom, idols and VTubers, games and visual novels, theatre and aesthetics, festivals and folklore, the NINJAL dialect atlas, the Japanese classical canon from Aozora Bunko, Japanese government white papers, and geography, prefecture and holiday data. See the [resource table](#in-this-repo-offline), and [`sources.md`](<sources.md>) for provenance and licences.
> - Two caveats the resource table repeats per item, because both are easy to walk into. First, the older English-language "classics" here (Hearn, Nitobe, Chamberlain, Mitford) are **pre-1930 primary sources, not current scholarship** - read them as evidence of how Japan was presented to the West, and read the actual scholarship for what happened. Nitobe's *Bushido* in particular is a foundational *nihonjinron* text rather than samurai history. Second, several Japanese texts here are **classical Japanese (古文), which is a different language variety, not merely harder Japanese** - a modern-Japanese learner cannot read 方丈記 or 土佐日記 in the original no matter how much vocabulary they have. Where a 現代語訳 exists it is provided alongside.

## What this is and why it matters

This guide owns the non-linguistic half of learning Japanese: the country, its past, its social machinery, and the practical business of living in it. Every other folder in `Resources/` owns a skill. This one owns the context that makes those skills interpretable.

The case for spending time here is not "culture is enriching". It is four specific, concrete arguments.

**1. Japanese encodes social relationships in the morphology, so you cannot produce a sentence without a social judgement.**

In English you can say "he said he would come" to your boss, your friend, your grandmother and a stranger, and the sentence does not change. In Japanese the same content forces a series of choices:

| Choice | Options | Determined by |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Sentence ending | 来ると言った / 来ると言いました / 来ると申しておりました | Who you are speaking **to** |
| Verb of saying | 言う / おっしゃる / 申す | Who the **subject** is, relative to you and the listener |
| Reference to the person | 彼 / 田中さん / 田中部長 / 田中 | Their position, and whose side they are on |
| Whether to mention them at all | explicit subject / omitted | Shared context |

There is no neutral default. ですます is not "polite Japanese", it is one register among several, and using it with a close friend communicates distance just as clearly as using だ with a client communicates contempt. Grammar can teach you the forms ([Grammar](<../Grammar/readme.md>) owns the machinery: 尊敬語, 謙譲語, 丁寧語, the お〜になる and お〜する patterns). Nothing in a grammar book tells you *which* to pick, because that is a social question. This guide answers the social question.

**2. Your comprehension failures at intermediate level will mostly be cultural.**

At beginner level you fail because of vocabulary. Look up the word, understand the sentence. That stops working around the point where you can read a page of a novel or follow a slice-of-life episode. From there the typical failure looks like this: you understood every word, you parsed the grammar, and you have no idea why the character reacted that way.

Concrete examples of sentences whose literal meaning is not their meaning:

- ちょっと難しいですね - literally "that's a little difficult". Actual meaning: no.
- 考えておきます - literally "I'll think about it". Actual meaning, in a business context: no.
- また今度 - literally "next time". Actual meaning: there is no next time.
- 空気読めよ - literally "read the air". Actual meaning: you have just violated a norm that nobody was going to state out loud.
- そうですね... with a trailing pause - a disagreement wearing the costume of agreement.
- 結構です - can mean "yes please" or "no thank you" depending on context and intonation, which is a genuinely hard problem for learners.

None of these are idioms you can memorise your way out of. They are the surface of a system where explicit refusal is costly, so refusal moves into hesitation, hedging and omission. Learn the system and the sentences become transparent.

**3. What is left unsaid carries load.**

Japanese drops arguments that English requires. Subjects, objects and topics are routinely absent, recovered from context. Sentences trail off with 〜んですけど… or 〜ので… and the listener completes them. Conversation runs on 相槌 (aizuchi) - the continuous stream of はい, ええ, そうですか, うん that signals attention. A learner who treats these as filler and stays silent reads as disengaged or hostile, which is a cultural error expressed as a listening habit. ([Listening](<../Listening/readme.md>) owns aizuchi as a comprehension and production skill; this guide owns why the language is built to require them.)

**4. If you intend to live there, this material has direct consequences.**

Visa categories, rental guarantors, how a decision gets made in a Japanese office, what お疲れ様です means and when ご苦労様です is an insult, whether your employer expects you at the 飲み会 - these are not colour. They determine whether your first year is workable. No amount of grammar study substitutes.

## Where it fits in the journey

**Prerequisites: almost none.** This is the one topic in the repo you can start on day one, in English, before you know a single kana. Most of Part II is readable with zero Japanese.

**But it has a sequencing trap.** Culture reading in English is enjoyable, feels productive, and produces no Japanese ability whatsoever. It is the single most seductive form of study-shaped procrastination available to a beginner, precisely because it is genuinely interesting and genuinely useful. Treat it as a small fixed budget, not an activity that expands to fill available time.

**What it unlocks:**

| Unlocks | How |
| :--- | :--- |
| Keigo that makes sense | Honorific choice is unlearnable as a list and obvious as a system. [Chapter 4](#chapter-4---social-structure-in-the-language) is the system. |
| Non-literal comprehension | Recognising refusals, hedges and implicature instead of translating them literally |
| Correct register in output | Knowing which of five ways to say something fits the room ([Speaking](<../Speaking/readme.md>)) |
| Higher-value immersion | The same anime episode teaches more when you can see which parts are stylised |
| Place-name and kanji reading | Geography is a large, high-frequency, irregular-reading vocabulary domain ([Kanji](<../Kanji/readme.md>)) |
| Media context | Seasonal references, festivals, school-year structure and holiday furniture appear constantly ([Reading](<../Reading/readme.md>), [AJATT](<../AJATT/readme.md>)) |
| A survivable relocation | [Chapter 6](#chapter-6---work-culture) and [Chapter 10](#chapter-10---being-a-foreigner-long-term) |

**Rough placement on a timeline:**

```
Kana ---- Grammar core ---- Intermediate ---- Advanced ---- Living there
  |            |                  |               |              |
  Ch 1-2    Ch 4-5             Ch 3, 7-9       Ch 1 deep       Ch 6, 10
 (1 week)  (light, weekly)    (via immersion)  (real books)   (intensive)
```

The practical layer ([Chapter 6](#chapter-6---work-culture), [Chapter 10](#chapter-10---being-a-foreigner-long-term)) is the exception to "start early". It goes stale, and reading about Japanese pension rules three years before you move is wasted effort. Read it once now for shape, then properly twelve to eighteen months out.

## The schools of thought

Learners disagree sharply about whether culture is a subject or a side effect. Four positions, honestly stated.

### 1. Deliberate and early - culture as its own subject

Study it explicitly, in English, alongside the language. Read history, read about social structure, read etiquette guides, treat it as a syllabus.

**Pros**
- Prevents a specific, predictable class of error. You will not need to discover uchi/soto by offending a client.
- Makes keigo learnable. Honorific tables are memorisation; the in-group/out-group principle is one idea that generates the tables.
- Front-loads the interpretive frame, so immersion input is more informative from the start rather than accumulating silent misunderstandings.
- Essential rather than optional if you have a concrete relocation or employment goal.
- Corrects an anime-derived model early, before it fossilises into habits.

**Cons**
- Reading about Japan in English is not Japanese practice. Zero hours of it improve your grammar.
- Produces confident wrong models. The "Japan explained" genre is full of over-generalisation, and a learner who has read three books about Japanese uniqueness is often *worse* calibrated than one who has read none (see [nihonjinron](#the-honest-critique---nihonjinron-and-over-generalisation)).
- Highly susceptible to becoming the whole of your study.
- Much of the practical material decays. Visa and tax details read at the wrong time are just noise.

**Best for:** people with a relocation or work goal; people whose only exposure is fiction; people who want honorifics to make sense rather than be memorised.

### 2. Pure immersion absorption

Do not study culture. Consume enormous volumes of Japanese media and let the culture arrive attached to the language, the way a child acquires it.

**Pros**
- Culture arrives contextualised, in Japanese, without a translation filter. You learn what a situation *feels* like, not what an English writer says it means.
- No risk of memorising a stereotype, because you are learning from primary sources.
- Every hour spent is also a Japanese hour. Nothing is displaced.
- Aligns perfectly with the [AJATT](<../AJATT/readme.md>) approach already documented in this repo.
- Teaches the things explicit study is worst at: pacing, tone, what an awkward silence sounds like, how a real apology is delivered.

**Cons**
- Media is genre-skewed and stylised, so your model is systematically biased in a direction you cannot see from inside. An anime-only diet gives you a specific, reproducible set of wrong beliefs.
- You absorb register errors along with everything else, and character speech patterns are the *least* transferable thing in the medium.
- Silent misunderstanding compounds. Nothing corrects you.
- Fiction covers almost none of the material in [Chapter 2](#chapter-2---geography), [Chapter 6](#chapter-6---work-culture) or [Chapter 10](#chapter-10---being-a-foreigner-long-term). No amount of anime teaches you about residence status or 住民税.
- Slow. You can watch a thousand hours and still not have noticed the honorific inversion in [Chapter 4](#chapter-4---social-structure-in-the-language) as a *rule*.

**Best for:** people whose goal is media consumption; intermediate-and-above learners with genuinely broad input including non-fiction.

### 3. Learn it on arrival

Do not prepare. Move, or visit, and let the environment teach you. Mistakes are the curriculum.

**Pros**
- Highest fidelity. Reality is not stylised and does not over-generalise.
- Feedback is unavoidable and immediate.
- Fastest possible route through the practical layer, because you are doing it rather than reading about it.
- Cuts through the whole nihonjinron problem. You meet individuals, not a national character.

**Cons**
- You make the expensive mistakes at the worst possible moment - the job interview, the landlord meeting, the first week on a team. First impressions in a hierarchical workplace are durable.
- You have the least Japanese exactly when you need the most social skill.
- Culture shock without a framework is much worse than culture shock with one. You will misread ordinary politeness as coldness, or ordinary indirectness as dishonesty.
- Some things you will simply never be corrected on, because nobody will tell you.

**Best for:** people already living there; people going on a program with institutional support (exchange, JET, a company relocation package with a handler).

### 4. Ignore it - pure language mechanics

Culture is not a skill. Grind grammar, kanji and vocabulary. Read. Pass the test. The rest is noise.

**Pros**
- Maximum efficiency toward measurable goals: JLPT levels, reading speed, kanji count.
- Intellectually honest about the fact that culture study does not improve your parsing.
- Avoids the entire stereotype hazard by declining to form a model.
- Perfectly adequate for a large real population - people who read Japanese novels, translate technical documents, or play untranslated games.

**Cons**
- Your comprehension plateaus in a way you cannot diagnose, because your failures are pragmatic and you are only ever debugging lexis and syntax.
- Your output is register-blind. This is worse than being ungrammatical: a flawless casual sentence directed at a superior is a real social error, whereas broken polite Japanese is charming.
- Useless if you intend to live or work there.
- You will still absorb a cultural model - just an accidental one, from whatever media you happen to consume, with no awareness that it is a model.

**Best for:** people with a narrow reading or academic goal and no intention of social or professional interaction.

## Recommended path (opinionated)

**Pick 1 + 2, weighted toward 2, with 3 deferred and 4 rejected.**

Concretely: a small, capped, deliberate budget of explicit culture study in English - front-loaded in the first month, then roughly **thirty to sixty minutes a week** - running alongside high-volume immersion that you learn to read critically. Make the budget explicitly subordinate: fit it in after that day's Anki reviews and immersion block, in whatever time is left, and skip it entirely on weeks when nothing is left. The practical layer ([Chapter 6](#chapter-6---work-culture), [Chapter 10](#chapter-10---being-a-foreigner-long-term)) gets skimmed once now and studied properly close to the move.

**The reasoning:**

Schools 2 and 4 share a hidden assumption - that culture is either automatic or irrelevant. Both are wrong in the same place. The uchi/soto honorific inversion in [Chapter 4](#chapter-4---social-structure-in-the-language) is one paragraph of explanation that reorganises a hundred hours of otherwise confusing input. Refusing to read that paragraph on purity grounds is not efficiency, it is superstition. There are perhaps a dozen such paragraphs in this guide. Reading them takes an afternoon and pays out for years.

Conversely, school 1 taken alone fails for a reason that should worry anyone with an engineering habit of mind: **the explicit-study path optimises for having a model, and the models available in English are bad.** They are old, they are over-generalised, they were largely written when Japan's economic success made "Japanese uniqueness" a lucrative publishing category, and they describe salarymen at large firms in the 1970s and 80s as though that were the population. Immersion is the corrective, because primary sources show you variation that summaries flatten.

School 3 is not a strategy, it is a consequence. Everyone who moves does school 3 whether they planned to or not. The question is only how much framework you bring to it. Bring some.

**Who should ignore this recommendation:**

- **You are in your first three months and still learning kana or basic grammar, with no relocation plans.** Read [Chapter 1](#chapter-1---history) and [Chapter 2](#chapter-2---geography) once for a map, then close this file and go do [Kana](<../Kana/readme.md>) and [Grammar](<../Grammar/readme.md>). Come back when you can read NHK News Web Easy. Culture study at zero Japanese has nothing to attach to.
- **You already live in Japan.** School 3 has happened to you. Use this guide as a decoder for things you have already experienced and could not name - read [Chapter 4](#chapter-4---social-structure-in-the-language) and [Chapter 6](#chapter-6---work-culture) first, and skip the phase plan entirely.
- **Your goal is strictly reading.** Untranslated novels, technical documents, older texts. Read [Chapter 1](#chapter-1---history) and [Chapter 4](#chapter-4---social-structure-in-the-language) and genuinely skip the rest. School 4 is a defensible choice when the goal is narrow, not a claim that everyone needs all of this.

## Phase-by-phase plan

Seven phases. Phases 3 and 4 run in parallel with everything else. Times are budgets, not targets.

### Phase 0 - Orientation

- **Goal:** a correct coarse map, so that every later piece of information has somewhere to attach. Specifically: no wrong model, rather than a detailed right one.
- **Time:** about two hours, once, in week one.
- **Materials:** [Chapter 1](#chapter-1---history) and [Chapter 2](#chapter-2---geography) of this guide, read straight through without trying to memorise. The [regions and prefectures table](#the-eight-regions-and-the-47-prefectures). Optionally japan-guide.com for pictures.
- **Done when:** you can name the four main islands and say why Okinawa is not one of them; place Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, Nagoya, Fukuoka, Hiroshima and Sapporo roughly correctly; name the eight conventional regions; and recite the period sequence Jomon - Yayoi - Kofun - Asuka/Nara - Heian - Kamakura - Muromachi - Sengoku - Edo - Meiji - Taisho - Showa - Heisei - Reiwa, with one sentence about what changed in each of Heian, Edo, Meiji and 1945.

### Phase 1 - The social grammar

- **Goal:** internalise uchi/soto, vertical relationships and indirectness well enough that they change what you hear. This is the highest-value phase in the plan.
- **Time:** weeks 2 to 6, about twenty minutes a week plus one longer sitting.
- **Materials:** [Chapter 4](#chapter-4---social-structure-in-the-language) read twice, a week apart. The Tofugu mirror articles on [uchi/soto](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japanese/uchi-soto/index.html>) and [keigo](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japanese/keigo/index.html>). Jay Rubin's *Making Sense of Japanese* - the essays on the invisible subject and on giving and receiving directions are exactly this material approached from the grammar side. For the forms themselves, [Grammar](<../Grammar/readme.md>).
- **Done when:** you can explain out loud, without notes, why you say 田中部長はいらっしゃいません inside your company and 田中はおりません to a client about the same person; and you have caught at least one register shift in something you were watching (a character moving from ですます to plain form, or the reverse) and correctly identified what it meant about the relationship.

### Phase 2 - Etiquette as behaviour

- **Goal:** not being rude, in the specific situations you will actually hit. Behaviour, not knowledge.
- **Time:** months 2 to 4, about fifteen minutes a week, plus dry runs.
- **Materials:** [Chapter 5](#chapter-5---etiquette-practically). The Tofugu mirror on [etiquette generally](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japan/japanese-etiquette/index.html>), [bowing](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japan/bowing-in-japan/index.html>), [table manners](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japan/table-manners/index.html>), [chopsticks](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japan/chopsticks-in-japan/index.html>), [trains](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japan/train-etiquette/index.html>) and [public baths](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japan/sento/index.html>). *Nakama 1*'s per-chapter culture notes cover greetings, homes, shopping and restaurants in exactly this register.
- **Done when:** you can talk yourself through four sequences from memory, including the Japanese phrases and the physical actions - a konbini transaction, ordering and paying at a restaurant, a shrine visit, and an onsen visit. If you have to look any of them up, you are not done.

### Phase 3 - Immersion with cultural eyes

- **Goal:** stop reading fiction as reportage. Start treating media as cultural evidence with known distortions.
- **Time:** months 3 to 18 and beyond. **No additional time budget** - this is a change in how you consume what you are already consuming.
- **Materials:** whatever you are watching and reading, plus a deliberate injection of non-fiction Japanese input - NHK news, documentaries, VTuber streams, variety shows, vlogs, reality television, recipe videos, train-window footage. [AJATT](<../AJATT/readme.md>) owns sourcing this in bulk; the saegusa [Japanese Media Recommendations](<../AJATT/saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations>) charts in this repo are a starting list. Read [Chapter 9](#chapter-9---pop-culture) at the start of this phase.
- **Done when:** you can name three specific, concrete ways a show you personally like misrepresents Japan - not "it's exaggerated" but "adults do not address each other by first name like that", "that workplace confrontation would never happen in the open" - and your input is at least a quarter non-fiction or non-anime.

### Phase 4 - Geography and place-name literacy

- **Goal:** place names stop being unparseable noise in news, weather, addresses and dialogue.
- **Time:** months 6 to 12, about five minutes a day, inside your existing SRS session.
- **Materials:** the [Japanese locations deck](<../Vocabulary/Japanese locations/Japanese locations.apkg>) in this repo - 295 cards covering regions, prefectures, cities and the old provinces, with maps and readings. [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) owns Anki setup; [Vocabulary](<../Vocabulary/readme.md>) owns deck strategy.
- **Done when:** you can read all 47 prefecture names, place at least thirty on a blank map, and recognise the irregular readings that turn up constantly - 大阪, 神戸, 日本橋 (Nihonbashi in Tokyo, Nipponbashi in Osaka), 上野, 飛鳥.

### Phase 5 - Deep history and society reading

- **Goal:** a narrative you can reason with, replacing a list of period names. Optional but high-value.
- **Time:** months 9 to 24, one to two hours a week. Skippable entirely.
- **Materials:** a proper single-volume history. Well-regarded English options include Andrew Gordon's *A Modern History of Japan*, Marius Jansen's *The Making of Modern Japan*, Conrad Totman's *A History of Japan* for the full span, Ian Buruma's *Inventing Japan* as a short polemical overview, and John Dower's *Embracing Defeat* specifically on the Occupation. None of these are in this repo. For the language's own history, the repo has [Frellesvig's *A History of the Japanese Language*](<../General content/Books/A history of the Japanese Language/A history of the Japanese Language.pdf>), which is a different and much more technical thing - see the [resource table](#in-this-repo-offline).
- **Done when:** you can explain the Meiji Restoration and the 1945 defeat and Occupation to another person, in your own words, in about five minutes each, including why each is still politically live.

### Phase 6 - The practical relocation layer

- **Goal:** know the actual machinery of moving, working and living there. Replace vibes with a checklist.
- **Time:** starting twelve to eighteen months before a move; several hours up front, then ongoing.
- **Materials:** [Chapter 6](#chapter-6---work-culture) and [Chapter 10](#chapter-10---being-a-foreigner-long-term) here for structure, then **primary sources only** for details - the Immigration Services Agency, the relevant municipal government, your prospective employer's HR. Job-market reality from TokyoDev and Japan Dev; lived reality from r/japanlife. [JLPT](<../JLPT/readme.md>) owns how certification interacts with hiring and visa points.
- **Done when:** you can state which residence status you would apply under and what it requires; you know what your employer must provide; you have a written first-month bureaucratic checklist (residence card, ward office registration, bank, phone, insurance, pension, My Number); and you have spoken to at least three foreign engineers who currently live there. That last item is worth more than all the reading.

### Phase 7 - Living it

- **Goal:** build a Japanese-language social life deliberately, because it will not happen by default.
- **Time:** ongoing after arrival.
- **Materials:** hobby circles, sports clubs, 習い事 (lessons - pottery, martial arts, instruments), the neighbourhood association 町内会, volunteering, local events. Not language exchanges, which are a transitional tool.
- **Done when:** you have at least two recurring social contexts in which the language is Japanese and nobody is being paid to talk to you; and you have had a real disagreement with a Japanese friend and stayed friends. That second one is the actual test of whether you have got past tatemae.

## Chapter 1 - History

A narrative, not a date list. Precise dates are given only where they are firm anchors; elsewhere this uses period names and centuries on purpose. Language-relevant points are marked with **[Language]** callouts, because the history of Japanese and the history of Japan are the same story told twice.

### Jomon and Yayoi - before the state

The Jomon period is named after the cord-marked pottery (縄文 "cord pattern") that characterises it, and it is astonishingly long - many thousands of years of a settled-but-not-agricultural society of hunters, gatherers and fishers, supported by an unusually rich marine and forest environment. Jomon pottery is among the oldest known anywhere, which is itself a useful corrective: this is not a late-developing periphery, it is a place with a very deep continuous occupation.

The transition to the Yayoi period, over roughly the last millennium BCE and into the early centuries CE, is the pivot. Wet-rice agriculture, bronze, and then iron arrive from the Asian continent via the Korean peninsula, along with new pottery styles, weaving, and new burial practices. Populations grow, land becomes worth fighting over, and social stratification appears - moated settlements, weapons, and elite burials.

Two things to take from this:

- **Rice is not merely a food here.** It arrives with the social order, becomes the basis of taxation for most of recorded Japanese history, and remains loaded with cultural meaning. This is why ご飯 means both "cooked rice" and "a meal", and why the vocabulary around rice is so dense ([Chapter 8](#chapter-8---food-culture)).
- **The continental connection is constitutive, not incidental.** The idea of Japan as an isolated island culture is a later ideological construction. Technology, writing, religion, political models and people came across the strait repeatedly, and the periods of relative closure were deliberate policy choices, not geography.

> **[Language]** There is genuine scholarly debate about the relationship between the Yayoi migrations and the origins of the Japonic language family, and about what was spoken in the archipelago before. It is contested and unsettled. What is not contested is that Japanese is not related to Chinese - the enormous Chinese-derived vocabulary is borrowed, the way English borrowed from Latin and French. Japanese is generally treated as the main branch of a small Japonic family whose other branch is Ryukyuan; Ainu is unrelated to both.

### Kofun and Yamato - the first state

From roughly the third to the sixth century, enormous keyhole-shaped burial mounds (古墳 kofun) appear, concentrated in the Kinai region around modern Nara and Osaka. They are a political statement: someone could command that much labour. Out of this emerges the Yamato polity, the ancestor of the imperial institution, consolidating control over other regional chieftaincies through a mixture of conquest, marriage and ritual authority.

Two imports define the period's legacy:

**Chinese writing.** Literacy reaches Japan through continental scribes, largely from the Korean kingdoms, and initially means literacy *in Chinese* - reading and writing classical Chinese (漢文 kanbun) as a foreign language of administration and learning, exactly as medieval Europe used Latin.

> **[Language] This is where kanji enter.** Everything in [Kanji](<../Kanji/readme.md>) descends from this moment. And crucially, Japanese had no writing system of its own, so the entire subsequent history of Japanese orthography is a long, messy, ingenious project of forcing a script designed for an unrelated, non-inflecting language to write an inflecting agglutinative one. That mismatch is the direct cause of on-yomi versus kun-yomi, of okurigana, of multiple readings per character, and of most of what makes Japanese writing hard. It is not arbitrary complexity - it is the fossil record of a solved engineering problem.

**Buddhism**, which arrives in the sixth century, traditionally via the Korean kingdom of Baekje. The exact year is disputed in the sources and not worth memorising. What matters is that it arrives as a *package* - along with continental art, architecture, medicine, calendrical science and political theory - and that it immediately becomes a factional issue among the Yamato elite rather than a private spiritual matter. Religion and state power are entangled from the start ([Chapter 7](#chapter-7---religion-and-holidays)).

### Asuka and Nara - building a Chinese-model state

From the late sixth through the eighth century, the Yamato court deliberately rebuilds itself on the Tang Chinese model. The Taika reforms, from the mid-seventh century, begin a long process of asserting central control over land and people. It culminates in the 律令 (ritsuryo) state - a codified legal and administrative system, with law codes compiled in the early eighth century, a ranked bureaucracy, a census, a land-allotment system and a rice tax.

A permanent capital is laid out on a Chinese grid plan at Nara (平城京) in 710, which gives the Nara period its name. Great state-sponsored temples are built, including Todai-ji with its colossal bronze Buddha - a project so expensive it strained the state, which tells you how seriously the court took Buddhism as a legitimising force.

The first surviving Japanese books date from this period:

| Work | Roughly | What it is |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 古事記 Kojiki | early 8th c. | "Record of Ancient Matters" - myth, genealogy and legend, compiled to legitimise the imperial line. The source of the Amaterasu and Izanagi/Izanami myths. |
| 日本書紀 Nihon Shoki | early 8th c. | A more formal court chronicle, written in Chinese, aimed at a continental readership as much as a domestic one. |
| 万葉集 Man'yoshu | late 8th c. | "Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves" - a huge poetry anthology, and the single most important document of Old Japanese. Includes poems attributed to emperors, courtiers, soldiers and commoners. |

> **[Language] Man'yogana and the road to kana.** The Man'yoshu is written in 万葉仮名 (man'yogana): Chinese characters used for their *sound* rather than their meaning, to spell out Japanese phonetically. One character per syllable, chosen fairly freely, so the same sound could be written many ways. This is the ancestor of both kana scripts - hiragana emerges from cursive simplifications of man'yogana, katakana from abbreviated fragments of them, over the following couple of centuries. See [Kana](<../Kana/readme.md>) for the scripts themselves. The other consequence is that the Man'yoshu lets linguists reconstruct Old Japanese phonology with real confidence, including distinctions later lost - which is the central subject matter of [Frellesvig's *A History of the Japanese Language*](<../General content/Books/A history of the Japanese Language/A history of the Japanese Language.pdf>) in this repo.

### Heian - the court, kana, and the birth of Japanese literature

In 794 the capital moves to Heian-kyo, modern Kyoto, where it stays for over a thousand years. The Heian period that follows is the classical age of Japanese court culture, and it is disproportionately important for anyone learning the language.

The political story is a slow hollowing-out. The ritsuryo land system erodes into private estates (荘園 shoen). The Fujiwara family perfects rule-by-marriage, supplying empresses and then governing as regents for child emperors. The emperor becomes a ritual centre rather than an executive. Meanwhile, out in the provinces, the families who actually do the fighting and the tax-collecting - the ancestors of the samurai - accumulate real power that the court does not notice in time.

The cultural story is more interesting. The court develops an intensely aestheticised culture in which sensitivity to seasons, colour, scent and poetry is a form of social competence. Rank is everything, indirection is the norm, and relationships are conducted through exchanged poems with precisely calibrated allusions. 物の哀れ (mono no aware) - a sensitivity to the transience of things - is the term later critics gave to its characteristic mood.

And then the part that actually matters most:

> **[Language] Kana, gender, and the invention of Japanese prose.** In the Heian court, serious writing by men meant writing in Chinese. Kanbun was the language of government, scholarship and Buddhism, and it was the mark of a properly educated man. Kana - specifically the cursive hiragana forms - were associated with women, to the extent that hiragana was called 女手 (onnade, "women's hand"). Women of the court were largely not taught Chinese, so they wrote in kana, in Japanese.
>
> The result is one of the genuine oddities of world literary history: **because the prestige script was closed to them, women writing in the "lesser" script produced the foundational works of Japanese prose.** Murasaki Shikibu's 源氏物語 (*The Tale of Genji*), from the early eleventh century, is a long psychological novel about court life, and is often described as the world's first novel - a claim worth hedging, but the work's stature is not in doubt. Sei Shonagon's 枕草子 (*The Pillow Book*) is a collection of observations, lists and opinions from roughly the same period, and is sharp, funny and startlingly modern in voice. Add the Izumi Shikibu diary, the Sarashina diary, the Kagero Nikki, and you have a body of women's writing with no real parallel elsewhere at that date.
>
> The nuance, because the neat version is too neat: men did write in kana when it suited them. Ki no Tsurayuki's 土佐日記 (*Tosa Nikki*) is written in kana, and he adopts a female narrative persona to do it - which tells you the association was real enough that he felt he needed the cover. The gendered split was a strong social convention, not an absolute rule.

For a learner, the practical consequences are: classical Japanese (古文 kobun) is a separate language you can safely ignore unless you want it; but Heian-period vocabulary and aesthetics are still the reference frame for a huge amount of literary and pop-cultural allusion, and the Hyakunin Isshu poetry anthology is a live cultural object, memorised in schools and played as a competitive card game (karuta).

### Kamakura and Muromachi - warriors, Zen and Noh

Late in the twelfth century the provincial warrior families settle the question of who actually holds power, in the Genpei War between the Taira and Minamoto clans. Minamoto no Yoritomo wins, and rather than taking over the court, he sets up a parallel military government at Kamakura, far from Kyoto, and takes the title 将軍 (shogun). The emperor and court remain, with ritual authority and no army. This arrangement - a legitimating imperial centre plus a military government that actually governs - lasts, in various forms, until 1868.

The Kamakura period (twelfth to fourteenth century) brings:

- **A warrior class with its own culture.** Land tenure in exchange for military service, values of loyalty and shame, and eventually a substantial literature about how a warrior ought to behave. Note that 武士道 (bushido) as a systematised code is largely a later, and substantially Edo-period and Meiji-period, construction - it is not a medieval rulebook that samurai carried around. This is a common and worthwhile correction.
- **Two attempted Mongol invasions** in the late thirteenth century, both broken up partly by storms - the origin of the 神風 (kamikaze, "divine wind") idea, which was recycled with grim consequences in the 1940s.
- **New popular Buddhism.** The Kamakura period produces schools aimed at ordinary people rather than aristocrats: Pure Land (浄土) and True Pure Land (浄土真宗) with their emphasis on the nenbutsu and salvation through faith, Nichiren Buddhism, and Zen (禅) imported from Song China. Zen becomes strongly associated with the warrior class.

The Ashikaga shogunate follows from the fourteenth century, ruling from the Muromachi district of Kyoto - so the warrior government moves back into the capital, and warrior and court culture fuse. This is the period that produces much of what is now presented as traditional Japanese aesthetics:

- **能 Noh** theatre, formalised by Kan'ami and Zeami - extremely slow, masked, poetic, and built on a theory of restraint and suggestion. Its comic counterpart 狂言 (kyogen) is much more accessible.
- **The tea ceremony** (茶の湯 / 茶道) develops toward its later form, along with the aesthetic vocabulary of 侘寂 (wabi-sabi) - beauty in imperfection, austerity and age.
- **Ink painting, rock gardens, flower arrangement** (生け花), and the 書院 / 和室 interior with tatami, tokonoma alcove and sliding screens.
- The Golden and Silver Pavilions in Kyoto, and the Higashiyama cultural moment around the Ginkaku-ji.

Central authority then collapses. The Onin War, in the later fifteenth century, wrecks Kyoto and effectively ends Ashikaga power, opening the Sengoku period.

### Sengoku - warring states and the first Europeans

For roughly a century, Japan has no functioning central government. Regional lords (大名 daimyo) fight continuously; castles get bigger and then get redesigned for artillery; peasant armies of 足軽 (ashigaru) replace mounted elites as the decisive arm; and social mobility briefly becomes real - Toyotomi Hideyoshi rises from peasant origins to rule the country, which would have been impossible before and was made impossible again afterwards.

Into this arrive the Europeans. Portuguese ships reach Japan in the 1540s, bringing matchlock firearms, which are copied and mass-produced with remarkable speed and change warfare within a generation. Jesuit missionaries follow, Francis Xavier among the first, and Christianity spreads significantly in western Japan, including among some daimyo. Nagasaki develops as the Portuguese trading port. Loanwords survive from this contact - パン (bread), タバコ, カステラ, てんぷら, ボタン, カルタ - and they are Portuguese, not English.

> **[Language] The first romanization.** The Jesuit mission produced the first systematic Latin-alphabet writing of Japanese, for the practical reason that missionaries needed to learn the language fast. The mission press printed Japanese texts in romanized form, and produced a Japanese-Portuguese dictionary (the *Vocabulario da Lingoa de Iapam*, usually called the 日葡辞書 Nippo Jisho) and a grammar by Joao Rodrigues, around the turn of the seventeenth century in Nagasaki. These are priceless linguistic evidence: because Portuguese orthography recorded distinctions that Japanese script did not, they document late-sixteenth-century pronunciation directly - including that は was pronounced closer to /fa/ and that now-merged sounds were still distinct. See [Romaji](<../Romaji/readme.md>) for the modern romanization systems and the arguments between them.

Unification comes in three stages: Oda Nobunaga breaks the old order with brutal efficiency and is assassinated; Toyotomi Hideyoshi completes the conquest, freezes the social classes, disarms the peasantry in a nationwide sword hunt, and launches two catastrophic invasions of Korea in the 1590s that end with his death; and Tokugawa Ieyasu wins the decisive battle at Sekigahara in 1600 and is appointed shogun in **1603**.

### Edo / Tokugawa - two and a half centuries of managed peace

The Tokugawa shogunate, ruling from Edo (modern Tokyo) from 1603, produces one of history's more remarkable stretches of internal peace. It does so by an elaborate system of control.

**The political machinery.** Daimyo are ranked by their relationship to the Tokugawa and their domains distributed strategically. The 参勤交代 (sankin kotai) system requires them to spend alternating periods in Edo and to leave their families there as effective hostages, which is enormously expensive and keeps them too poor to rebel. Domains are surveilled. Castle-building is restricted.

**The closed country, and its limits.** Christianity is banned and violently suppressed - the Shimabara rebellion in the 1630s is the breaking point - and a series of edicts in the 1630s restricts foreign contact and forbids Japanese subjects from leaving. This is conventionally called 鎖国 (sakoku, "closed country"), but the term is a later coinage and the picture it suggests is misleading. Japan was not sealed; it had **four managed gateways**, and the shogunate used them deliberately:

| Channel | Through | What flowed |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Dutch | Dejima, an artificial island in Nagasaki harbour | Trade, and a controlled trickle of European books and science - the basis of 蘭学 (rangaku, "Dutch learning"), which produced serious Japanese work in medicine, astronomy and botany |
| Chinese | Nagasaki | Trade, books, and continuing scholarly contact |
| Korean | Tsushima and the So clan | Trade and periodic diplomatic embassies to Edo |
| Ryukyu | The Satsuma domain | Trade with a nominally independent kingdom that was itself also tributary to China |
| (plus) | The Matsumae domain in southern Ezo | Trade with, and steadily worsening exploitation of, the Ainu |

So the correct mental model is not isolation but **monopolised, state-controlled foreign contact.** That is a different thing, and it is why the country could modernise so fast later: the elite knew roughly what was out there.

**The social order.** Ideologically, society was arranged in four ranked estates, 士農工商 (shi-no-ko-sho): warriors, farmers, artisans, merchants, with the emperor and court and clergy outside the scheme, and outcast groups (えた and 非人, whose descendants became the 部落民 burakumin question discussed in [Chapter 10](#chapter-10---being-a-foreigner-long-term)) placed below it. Modern historians treat this as an official Confucian-derived ideal rather than a description of how society actually worked - it obscures enormous variation, and it inverted the actual economics, since merchants got rich while many samurai fell into genteel poverty on fixed rice stipends.

**Urbanisation and popular culture.** This is the part that matters most culturally. Peace plus the sankin kotai system plus commercialised agriculture produced huge cities. By the eighteenth century Edo was one of the largest cities in the world, with roughly a million residents, and Osaka and Kyoto were major centres too. Temple schools (寺子屋 terakoya) and commercial publishing pushed literacy high by pre-industrial standards. The result was a genuinely commercial popular culture, produced for and consumed by townspeople (町人 chonin):

- **浮世絵 ukiyo-e** - woodblock prints, mass-produced and cheap: actors, courtesans, landscapes (Hokusai's waves, Hiroshige's road stations), and a great deal of erotica (春画 shunga). This was disposable commercial art, not high art, which is worth remembering given its later prestige.
- **歌舞伎 kabuki** - loud, spectacular, star-driven popular theatre, and **文楽 bunraku** puppet theatre, often sharing plays. Chikamatsu Monzaemon's domestic tragedies turn on the 義理 / 人情 conflict discussed in [Chapter 4](#chapter-4---social-structure-in-the-language).
- **Popular fiction and poetry** - Ihara Saikaku's stories of merchants and pleasure districts; Matsuo Basho's haiku and travel writing, which fixes 俳句 as a form built around seasonal words (季語 kigo).
- **Sumo, licensed pleasure quarters, guidebooks, fashion, food stalls, the first restaurant culture, hot-spring tourism.** Recognisably modern urban consumer life.

> **[Language]** The Edo period is where the ancestor of modern standard Japanese takes shape, as the speech of the Edo townspeople and samurai - which is why the modern standard is Tokyo-based. Kansai speech had been the prestige variety when Kyoto was the capital; the shift is political ([Chapter 3](#chapter-3---dialects-and-regional-identity)).

### Meiji - the fastest state rebuild on record

American warships under Commodore Perry arrive in **1853** and again in **1854**, and force the opening of ports. A series of unequal treaties follows, granting extraterritoriality and removing tariff control, which becomes the central national humiliation and the organising political grievance for decades. The shogunate, unable either to expel the foreigners or to satisfy them, loses legitimacy. After a short civil war, the Tokugawa are overthrown and the emperor is formally restored to power in the **Meiji Restoration of 1868** - though real power sits with a small group of young reforming samurai from the domains that won.

What follows in about thirty years is genuinely without close parallel:

- **The feudal structure is dismantled.** Domains are abolished and replaced with prefectures in the early 1870s. Samurai stipends are converted to bonds and then effectively wiped out; the class is legally abolished and its members told to find jobs. Some rebel; the rebellions are crushed by the new conscript army.
- **Universal conscription** is introduced in the 1870s, which along with universal schooling becomes one of the two great engines of national standardisation.
- **Compulsory elementary education** is built out nationwide, with a centrally controlled curriculum.
- **Industry is built from the top down** - state model factories, railways, telegraph, shipyards, then sold to private conglomerates (財閥 zaibatsu).
- **Institutions are shopped for deliberately.** The Iwakura Mission tours the United States and Europe in the early 1870s explicitly to choose which models to copy. The legal system comes largely from France and Germany, the navy from Britain, the army eventually from Prussia, the education system partly from the US. A constitution and an elected lower house arrive around 1889 to 1890 - a constitutional monarchy with the emperor as sovereign and limited suffrage.
- **Empire.** Japan wins a war against Qing China in the 1890s and takes Taiwan; defeats Russia in 1904 to 1905, the first modern defeat of a European power by a non-European one, which reverberates globally; and annexes Korea in **1910**.

Culturally, Meiji is a period of fierce argument about how much to Westernise. 文明開化 ("civilisation and enlightenment") produced brick buildings, ballroom dancing, beef eating, Western clothes for officials, and the slogan 和魂洋才 ("Japanese spirit, Western learning") as a compromise formula. There was a serious backlash, and the "traditional Japan" that gets presented today is substantially a Meiji-era curation - including the reorganisation of Shinto into a state institution ([Chapter 7](#chapter-7---religion-and-holidays)).

> **[Language] 標準語 as a political project.** A nation-state with mass schooling, a conscript army and newspapers needs everyone to speak the same language. They did not. Japanese dialects differed enough that speakers from distant regions could struggle badly. So the Meiji state *created* a standard - 標準語 (hyojungo), based on educated Tokyo speech - and propagated it through schools, the military, print, and later radio. The genbun itchi movement worked to bring written Japanese closer to speech, replacing classical written forms with a modern written standard.
>
> This was nation-building, and like most nation-building it had victims. Dialect use in schools was actively suppressed, in some places by public humiliation - the 方言札 (hogen fuda), a placard hung around the neck of a child caught speaking dialect, is the best-known instrument, documented most extensively in Okinawa and also in Tohoku. Okinawan and the other Ryukyuan languages, and Ainu, were treated not as languages but as obstacles. The stigma attached to Tohoku and Kyushu accents today is downstream of this policy. See [Chapter 3](#chapter-3---dialects-and-regional-identity).

### Taisho and early Showa - liberalisation, then militarisation

The Taisho period (1912 to 1926) is short and has a distinctive flavour: urban, consumerist, and politically loosening. Party cabinets gain real power, mass media and mass culture take off, women enter offices and department stores, cafes and jazz and Western fashion mark the modern (モダンガール、モダンボーイ). The mid-1920s bring universal *male* suffrage - and, passed in the same period, the Peace Preservation Law, which gave the state sweeping powers against political dissent. Both at once. "Taisho democracy" is a real phenomenon and a fragile one.

The **1923 Great Kanto earthquake** devastates Tokyo and Yokohama, with an enormous death toll driven mostly by fire. In the chaos afterwards, rumours circulated that Koreans were poisoning wells and committing arson, and mobs - in some cases with the involvement of police and militia - killed a large number of ethnic Koreans, along with Chinese residents and Japanese socialists. The death toll and the extent of official responsibility are disputed, and public commemoration remains politically contested in Japan today, including disputes over whether government officials should send messages to memorial ceremonies. Flagging this plainly: it is a documented atrocity and it is also an active political argument.

The Showa period begins in 1926, and the 1930s go badly. The Depression hits rural Japan hard and discredits party politics. The army increasingly acts on its own initiative, and assassination becomes a tool of domestic politics. The Manchurian Incident of 1931 - a staged sabotage used as a pretext - leads to the occupation of Manchuria and the puppet state of Manchukuo. Japan leaves the League of Nations. Full-scale war with China begins in 1937 and does not end until 1945. Colonial rule in Korea and Taiwan intensifies, including assimilation policies requiring Japanese language and Japanese names. At home, the state moves to full mobilisation, censorship and thought policing.

### The Pacific War and 1945

Japan attacks Pearl Harbor and the British and Dutch colonies in Southeast Asia in December 1941, initially with sweeping success, and then loses a war of attrition it had no industrial capacity to win. From 1944 the home islands come within bomber range. The firebombing campaign destroys most major Japanese cities; the Tokyo raid of March 1945 killed roughly a hundred thousand people in a single night, which is a scale worth registering because it is often overshadowed. **Hiroshima and Nagasaki are destroyed by atomic bombs in August 1945**, the Soviet Union enters the war, and Japan surrenders. Total Japanese deaths run into the millions, civilian and military; deaths across Asia caused by Japanese military action are far higher.

The Allied Occupation, effectively American and run under MacArthur, lasts from 1945 until 1952. It was unusually ambitious and unusually consequential:

- **A new constitution**, drafted under Occupation direction, promulgated in 1946 and in force from 1947. It makes the emperor "the symbol of the State" with no political power, establishes popular sovereignty, guarantees a long list of rights, and gives women the vote.
- **Article 9**, in which Japan renounces war and the maintenance of war potential. The Self-Defense Forces exist under an interpretation that they are not "war potential"; whether and how to revise Article 9 has been a central, unresolved political argument for seventy years, and a 2010s reinterpretation permitting collective self-defence was itself deeply contested.
- **Land reform**, which broke up tenancy and created a conservative owner-farmer class - one of the most consequential and least discussed reforms.
- **Education reform**, breaking up the centralised prewar system, dissolving the old ethics curriculum, and rewriting textbooks.
- **Zaibatsu dissolution**, attempted and then substantially abandoned as the Cold War changed American priorities - the "reverse course". The companies reassembled as looser 系列 (keiretsu) groupings.
- **The emperor was not tried.** Hirohito remained on the throne, a decision made for occupation-management reasons that shaped how war responsibility was subsequently discussed in Japan.

> **[Language] The postwar script reforms.** Occupation-era reformers considered abolishing kanji entirely, and some Japanese intellectuals agreed. What actually happened was a substantial simplification. In **1946** the government issued the 当用漢字 (toyo kanji) list of about 1,850 characters for official and general use, along with 現代仮名遣い - modern kana orthography, which brought kana spelling into line with pronunciation and retired the historical spellings (so けふ became きょう). Character forms were simplified. The toyo list was replaced by the 常用漢字 (joyo kanji) list in 1981 with about 1,945 characters, revised in 2010 to the current 2,136. This is why pre-1946 text looks noticeably different, and it is the reason "how many kanji do I need" has an official-ish answer at all. See [Kanji](<../Kanji/readme.md>) and [Writing](<../Writing/readme.md>).

### Postwar - miracle, bubble, and the long aftermath

**The economic miracle.** From the mid-1950s into the early 1970s Japan grows at extraordinary rates, moving from textiles to steel and shipbuilding to cars and consumer electronics. Government-industry coordination, high savings, a cheap fixed exchange rate, an educated workforce and the American security umbrella all contribute; economists still argue about the weighting. The **1964 Tokyo Olympics** are the coming-out event, and the Tokaido Shinkansen opens the same year - both deliberately timed as a statement that Japan was back. The 1970 Osaka Expo continues it.

Costs came with it: severe industrial pollution, including the mercury poisoning at Minamata, which produced a major environmental movement and eventually strong regulation. Also a housing and commuting model - long commutes, small apartments, the salaryman family with an absent father - that the culture is still processing.

**The bubble.** After the oil shocks of the 1970s and the Plaza Accord of 1985, an enormous asset bubble inflates in the late 1980s. Tokyo land values reach genuinely absurd levels, Japanese firms buy landmark foreign assets, and Western commentary shifts to predicting Japanese economic domination - which is the context in which the "Japanese uniqueness" publishing boom occurred ([Chapter 4](#the-honest-critique---nihonjinron-and-over-generalisation)).

**The collapse and the lost decades.** The bubble bursts around 1990 to 1991. What follows is not a crash so much as a very long stagnation: bad loans left unresolved, deflation, low growth, and a generation of graduates who entered a frozen job market and never recovered the career trajectory their predecessors had - the 就職氷河期 ("employment ice age"). 1995 brought two shocks in the same year: the Great Hanshin-Awaji earthquake destroyed much of Kobe, and the Aum Shinrikyo cult released sarin on the Tokyo subway. Together they did serious damage to public confidence in both state competence and social cohesion, and they are part of the background of a lot of 1990s culture, Evangelion very much included.

**2011.** The **Tohoku earthquake and tsunami** in March 2011 killed roughly twenty thousand people, destroyed coastal towns, and caused the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear accident. The subsequent handling of the accident, the evacuation zones, the reactor restart question and the treated-water discharge are all continuing political disputes. 東日本大震災 is a reference point in contemporary Japanese the way 9/11 is in American English.

**Heisei to Reiwa.** The Heisei era (1989 to 2019) maps almost exactly onto the stagnation, which is why it carries a melancholy connotation. Akihito abdicated - the first Japanese abdication in about two centuries - and the Reiwa era began in 2019. The dominant current pressure is demographic: a low birth rate, a long-lived population, a shrinking workforce, rural depopulation, and rising social-insurance costs. This is the force quietly changing labour law, immigration policy, and the entire employment model described in [Chapter 6](#chapter-6---work-culture). Avoid quoting population figures - they change, and they are usually quoted wrong.

### Contested memory - what a learner should know exists

You do not need a position on any of this. You do need to know these arguments exist, because they appear in the news, in conversation, and in the subtext of a lot of media, and being oblivious to them is how a foreigner says something harmful by accident.

| Issue | The dispute, stated neutrally |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Yasukuni Shrine** | A Shinto shrine that enshrines Japan's war dead, including individuals convicted as war criminals by the postwar tribunal. Visits by prime ministers and cabinet members draw formal protests from China and South Korea and domestic constitutional objections about state-religion separation. Defenders describe the visits as honouring the dead and a domestic matter; critics describe them as endorsing a militarist reading of the war. |
| **Textbooks** | Japanese school textbooks are screened by the education ministry. Critics, domestic and foreign, argue that the process has produced descriptions of wartime conduct that are too brief or too euphemistic; defenders argue the criticism is politicised and that Japanese textbooks do cover the war. Specific approvals become diplomatic incidents. |
| **"Comfort women" (慰安婦)** | Women, many Korean, who were made to provide sex to the Japanese military. Disputes concern the degree of direct official coercion, numbers, the adequacy and finality of Japanese apologies and compensation - including the 1965 normalisation treaty, the 1990s Asian Women's Fund, and a 2015 bilateral agreement - and whether the matter is settled. It is unresolved politically and emotionally in both countries. |
| **Nanjing (1937)** | That a large-scale massacre of Chinese civilians and prisoners occurred is accepted by mainstream historians in Japan and elsewhere. The death toll is disputed, with Chinese official figures far above most Japanese scholarly estimates, and a small but vocal Japanese revisionist current disputes the scale or the event's characterisation. |
| **Forced labour** | Wartime conscription of Korean and Chinese labourers, and litigation in South Korean courts against Japanese companies. Japan's position is that claims were settled by the 1965 treaty; South Korean court rulings have held otherwise. This has caused serious diplomatic and trade friction. |
| **Article 9** | Whether to revise the constitution to explicitly recognise the Self-Defense Forces or permit a broader military role. Supporters cite the regional security environment; opponents cite the postwar pacifist settlement and regional distrust. Public opinion is divided and has shifted. |
| **Hiroshima and Nagasaki** | Within Japan, largely framed through the hibakusha experience and nuclear abolition. Elsewhere, often framed as a decision to end the war. The two framings talk past each other, and a visitor to the Hiroshima Peace Memorial Museum should know both exist. |
| **The Ainu and Ryukyuans** | Whether the historical treatment amounted to colonisation, and what recognition, land, language and self-determination claims follow. A 2019 law recognised the Ainu as an indigenous people; activists consider it insufficient. Okinawa's US base concentration is a live and bitter dispute. See [Chapter 10](#internal-difference-and-how-japan-handles-it). |

The honest summary: **there is no single Japanese position on any of these.** Japanese public opinion spans the full range, historians of the period in Japan are mostly mainstream in their findings, the political right and left disagree sharply, and older and younger generations differ. Treating "Japan" as having a unified view on wartime memory is itself an error.

## Chapter 2 - Geography

Geography is not decoration for a language learner. It determines what the news is about, what the weather report means, where the dialects are, why cities look the way they do, and it is one of the densest sources of irregular kanji readings you will encounter.

### The islands

Japan is an archipelago of thousands of islands. **Four are conventionally called the main islands:**

| Island | Kanji | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Honshu** | 本州 | By far the largest, and where almost everything you have heard of is - Tokyo, Osaka, Kyoto, Nagoya, Hiroshima, Sendai, Mt Fuji. Contains five of the eight regions. |
| **Hokkaido** | 北海道 | The northern island. Cold, snowy, sparsely populated, large-scale agriculture and dairy. The Ainu homeland; settled intensively by the Japanese state only from the Meiji period. Also a prefecture in its own right. |
| **Kyushu** | 九州 | The southwestern island. Historically the point of contact with the continent and with Europe. Fukuoka, Nagasaki, Kumamoto, Kagoshima; active volcanoes; strong regional identity and distinctive dialects. |
| **Shikoku** | 四国 | The smallest and least populous of the four. Separated from Honshu by the Inland Sea. Known for the 88-temple pilgrimage, udon, and relative quiet. |

**Okinawa is not one of the four main islands.** This matters and gets stated wrongly constantly. Okinawa (沖縄本島) is the largest island of the **Ryukyu Islands** (琉球諸島), a long chain stretching southwest from Kyushu toward Taiwan. The Ryukyus were an independent kingdom trading with China, Japan and Southeast Asia; they came under the control of the Satsuma domain in the early seventeenth century while remaining nominally independent and tributary to China; Japan asserted full control in the 1870s and formally created Okinawa Prefecture in 1879. Okinawa was the site of the only large ground battle on Japanese soil in 1945, with catastrophic civilian casualties; it remained under US administration until reversion in **1972**; and it still hosts a disproportionate share of US military bases, which is a continuing political dispute. Ryukyuan culture, cuisine, music and languages are distinct - see [Chapter 3](#chapter-3---dialects-and-regional-identity).

Also worth knowing: the Izu and Ogasawara (Bonin) islands, administratively part of Tokyo, stretching far into the Pacific; Sado, Awaji and Tsushima; and the disputed territories - the Northern Territories / Southern Kurils (administered by Russia, claimed by Japan), Takeshima / Dokdo (administered by South Korea, claimed by Japan), and the Senkaku / Diaoyu islands (administered by Japan, claimed by China and Taiwan). All three are live nationalist issues and appear in news constantly.

### The eight regions and the 47 prefectures

Japan has **47 prefectures** (都道府県 to-do-fu-ken). The four words are not interchangeable: 43 are 県 (ken), Osaka and Kyoto are 府 (fu), Hokkaido is 道 (do), and Tokyo is 都 (to) - a metropolis, which is administratively unusual in that its central area consists of 23 special wards (区) rather than cities.

The **eight regions** (地方 chiho) are a conventional grouping used in weather forecasts, school textbooks, sports leagues and everyday speech. They have **no administrative status**, and the boundaries vary by source - Mie is often grouped with Kansai but sometimes with Chubu or Tokai, and Niigata floats between Chubu, Tohoku and its own Hokuriku or Koshinetsu grouping. Do not treat the table as canonical.

| Region | Prefectures | Known for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| **Tohoku** 東北 | Aomori, Iwate, Miyagi, Akita, Yamagata, Fukushima | The northern Honshu six. Rice, sake, apples (Aomori), heavy snow on the Sea of Japan side, onsen, and major summer festivals (Nebuta in Aomori, Kanto in Akita, Tanabata in Sendai). The 2011 tsunami hit this coast. | **Kanto** 関東 | Ibaraki, Tochigi, Gunma, Saitama, Chiba, Tokyo, Kanagawa | The political and economic core. Greater Tokyo is the largest metropolitan area in the world by most definitions. Yokohama, Kawasaki, Chiba's industry and Narita airport, Nikko's shrines (Tochigi), Kamakura (Kanagawa). The home of standard Japanese. |

Prefecture and region names are easy to treat as the most skippable material in this guide, until watching Japanese news and weather with the sound on: a large share of the proper nouns in any five-minute segment are place names, they are frequently read irregularly, and not knowing them makes an ordinary segment feel harder than it is. The [Japanese locations deck](<../Vocabulary/Japanese locations/Japanese locations.apkg>) in this repo - 295 cards with maps and readings, roughly three weeks of small daily additions - is a high-return way to close that gap, and it is filed under [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) rather than under geography.

### Mountains, and why the cities are so dense

Roughly three-quarters of Japan's land area is mountainous, and much of the rest is steep. The habitable, buildable, farmable fraction is small, and it is mostly coastal plain. Nearly everything follows from this:

- **Density.** Population concentrates on a few plains - the Kanto plain around Tokyo is the largest - producing some of the highest urban densities in the developed world. This is a land constraint, not a cultural preference for crowding.
- **Small homes and long commutes.** Tokyo apartment sizes and one-hour-plus commutes are consequences of the same constraint, mediated through the world's best commuter rail network.
- **Rail rather than roads.** Dense linear corridors between mountain ranges are ideal for rail and bad for sprawl. Hence the Shinkansen, and hence train stations as the organising centre of towns - the 駅前 (ekimae, "in front of the station") district is the commercial core almost everywhere.
- **Terraced and intensive agriculture**, small plots, and a rice-centred food system suited to wet valley floors.
- **Tunnels and bridges everywhere.** Japanese civil engineering is world-class because it had to be.

Mt Fuji (富士山), at about 3,776 metres, is the highest peak, a near-perfect stratovolcano, and the single most reproduced image in Japanese visual culture. It is also an active volcano that has not erupted since the early eighteenth century, which is a fact people in Tokyo prefer not to dwell on.

### Climate

Japan stretches a long way north to south, so the climate range is genuinely large. The usual summary - "four distinct seasons" - is presented in Japan as a national characteristic, which is worth noting as a cultural fact even though it is not geographically distinctive.

| Zone | Character |
| :--- | :--- |
| Hokkaido | Cold winters with heavy snow, cool pleasant summers, no distinct rainy season by convention |
| Sea of Japan side (Hokuriku, western Tohoku) | Extraordinary winter snowfall - winter winds pick up moisture crossing the Sea of Japan and dump it on the mountains. Some of the snowiest inhabited places on earth. Grey winters, hot humid summers |
| Pacific side (Kanto, Tokai, Setouchi) | Dry sunny winters, hot and very humid summers |
| Kyushu and Shikoku | Milder winters, hotter and wetter summers, first in line for typhoons |
| Okinawa and the Ryukyus | Subtropical. No real winter, very humid, typhoon-prone |

Two seasonal events structure the year:

- **梅雨 tsuyu, the rainy season.** Roughly June into mid-July across most of the country, moving north as the season progresses. Weeks of grey, humid, drizzly weather. Hokkaido conventionally does not have one. Culturally it is a whole mood - hydrangeas, umbrellas, mould, and a great deal of vocabulary.
- **台風 taifu, typhoon season.** Roughly summer into autumn, typically peaking around August and September. Storms can shut down rail and air travel nationwide, and 計画運休 (planned suspension of service) announcements are now standard practice.

Add 花粉症 (hay fever, mostly cedar pollen, in spring) as a mass seasonal affliction that explains a lot of the masks, and the summer 熱中症 (heatstroke) warnings, which have become a serious public-health issue as summers have got hotter.

Seasonality is also a linguistic and aesthetic system, not just weather. 季語 (kigo, seasonal words) are structurally required in haiku; letters traditionally open with a seasonal greeting; foods, sweets and even convenience-store products rotate seasonally; and the seasonal marker is a standard device in fiction and anime.

### Earthquakes, volcanoes and tsunami - a permanent condition

Japan sits in the convergence zone of several major tectonic plates. The consequences are not an occasional disaster; they are a constant background condition that shapes engineering, institutions and psychology.

- **Earthquakes** are routine. A resident of Tokyo will feel several a year. The Japanese intensity scale 震度 (shindo, 0 to 7) measures shaking at a location and is what news reports lead with, rather than magnitude.
- **Early warning.** The 緊急地震速報 system detects the fast P-wave and broadcasts alerts to phones, TV, radio and public address seconds before the shaking arrives - enough to stop trains, halt surgery and get under a desk. Every phone in the country makes the same alarming noise. J-Alert covers missiles and other emergencies.
- **Building codes** are extremely strict and were revised significantly after major quakes, notably in the early 1980s and after Kobe in 1995. Rental listings advertise 新耐震基準 (post-1981 earthquake standard) as a selling point, which tells you how normal this is. Base isolation and damping systems are common in tall buildings.
- **Drills.** School and workplace 避難訓練 are routine. 防災の日 (Disaster Prevention Day) is September 1, the anniversary of the 1923 Great Kanto earthquake. Households are expected to keep an emergency bag; municipalities publish hazard maps and designated evacuation sites (避難所), and knowing yours is a normal part of moving into an apartment.
- **Volcanoes.** More than a hundred active volcanoes, monitored continuously and rated on an alert scale. Sakurajima erupts routinely next to Kagoshima city; Aso, Asama, Ontake and Shinmoedake are all active. The upside is the onsen.
- **Tsunami.** The 2011 Tohoku tsunami overwhelmed seawalls in places and killed roughly twenty thousand people. Coastal areas have evacuation route signage, elevation markers, sirens and high-ground designations. The lesson drilled since is simple: shaking near the coast means move uphill immediately, do not wait for confirmation.
- **The next one.** The government treats a major Nankai Trough earthquake off the Pacific coast, and a large earthquake directly under the Tokyo metropolitan area, as matters of when rather than if, and publishes long-term probability estimates and damage projections. This is stated openly in public planning documents.

The psychological effect is worth naming because it is easy to misread. It is not anxiety. It is a broadly shared, matter-of-fact competence - you know what to do, the systems work, and you get on with it. It also plausibly feeds into some cultural tendencies often explained in more mystical terms: the aesthetic of impermanence, the emphasis on collective preparedness and orderly behaviour under stress, and a certain fatalism captured in 仕方がない (shikata ga nai, "it cannot be helped"). Plausibly - not provably. Be careful with this kind of explanation.

### The megalopolis, and the emptying countryside

The **太平洋ベルト (Taiheiyo Belt)** is the industrial and urban corridor running along the Pacific coast from the Kanto plain through Tokai to Kansai and on toward northern Kyushu. Most of the population, industry and infrastructure is in it. Its core is the **Tokaido corridor**, the old Edo-period highway route, now the Tokaido Shinkansen and the country's economic spine, linking three metropolitan clusters:

| Cluster | Core cities |
| :--- | :--- |
| Greater Tokyo | Tokyo, Yokohama, Kawasaki, Saitama, Chiba |
| Chukyo | Nagoya and the surrounding Aichi industrial belt |
| Keihanshin | Osaka, Kyoto, Kobe |

The other side of that concentration is **rural depopulation**, which is one of the defining domestic issues of contemporary Japan:

- **過疎化 kaso-ka** - depopulation. Young people leave for the cities, birth rates fall, and the remaining population ages.
- **限界集落 genkai shuraku** - "marginal villages", where such a large share of residents are elderly that the community can no longer sustain basic functions. Some are formally abandoned.
- **空き家 akiya** - vacant houses, now numbering in the millions nationwide and rising. Some are essentially free; there are municipal 空き家バンク (vacant house banks) trying to give them away. This gets breathless foreign coverage that omits the renovation costs, the inheritance and title complications, the property taxes, and the absence of jobs and healthcare nearby.
- **Municipal mergers** have consolidated thousands of towns and villages into fewer, larger units, partly to keep local government viable.
- **Schools closing**, bus routes ending, local rail lines being abandoned as unprofitable, and clinics disappearing.
- Counter-currents exist: 地方創生 (regional revitalisation) policy, remote work shifting some people out of Tokyo, 移住 (domestic relocation) support schemes, and pockets of genuine rural renewal. They are not currently winning.

This matters for a prospective resident in a concrete way. Tokyo, Osaka and Fukuoka are where the engineering jobs, the international community, the English-capable services and the specialist healthcare are. The countryside is beautiful, cheap and hard, and it is a genuinely different proposition.

### Place names as kanji practice

Japanese place names are a large, high-frequency vocabulary domain with unusually irregular readings, which makes them both a real obstacle and a good training ground.

- **Regular-looking but arbitrary readings.** 大阪 Osaka, 神戸 Kobe, 札幌 Sapporo (originally an Ainu-derived name written with phonetic kanji), 京都 Kyoto, 東京 Tokyo.
- **Same characters, different readings by region.** 日本橋 is Nihonbashi in Tokyo and Nipponbashi in Osaka. Both are real, both are correct, in their own places.
- **Jukujikun and gikun** - readings assigned to a whole compound rather than derived from the characters: 飛鳥 Asuka, 日下 Kusaka.
- **Ainu-derived Hokkaido names** written with phonetically chosen kanji, which is why Hokkaido place names look strange even to Japanese readers: 稚内 Wakkanai, 小樽 Otaru, 登別 Noboribetsu.
- **Ryukyuan names** with their own patterns: 那覇 Naha, 石垣 Ishigaki, 今帰仁 Nakijin.
- **The address system** is itself a geography lesson - prefecture, city, ward, district (町), then block-and-building numbers (丁目・番・号) rather than street names. Streets mostly do not have names. This is the reason everyone navigates by landmark and by station.

A practical consequence: when you hit an unfamiliar kanji compound in a news article and the readings make no sense, check whether it is a place name or a surname before assuming you have found a vocabulary gap. Japanese surnames are just as bad, and both are outside the joyo reading conventions.

## Chapter 3 - Dialects and regional identity

This chapter owns dialects **as culture** - regional identity, prestige, stigma, and standardisation as a political project. [Listening](<../Listening/readme.md>) owns dialect as a comprehension problem: recognising Kansai negative forms in the wild, dealing with unfamiliar accents, subtitling. [Speaking](<../Speaking/readme.md>) owns whether you should ever produce one. Read this for the why; read those for the how.

### Standard language is a recent invention

The single most important thing to understand is that **標準語 (hyojungo) is about 150 years old and was made on purpose.** Before the Meiji period there was no national spoken standard. There was a written classical language, there were regional prestige varieties, and there were local dialects differing enough that a conversation between a Kagoshima speaker and an Aomori speaker could fail entirely. The Meiji state needed a common language for schools, conscription, courts and newspapers, and built one from educated Tokyo speech.

Two terms, worth distinguishing:

- **標準語 hyojungo** - "standard language". Carries a prescriptive edge: this is the correct form, others are deviations. Now somewhat out of favour precisely because of that implication.
- **共通語 kyotsugo** - "common language". The neutral modern term, meaning the shared variety everyone can use, without implying that regional speech is wrong. Linguists prefer it.

The word for dialect is **方言 (hogen)**, and the informal suffix for a regional variety is **〜弁 (-ben)**: 関西弁, 大阪弁, 博多弁, 東北弁. There is also **なまり (namari)** for "accent" in the sense of a regional inflection in otherwise standard speech.

The standardisation project had teeth. Dialect was suppressed in schools, and the **方言札 (hogen fuda)** - a placard hung around the neck of a pupil caught speaking dialect, who had to wear it until they caught someone else - is the notorious instrument. It is documented most extensively in Okinawa, where the linguistic distance was greatest and the assimilation pressure most explicit, and also in Tohoku. Broadcast media then did the rest: NHK's standardised announcer speech became the model for what educated Japanese sounded like, and by the postwar period every household had it.

The result today is **diglossia in practice**. Most people below a certain age control something close to the standard for formal and public use and use their regional variety at home and with friends, sliding between them. Heavy dialect is increasingly restricted to older speakers and rural areas, and younger regional speech is often a levelled hybrid - sometimes called neo-dialect - retaining accent and a handful of local forms over a basically standard grammar.

### Kansai-ben - the exception that proves the rule

**関西弁** (loosely; more precisely 大阪弁, 京都弁, 神戸弁 and others) is the only major regional variety with confident national prestige, and it is a genuine sociolinguistic oddity.

What makes it distinctive, in rough order of noticeability:

| Feature | Standard | Kansai |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Copula | だ | や |
| Negative | 〜ない | 〜へん / 〜ん |
| "Really" | 本当 | ほんま |
| "No good / stop it" | だめ | あかん |
| "Why?" | なんで / どうして | なんで (with rising force), なんでやねん as a set retort |
| Emphatic | 〜じゃないか | 〜やんか / 〜やん |
| "Thanks" | ありがとう | ありがとう / おおきに (older) |
| Progressive | 〜ている | 〜てる / 〜とる |
| Accent | Tokyo pattern | A different pitch-accent system, not simply the inverse of Tokyo, though many individual words do differ in exactly that way |

Why it has prestige when Tohoku-ben does not:

1. **History.** Kyoto was the capital for over a thousand years, and Kansai speech was the prestige variety for most of that. The shift to Tokyo is recent and political, and Kansai has never fully conceded it.
2. **Osaka's economic weight** and its self-image as the merchant city, commercially confident and not deferential to Tokyo.
3. **Comedy.** Japanese stand-up and double-act comedy (漫才 manzai) is overwhelmingly Kansai in origin, and the Osaka-based Yoshimoto Kogyo agency dominates the industry. Kansai-ben is consequently the sound of being funny, and funny is a defensible thing to sound like on national television.
4. **Speaker confidence.** Kansai speakers who move to Tokyo frequently keep their accent, which is much rarer for Tohoku or Kyushu speakers. That refusal to converge sustains the variety's status.

In media, Kansai-ben is used as a character shorthand - loud, direct, funny, mercantile, warm, sometimes thuggish. That is a stereotype, and like most stereotypes it is deployed lazily. But it does mean that recognising Kansai forms unlocks a large amount of comedy, variety television and manga dialogue.

Kyoto speech deserves a separate note: it is perceived as softer and more indirect than Osaka speech, and it has its own honorific system - the **〜はる** form (行かはる for 行かれる / いらっしゃる), a light honorific used far more casually and broadly than standard keigo. It is also the source of the stereotype about Kyoto indirectness, where an apparently gracious remark carries a sharp edge.

### Stigma - Tohoku, Kyushu and the others

**Tohoku** dialects carry the heaviest stigma. The label ずーずー弁 refers to characteristic consonant and vowel realisations that standard-speaker ears hear as slurred or indistinct, and the associated stereotype is rural, slow and unsophisticated. In media, a Tohoku accent marks a character as a country person - the equivalent of a broad rural accent in English-language film. This is a real and well-documented prejudice, and older Tohoku speakers frequently report having been made ashamed of their speech at school.

**Kyushu** dialects vary a great deal across the island. Hakata-ben (Fukuoka) is relatively well-liked and has a mild positive stereotype. Kagoshima-ben is famously difficult - other Japanese speakers genuinely struggle with it, and it is sometimes subtitled on national television, which tells you something.

Other notable varieties: Hiroshima-ben (marked in media as tough, partly from yakuza films), Nagoya-ben (mocked more than stigmatised), Hokkaido-ben (comparatively close to standard, because Hokkaido was settled from many regions and the speech levelled), Tsugaru-ben in Aomori (among the hardest on Honshu), and Hachijo, spoken on Hachijojima, which is divergent enough that some linguists treat it as a separate language.

**The recent shift is toward dialect as an asset.** Regional varieties are now marketed - dialect goods, local mascot characters speaking in dialect, tourism campaigns, regional-pride television. ご当地 (local speciality) culture is commercially significant. There is a genuine revaluation going on. It coexists with the older stigma rather than replacing it, and it coexists with continuing levelling among young speakers.

### Not dialects - Ryukyuan and Ainu

This is where accuracy matters most, because the conventional Japanese framing and the linguistic framing differ.

**The Ryukyuan languages.** Okinawan (沖縄口 Uchinaaguchi) and its relatives are **not dialects of Japanese.** They are a separate branch of the Japonic family, having split from mainland Japanese far enough back that they are not mutually intelligible with it - the relationship is roughly comparable in depth to that between distinct Romance languages. Linguists generally identify several distinct Ryukyuan languages, including Amami, Kunigami, Okinawan, Miyako, Yaeyama and Yonaguni. Calling them dialects is an artefact of the Meiji-era assimilation framing, and it is still the common usage in Japan.

They are all endangered. UNESCO has classified the Ryukyuan languages, along with Ainu and Hachijo, as endangered. A century of suppression followed by the postwar generation's understandable choice to raise children in Japanese has left first-language competence concentrated among the elderly. What survives robustly is **ウチナーヤマトグチ** - Okinawan-influenced Japanese, with distinctive vocabulary, intonation and sentence-final particles (だからね, 〜さー, 〜よー, めんそーれ for welcome, にふぇーでーびる for thanks) - which functions as a strong marker of Okinawan identity even among speakers who do not know the older language. Okinawan music, dance, the sanshin, and a cuisine visibly distinct from mainland Japanese food are all part of the same identity, and it is a live one.

**Ainu (アイヌ語).** The Ainu language is **unrelated to Japanese** - a language isolate with no demonstrated relatives. The Ainu are the indigenous people of Hokkaido, and historically also of northern Honshu, Sakhalin and the Kurils. The history is a colonial one: trade monopolised and then exploitative under the Matsumae domain in the Edo period; then, from around 1869 when Ezo was renamed Hokkaido and opened to state-sponsored settlement, systematic dispossession. An assimilationist "protection" law at the end of the nineteenth century restricted traditional livelihoods such as salmon fishing and deer hunting, imposed Japanese-language schooling and Japanese names, and moved people onto allotted land.

The recognition timeline is recent: a cultural promotion law in the 1990s replaced the old assimilation law; in **2019** a law recognised the Ainu as an indigenous people of Japan for the first time in statute; the Upopoy National Ainu Museum and Park opened in Shiraoi, Hokkaido, in **2020**. Activists consider the recognition insufficient - it addresses culture more than land, resource rights or self-determination. The language has very few fluent first-language speakers and revitalisation efforts are ongoing. Ainu words survive in Hokkaido place names and in a handful of Japanese borrowings (ラッコ sea otter, トナカイ reindeer, シシャモ). Whether the historical treatment should be called colonisation is politically contested in Japan, which is itself a fact worth knowing.

### Where dialect meets keigo

A practical interaction that catches learners out: **the honorific system and regional varieties are partly independent axes.** People do not simply switch from dialect to standard when being polite; they often use regional honorific forms, or dialect with polite endings, or standard keigo with regional accent. Kansai's 〜はる is the clearest case - a genuine honorific that is not standard Japanese. Regional politeness formulas (おこしやす in Kyoto, だんだん in parts of the San'in region) exist too.

For a learner the guidance is simple and not very satisfying: **learn standard Japanese, learn to understand the major dialects, and be extremely cautious about producing one.** A foreigner speaking fluent Kansai-ben is charming if it was acquired by living in Osaka and faintly ridiculous if it was acquired from anime. Dialect signals belonging, and claiming belonging you have not earned reads badly. Standard Japanese is never wrong anywhere; dialect is right only where you actually are.

See [Listening](<../Listening/readme.md>) for the comprehension side and [Speaking](<../Speaking/readme.md>) for the production question and for pitch accent, which is a genuinely regional feature.

## Chapter 4 - Social structure in the language

**This is the most valuable chapter in this guide.** Everything else here is context. This is the part that changes what you can parse.

The boundary, stated once: [Grammar](<../Grammar/readme.md>) owns the **machinery** - the actual forms of 尊敬語 (sonkeigo, honorific), 謙譲語 (kenjougo, humble) and 丁寧語 (teineigo, polite), the お〜になる and お〜する patterns, the special verbs (いらっしゃる、おっしゃる、召し上がる、伺う、申す、おる). [Speaking](<../Speaking/readme.md>) owns **using register live** under time pressure. This chapter owns the **sociology**: why a language would encode hierarchy and group membership at all, what the categories are, and how to work out which form a situation calls for.

### Uchi and soto - the single most useful insight

If you take one thing from this guide, take this.

Japanese social space is organised around **内 (uchi, inside) and 外 (soto, outside)**. Uchi is your group - your family, your company, your department, your club, your team. Soto is everyone else. The boundary is **not fixed**; it shifts with context, and that shifting is the whole point.

The grammatical consequence is a rule with almost no exceptions:

> **You humble your own side and elevate the other side.**

Which produces the thing that confuses every learner who meets it without the framework:

**Inside your company**, talking to a colleague about your department head Tanaka:
```
田中部長はいらっしゃいません。
Tanaka-buchou is not here.  [honorific いらっしゃる, plus his title]
```
Tanaka is senior to you, so he gets honorific treatment.

**To a client**, about the same man:
```
田中はおりません。
Tanaka is not here.  [humble おる, no title, no さん]
```
The client is soto. Tanaka is now uchi - on *your* side of the line. So you strip his title, drop the honorific suffix, and use a humble verb for him, exactly as you would for yourself. To an outsider, **your boss is part of your group, and your whole group is humbled.**

Learners find this bizarre because it looks like disrespecting your boss. It is the opposite. **Relative position is always computed against the person you are speaking to**, not in absolute terms. Absolute rank does not exist in the honorific system; only relations do.

Once you have this, an enormous amount falls into place:

**Family terms come in pairs** - one humble set for your own family, one honorific set for other people's:

| | Your own (to outsiders) | Someone else's |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Mother | 母 haha | お母さん okaasan |
| Father | 父 chichi | お父さん otousan |
| Older sister | 姉 ane | お姉さん oneesan |
| Wife | 妻 tsuma | 奥さん okusan |
| Husband | 夫 otto | ご主人 goshujin / 旦那さん |
| Son | 息子 musuko | 息子さん / お坊ちゃん |
| Company | 弊社 heisha / 当社 | 御社 onsha / 貴社 |
| Opinion | 私見 | ご意見 |

Note that you call your own mother お母さん *to her face* - that is a within-group address form. The 母 form is for when you are describing her to an outsider. Two different jobs.

**Giving and receiving verbs encode direction relative to the group.** あげる moves things away from your side, くれる moves things toward it, もらう is receiving. Which is why 教えてくれた and 教えてあげた are not interchangeable and why the choice tells you where the speaker's loyalties sit. Jay Rubin's *Making Sense of Japanese* - [in this repo under Grammar](<../Grammar/Books/Making Sense of Japanese What the Textbooks Don't Tell You/Making Sense of Japanese What the Textbooks Don't Tell You.pdf>) - has an excellent chapter on exactly this, framed as "the invisible man's family reunion", and it is an excellent treatment of why the subject can be omitted: the directional verbs already tell you who did what to whom.

**うち as a word** means house, home, household, and by extension "my company" or "us" - and in Kansai it does duty as a first-person pronoun, mostly for women. Same concept, surfacing as vocabulary.

**The boundary moves.** Your department is uchi against another department, and both are uchi against another company. Your company is uchi against a competitor, and both are uchi against a foreign firm. Japan is uchi against abroad - which is where 外国人 and the whole [foreigner question](#chapter-10---being-a-foreigner-long-term) come from. It is the same machinery at every scale.

Keigo often gets studied as a memorisation problem - tables, irregular verbs, exceptions - and stays miserable and brittle that way. The uchi/soto framework collapses it into one rule plus a lookup table instead, which is a different and far more tractable kind of problem. If keigo study feels like rote memorisation of unrelated facts, that is a sign of missing the model, not the vocabulary.

### Tatemae and honne - public position and true feeling

**建前 (tatemae)** is the position you present publicly. **本音 (honne)** is what you actually think. The existence of a named, socially accepted gap between them is one of the most commented-on features of Japanese social life, and one of the most badly explained.

The bad explanation is "the Japanese are indirect and do not say what they mean", which shades quickly into an accusation of dishonesty. That reading is wrong, and it is worth being precise about why.

Every culture has this distinction. English speakers say "we should do this again sometime" without meaning it, answer "how are you" with "fine" while miserable, and tell colleagues their bad idea is "interesting". Professional courtesy, small talk, diplomacy and tact are all tatemae. What differs is not the existence of the gap but **its size, and where the honne is permitted to come out.**

In Japanese social practice:

- The gap is **wider** in formal, hierarchical and public contexts - workplaces, first meetings, dealings with customers.
- It is **narrower** than English-speaking norms in certain private contexts. Close friendships, family, and drinking sessions can be very direct.
- The **designated venues** for honne are real institutions. The 飲み会 (nomikai) functions partly as a licensed space where the hierarchy relaxes and people say things they could not say in the meeting, with a tacit agreement that it does not count in the morning. 本音を言うと ("to tell the truth") and 正直に言うと are explicit markers that the speaker is crossing over.
- The maintenance of tatemae is understood as **consideration, not deception.** Publicly contradicting a superior, or telling someone their work is bad in front of others, imposes a cost on them that the norm exists to prevent. The person doing it is not being fake; they are being careful.

The practical upshot for a learner: **do not assume the first answer is the real answer, and do not assume that means people are lying to you.** Learn to look for the honne through the standard indicators - hesitation, hedging, the topic being changed, an answer that does not address the question, a colleague telling you privately afterwards. And accept that as a foreigner you will be shown tatemae for longer than a Japanese person would be, and that getting past it is what the [Phase 7](#phase-7---living-it) social goal is actually about.

Related: **裏 and 表** (ura/omote, back and front) as a broader metaphor for the same split, and 猫を被る ("wearing a cat") for putting on a false demure front.

### Senpai and kouhai - vertical relationships

**先輩 (senpai)** is someone who entered the institution before you; **後輩 (kouhai)** someone who entered after. The institution can be a school, a university club, a company, a martial arts dojo, a hobby circle. The relationship is not about age or ability. It is about entry order, and it is remarkably durable - people maintain senpai/kouhai relations decades after leaving the school where they were formed.

What it governs, concretely:

- **Speech level.** Kouhai use polite forms to senpai; senpai may use plain forms to kouhai. This asymmetry is normal and not rude.
- **Address.** 先輩 can be used as a term of address on its own, or attached to a surname (田中先輩). 後輩 is not used as address.
- **Obligations in both directions.** The senpai is expected to teach, look after, cover for and speak up for the kouhai. The kouhai is expected to defer, do the unglamorous work, pour the drinks and not embarrass anyone. It is a mutual-obligation relationship, not simple subordination - and the obligations on the senpai are real.
- **Practical rituals.** In a traditional company setting the junior pours, arranges the room, arrives first, sits furthest from the door, and reserves the 花見 spot hours early.

Related terms: **同期 (douki)**, people who entered in the same year - a genuinely strong and lasting bond, particularly in companies that hire in annual cohorts, and often the only horizontal relationship in the structure. **上司 / 部下** for workplace superior and subordinate. **師匠 / 弟子** for master and apprentice in traditional arts. **目上 / 目下** for those above and below you generally.

The honest caveats: this is much weaker in startups, foreign-owned firms and modern tech companies, much weaker among younger people, and considerably weaker than it was thirty years ago. In some environments it is barely operative. In others - traditional companies, university sports clubs, the trades - it is as strong as ever. Do not assume either way; observe.

### Wa - group harmony and how decisions get made

**和 (wa)** is the term for group harmony, and it is old - it appears as a governing principle in the seventh-century Seventeen Article Constitution attributed to Prince Shotoku, and 和 is also an old name for Japan itself (hence 和食 Japanese food, 和室 Japanese room, 和服 Japanese clothing).

As an operative social norm, wa means that **maintaining the functioning of the group takes priority over individual self-expression within it.** The consequences are mostly procedural.

**根回し (nemawashi)** - literally "going around the roots", from transplanting a tree. Before a proposal is raised formally, you circulate it informally: talk to each stakeholder individually, hear objections privately, adjust, secure buy-in. By the time it reaches a meeting, everyone has already agreed.

**稟議 (ringi)** - the formal counterpart. A proposal document (稟議書) circulates through the relevant people, each of whom stamps it with their 判子 (hanko) seal. Approval accumulates.

The combined effect is a decision process with a very specific shape:

| | Behaviour |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Slow to decide** | Consensus-building takes far longer than a decision meeting would |
| **Fast to execute** | Once decided, everyone is already committed - no residual resistance, no re-litigation |
| **Meetings ratify, not debate** | If a real argument happens in a meeting, the nemawashi failed. Surprising people in a meeting is a serious misstep |
| **Diffuse accountability** | No single person owns the decision, which makes reversing it hard and assigning blame harder |
| **Objections go private** | The place to disagree is beforehand, one-on-one, not in the room |

This is genuinely hard for engineers from direct-feedback cultures, and it is the single most common source of foreign frustration in Japanese offices. The instinct to say "actually I think that is wrong" in the meeting is not brave there; it is a process failure that costs the proposer face and marks you as someone who cannot be briefed. The functional equivalent is to go and see people beforehand. Once you understand nemawashi as the debugging channel rather than an obstacle, the system is workable - it is just front-loaded.

Related concepts worth knowing:
- **出る杭は打たれる** - "the stake that sticks out gets hammered down". Cited constantly, including by Japanese people criticising their own institutions. Take it as a real pressure, not a law of nature.
- **報連相 (ho-ren-so)** - 報告・連絡・相談, report/inform/consult. The expected communication discipline in a Japanese workplace: keep your superior informed continuously rather than presenting finished work. Under-communicating is a much bigger sin than over-communicating.
- **迷惑をかける** - "to cause trouble/inconvenience to others". Avoiding this is one of the strongest everyday social motivations, and it explains a great deal of public behaviour - the quiet trains, the queueing, taking your rubbish home, going to work while ill ([Chapter 5](#chapter-5---etiquette-practically)).
- **世間 (seken)** - "society", in the sense of the watching public whose judgement matters. 世間体 is appearances before that public.

### Amae, giri and ninjou - and a warning

These three get invoked constantly in writing about Japan. They are real words describing real things. They are also the exact terrain where over-generalisation lives, so handle with care.

**甘え (amae)** is usually glossed as indulged dependence - presuming on another person's goodwill, behaving in a way that invites indulgence, the security of being able to be a bit unreasonable with someone who will accept it. The verb 甘える is ordinary Japanese and completely unremarkable in use: a child 甘える to a parent, and adults do it to close friends or partners. 甘えないで means roughly "don't be so needy".

Its fame in English comes from the psychoanalyst Doi Takeo's 『甘えの構造』 (*The Anatomy of Dependence*), which argued that amae is a structuring principle of Japanese psychology and social relations. **That book is itself a founding text of the nihonjinron genre**, and its claim - that a national psychology can be derived from one indigenous concept - is not something modern scholarship accepts as stated. So: learn amae as a useful and genuinely untranslatable word. Do not learn it as an explanation of Japanese people.

**義理 (giri)** is obligation - specifically social obligation, owed because of your position or a past kindness, and owed whether or not you feel like honouring it. Gift-giving cycles, attending a colleague's wedding, 義理チョコ (obligation chocolate given at Valentine's to male colleagues you have no interest in).

**人情 (ninjou)** is human feeling - natural compassion, affection, what you actually want to do for someone.

The pair matters because **義理と人情の板挟み** - being caught between duty and feeling - is one of the great structural themes of Japanese narrative. It drives Chikamatsu's Edo-period domestic tragedies, it is the engine of the entire yakuza film genre, and it recurs constantly in manga and anime. If you have watched a story where a character is destroyed by an obligation they did not choose and cannot honourably refuse, you have watched giri/ninjou. Recognising the pattern makes a whole shelf of fiction legible.

Also in this cluster: **恩 (on)** - a debt incurred by receiving a favour, and 恩返し, repaying it; **遠慮 (enryo)** - self-restraint, holding back from imposing or from accepting readily; **気を使う** - expending attention on others' comfort; **お陰で** - "thanks to you", an everyday grammaticalisation of received benefit.

### Indirectness, implicature, and reading the air

Japanese is a **high-context** language in the sense that a large amount of the message is carried by shared situation rather than by words. Structurally:

- **Arguments are freely omitted.** Subject, object and topic are dropped whenever recoverable. Roughly, the more explicit the pronouns, the less natural the sentence. あなた is not the normal way to address someone - use their name and title, or nothing.
- **Sentences are left unfinished** on purpose. 〜んですけど…, 〜ので…, 〜ちょっと…, trailing off with が. The incompleteness *is* the message, usually a refusal or a request the speaker will not state.
- **The listener does active work.** Aizuchi keeps the channel open and signals uptake; silence signals a problem.
- **空気を読む (kuuki wo yomu)** - "read the air", perceive the unstated state of the room and act accordingly. Failing at it makes you **KY (空気読めない)**.

The most practically important application is **refusal**. Explicit no (いいえ, だめです, 嫌です) is strong and used sparingly with people you are not close to. The standard inventory:

| Phrase | Literally | Means |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ちょっと難しいですね | It's a little difficult | No |
| ちょっと… (trailing, with a wince) | A little... | No |
| 考えておきます | I'll think about it | No |
| 検討させていただきます | Allow me to consider it | No, in business register |
| 前向きに検討します | I'll consider it positively | Probably still no |
| また今度 | Another time | Never |
| 微妙 | Subtle / delicate | It's bad, or it's not happening |
| 難しいかもしれません | It might be difficult | No |
| 善処します | I'll do what I can | Famously, no - it has a political history as a non-commitment |
| 大丈夫です | It's fine / I'm fine | Frequently "no thanks", when declining an offer |
| 結構です | Fine / sufficient | Can be "yes please" or "no thank you" - context and tone decide |

Correspondingly, requests are softened into near-invisibility: 〜ていただけませんか, 〜てもらえたら助かります, もしよろしければ, お手数ですが, 恐れ入りますが. And criticism arrives pre-cushioned: 〜のほうがいいかもしれません ("it might be better to..."), which in a Japanese workplace can mean "do it this way".

**How to actually learn this:** you cannot memorise a list into competence, but the list gets you started. The real method is to watch for the mismatch between literal content and outcome. When you notice that a conversation ended in nothing happening despite nobody saying no, go back and find where the no was. Do that thirty times and the pattern becomes audible. This is exactly the [Phase 3](#phase-3---immersion-with-cultural-eyes) skill.

### Other pieces of the system

**Honorific suffixes** are a compressed social map. さん is the default and safe for almost everyone. 様 (sama) is formal and written, used for customers and on envelopes. 君 (kun) is used downward or among familiars, often to boys and junior male colleagues. ちゃん is affectionate and diminutive. 先生 for teachers, doctors, lawyers and published authors. 氏 in writing about third parties. Job titles substitute for suffixes: 部長, 課長, 社長 - and you address a person as 部長 alone, or 田中部長, but **never** 田中部長さん.

**呼び捨て (yobisute)** - using someone's name with no suffix at all. Between adults who are not intimate, this is a significant act: either great closeness or deliberate disrespect. In fiction, the moment a character drops the honorific is a plot point. Real adults default to **surname + さん** and frequently stay there for years, including with people they consider friends. Anime's constant first-name and nickname use is one of its most misleading habits.

**First-person pronouns are a register choice**, not a neutral word. 私 (watashi) is standard and polite for both genders; わたくし is more formal; 僕 (boku) is softer and mostly male; 俺 (ore) is casual and assertively masculine; あたし is casual and feminine; うち in Kansai; plus the fictional-only set (わし, 拙者, 我輩, 余). Choosing one of these tells people who you are trying to be. [Speaking](<../Speaking/readme.md>) owns the production decision.

**Gendered speech** is real but much softer in practice than in fiction. The sentence-final particles (わ, の, かしら as feminine; ぞ, ぜ as masculine), the pronoun sets, and differences in politeness baseline all exist - and are strongly exaggerated in media, shifting generationally, and increasingly treated as a style rather than a requirement. Tofugu has an article on [gendered language](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japanese/japanese-gendered-language/index.html>) and another on [queer Japanese](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japanese/queer-japanese/index.html>).

**自己紹介 (jikoshoukai)** - the self-introduction is a formalised genre with an expected shape (name, affiliation, a brief personal detail, よろしくお願いします) and it is the first thing you will actually have to perform. [Tofugu's article](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japanese/jikoshoukai/index.html>) is in the repo.

### The honest critique - nihonjinron and over-generalisation

Everything above is real, and everything above is routinely abused. You need both halves of this.

**日本人論 (nihonjinron)** - literally "theories about the Japanese" - is a genre of writing, in Japanese and in English, that explains Japan by reference to a unique national character. Its landmarks include Ruth Benedict's *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword*, written for the US government during the Second World War **without the author ever having visited Japan**; Nakane Chie's *Japanese Society*, which framed Japan as a 縦社会 (vertical society); and Doi Takeo's amae book. The genre boomed in Japan and abroad in the 1970s and 1980s - which, note, is exactly when Japanese economic success made "what makes Japan special" a commercially attractive question. It has been extensively criticised, notably in Peter Dale's *The Myth of Japanese Uniqueness* and in work by Harumi Befu and others, on grounds that are worth stating precisely:

1. **It reasons from national character rather than from institutions and incentives.** Long working hours are better explained by employment law, promotion structures and the cost of dismissal than by a spiritual disposition toward work.
2. **It treats a slice as the whole.** Most of the classic descriptions generalise from male white-collar regular employees at large firms in a specific postwar period - a minority of the population even then.
3. **It is unfalsifiable and self-sealing.** Any counterexample becomes an exception, or evidence of Westernisation, or a sign of decline.
4. **It flatters.** Uniqueness narratives are pleasant to their subjects and exotic to foreigners, which is a bad sign for a descriptive claim.
5. **It is ahistorical.** It presents as ancient essence things that are often demonstrably recent: lifetime employment is a postwar institution, the modern honorific standard is a Meiji project, and much of "traditional Japan" was curated in the nineteenth century.

**So how should you hold these concepts?** As **vocabulary for noticing**, not as a predictive model. Uchi/soto genuinely is the grammar of the honorific system - that one is a linguistic fact, not a psychological claim. Nemawashi genuinely is how many Japanese organisations make decisions - that is an observable institutional practice. Tatemae/honne genuinely names something. But:

- **Variation is enormous.** By person, generation, region, industry, company, family, and by whether someone has lived abroad. The spread within Japan dwarfs the average difference between Japan and anywhere else.
- **When someone behaves "un-Japanesely", your model is wrong, not the person.** This is the actual test. If you find yourself thinking a Japanese person is doing Japan incorrectly, stop.
- **Change is fast.** Descriptions written in 1990 are describing a country that has since had thirty years of stagnation, demographic transformation, the internet, and a substantial generational shift in attitudes to work and hierarchy.
- **Be especially sceptical of anything that explains a practical problem by reference to national character.** It is almost always a rule, an incentive or a law instead.

## Chapter 5 - Etiquette, practically

Behaviour, not theory. The framing principle before the details: **a foreigner is not expected to be perfect, and trying too hard is its own error.** Nobody expects you to bow correctly. What people notice is whether you are paying attention and making an effort - and a competent, relaxed attempt at the ordinary version of a thing lands far better than an over-formal performance. The standard you are aiming for is "clearly making an effort and not causing anyone trouble", not "indistinguishable from a native".

Where norms have shifted recently, this says so.

### Greetings and bowing

Bowing (お辞儀 ojigi) is graded. The angles below are the conventional teaching figures, not measured requirements.

| Bow | Angle | Used for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 会釈 eshaku | roughly 15 degrees | Passing a colleague, casual acknowledgement, thanks to a shop assistant. The everyday one |
| 敬礼 keirei | roughly 30 degrees | Business greetings, meeting a client, formal thanks |
| 最敬礼 saikeirei | roughly 45 degrees | Serious apology, deep gratitude, formal ceremony |

Practical points:

- **Bend from the waist, back straight, eyes down.** Hands at your sides for men, folded in front for women, conventionally.
- **The junior bows lower and holds longer**, and rises after the senior. If in doubt, follow the other person and go slightly deeper.
- **Do not bow and shake hands simultaneously** in a way that produces a collision. Many Japanese people will offer a handshake to a foreigner, in which case take it and add a small nod. A handshake with a slight bow is completely normal and safe.
- **Repeated small bows** are normal when thanking or apologising - it is not one formal bow but a series of decreasing nods as you back away.
- People bow on the phone, bow to lifts, and bow while cycling past. It is a reflex, not a ceremony.
- **Do not bow while walking down stairs or through a doorway.** Stop, bow, continue.

Verbal greetings by time and register: おはようございます, こんにちは, こんばんは; お疲れ様です as the all-purpose workplace greeting and farewell; 失礼します on entering or leaving a room; いってきます / いってらっしゃい and ただいま / おかえりなさい at home; お世話になっております as the standard business-call and email opener.

**The お疲れ様です / ご苦労様です trap.** Both mean roughly "thank you for your hard work". **お疲れ様です is the neutral and upward-safe one** - use it with everyone. **ご苦労様です is downward-directed**, from a superior to a subordinate. Saying it to your boss is a real error, and a memorable one. If you learn one piece of workplace etiquette from this chapter, learn this one.

### Business cards

名刺 (meishi) exchange is a genuine small ceremony in a Japanese business setting, and it happens in the first thirty seconds of a first meeting.

- Present with **both hands**, text facing the recipient, slightly lower than theirs if they are senior, with a small bow and your name and company.
- **Receive with both hands.** Read it. Actually read it - name, company, title. This is the point of the exercise.
- **Place it on the table** in front of you for the duration of the meeting, or in a card case. Do not stuff it into a pocket, and absolutely do not put it in a back pocket.
- **Do not write on it** in front of the giver.
- If several people are present, lay the cards out on the table in the seating order. This is both respectful and practically useful for remembering who is who.
- Carry a card case. Carry more cards than you think you need.

Modern caveat: in startups, foreign-owned firms and casual tech environments this is much lighter, and some companies have moved to digital cards. In a traditional firm or any first client meeting, do it properly.

### Shoes, the genkan, and homes

The **玄関 (genkan)** is the recessed entry area of any Japanese home, and many traditional restaurants, clinics, temples, schools and some offices.

- **Shoes come off in the genkan.** Step out of them onto the raised floor - do not step on the raised floor with shoes on, and do not step down into the genkan in socks.
- **Turn your shoes to face the door** after removing them. Neat, and it means you can step straight into them leaving.
- **Wear the provided slippers** if there are any.
- **Toilet slippers are a separate pair**, kept in the toilet. Change into them, and - the classic foreigner mistake - **change back out of them.** Walking into the living room in toilet slippers is a reliable source of gentle horror.
- **Never wear slippers on tatami.** Socks or bare feet only.
- **Socks matter.** No holes. Consider that anywhere you go might involve taking your shoes off.
- Visiting a home: bring a small gift (手土産 temiyage), say お邪魔します on entering and お邪魔しました leaving, do not wander, and accept what you are offered. The guest is seated in the position furthest from the door (上座 kamiza), which is the honoured seat - the same logic operates in restaurants, meeting rooms and taxis.

### Bathing, onsen and sento

This is the etiquette area where foreigners most often go badly wrong, and the rules are simple and non-negotiable.

- **Wash before you get in.** Fully. There are seated shower stations with stools, bowls, soap and shampoo. Sit, wash, rinse **all** the soap off, then enter the bath. The bath is for soaking in clean water, not for cleaning yourself.
- **No swimwear.** You are naked. Baths are sex-segregated (blue/男 and red/女 curtains - check, they sometimes swap by day).
- **The small towel does not go in the water.** Use it for modesty walking around, then fold it on your head or leave it at the edge.
- **Tie long hair up.** No hair in the water.
- **No swimming, splashing, diving or loud noise.** It is a quiet space.
- **Do not drain or add to the bath.** Do not put your towel in, and do not rinse in it.
- Rinse off (or do not - some prefer to leave the minerals on), dry with the small towel *before* stepping into the changing room, so you do not soak the floor.
- In a home bath, the same principle: you wash outside the tub, and the whole family uses the same clean bathwater in sequence. Do not drain it.

**The tattoo question.** Tattoos have a historical association with organised crime in Japan, and many onsen, sento, public pools and gyms still refuse entry to people with visible tattoos. The situation is changing but unevenly. Your options, roughly in order of reliability:

1. **Book a private bath** - 貸切風呂 (kashikiri) at an onsen, or a ryokan room with its own bath. Reliable, costs more.
2. **Check ahead.** Many facilities state their policy online; the phrase to look for is タトゥーOK or 入れ墨可 / お断り. Tattoo-friendly listings exist.
3. **Cover small tattoos** with a waterproof cover sticker. Widely accepted for small ones, useless for large work.
4. **Ask.** Policies at smaller local sento are often applied at the owner's discretion, and politely asking sometimes works.

Do not simply ignore the rule and hope. Being asked to leave is worse than having planned.

### Table manners

Say **いただきます** before eating - hands together, a small bow - and **ごちそうさまでした** after. These are not optional politenesses; they are close to automatic, and skipping them is noticeable. いただきます is the humble form of "receive", so it is thanking everyone in the chain from the food to the cook.

**Chopstick taboos**, most of which derive from funeral practice, which is why they land harder than they look:

| Don't | Name | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Stick chopsticks upright in rice | 立て箸 tatebashi | That is how rice is offered to the dead |
| Pass food chopstick-to-chopstick | 箸渡し hashiwatashi | Bones are passed this way at a cremation |
| Spear food | 刺し箸 sashibashi | Just rude |
| Hover and hesitate over dishes | 迷い箸 mayoibashi | Indecisive, and mildly greedy |
| Rummage through a communal dish | 探り箸 saguribashi | Obvious |
| Point or gesture with them | 指し箸 | Rude |
| Rest them across a bowl | 渡し箸 | Use the rest, or the paper sleeve folded into one |
| Lick them | ねぶり箸 | Rude |
| Pull a dish toward you with them | 寄せ箸 | Rude |

Other points:

- **Slurping noodles is fine** and arguably correct - it cools them and aerates the broth. Ramen, soba and udon, yes. Pasta, no.
- **Lift the rice bowl and the soup bowl** to your mouth. Drink miso soup directly from the bowl; there is no spoon.
- **Do not pour soy sauce on rice.** Pour a small amount into the dish provided and dip.
- **Finish your rice.** Leaving rice is poor form, connected to 勿体ない (mottainai - wastefulness).
- Communal dishes: use the serving chopsticks (取り箸) if provided, or the reverse ends of your own if not, though this last one is itself debated.
- **Eating while walking** is mildly frowned on in most contexts, fine at a festival or a food street.
- **Blowing your nose at the table** is considered worse than sniffing. Go to the bathroom.

### Drinking

- **Do not pour your own drink.** Pour for others, watch their glass, and refill it. Someone will do the same for you. This is the single mechanic that makes Japanese drinking sessions work - attention circulates.
- **Hold your glass with both hands** when someone pours for you, and lift it slightly toward them.
- **Wait for 乾杯 (kanpai)** before drinking. Someone will make a short speech first. When clinking with a senior, hold your glass slightly lower than theirs.
- **お酌 (oshaku)** is the act of pouring for someone. The junior person does more of it.
- **Declining is increasingly acceptable.** 飲めないんです ("I can't drink") or asking for ソフトドリンク / ウーロン茶 is fine and normal now. アルハラ (alcohol harassment - pressuring people to drink) is a recognised term and pushing drinks on someone who has declined is socially disapproved of in a way it was not twenty years ago. If you do not drink, say so once, clearly, and hold.
- The 二次会 (nijikai) is the second venue, and the 三次会 exists too. It is acceptable to leave after the first, especially now.
- The tacit amnesty on things said while drunk is real but weakening, and you should not rely on it.

### Trains and public space

- **Queue** at the marked positions on the platform, and let people off before boarding.
- **Do not take phone calls.** Texting is fine; talking is not. Near the 優先席 (priority seats) you are asked to silence your phone entirely, though enforcement is now relaxed.
- **優先席** are for elderly, disabled, pregnant and injured passengers. The マタニティマーク badge indicates pregnancy. Note that offering your seat is less automatic in Japan than the reputation for politeness might suggest - people frequently do not, partly to avoid embarrassing the other person.
- **Backpacks** go on the rack or held in front of you in a crowded car.
- **Eating and drinking** is normal on the Shinkansen and limited expresses (ekiben are a whole cuisine), and discouraged on commuter trains.
- **Keep quiet.** The dominant sound in a packed Tokyo train is nothing.
- **Escalators:** stand left in Tokyo, stand right in Osaka. Both true. Note that rail operators now campaign against walking on escalators at all, so this norm is actively in flux.
- **Women-only cars** (女性専用車両) operate on some lines at certain hours. Read the signs.
- Do not put your feet on seats, do not block doors, and move to the centre of the car.

Tofugu mirror: [train etiquette](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japan/train-etiquette/index.html>).

### Rubbish

There are almost **no public bins**, a legacy partly of security measures after the 1995 subway attack. You carry your rubbish until you get home or to a convenience store. Konbini bins are for things bought at the konbini.

Household separation is strict and **varies by municipality** - your ward office will give you a chart, often in English. Typically: 燃えるゴミ (burnable), 燃えないゴミ (non-burnable), プラスチック, 缶・瓶 (cans and bottles), ペットボトル, 古紙, and 粗大ゴミ (oversized items, which require a paid sticker and a booked collection). PET bottles: cap off, label off, rinsed, into separate streams. Collection days are fixed per category, and putting the wrong bag out on the wrong day is the classic way to annoy your neighbours - in some buildings someone will go through it and work out it was you.

### Punctuality, money and the rest

- **Punctuality** is real and it is asymmetric: being late is a genuine offence, being early is expected. "Five minutes early is on time." Trains apologise for minute-scale delays and issue 遅延証明書 (delay certificates) you can hand to your employer.
- **Cash versus cashless.** Cash still matters more than in most comparable countries, but this is changing fast. IC transit cards (Suica, Pasmo, ICOCA) work for transit, konbini, vending machines and many shops; QR payment apps (PayPay and others) are widespread; credit cards are accepted in most chains and large stores. Cash-only holdouts: small restaurants, some clinics, older shops, shrine offerings, small local businesses. Carry some. ATMs at convenience stores and post offices accept foreign cards; many bank ATMs do not.
- **Do not tip.** Ever. Not in restaurants, not in taxis, not to hotel staff. It causes confusion, and it may be refused or chased down the street to be returned to you. Some higher-end restaurants and ryokan add a service charge, which is on the bill.
- **Money is handed over on a tray**, not into a hand, in shops. Take the tray.
- **Masks.** Mask-wearing when ill or during hay fever season was completely normalised long before COVID. Official guidance moved to personal choice from 2023, but voluntary use remains common and entirely unremarkable, particularly on crowded transport and when someone has a cold. Wearing one is never odd; not wearing one is now also fine.
- **Smoking.** Indoor smoking in restaurants, bars and workplaces was heavily restricted by legislation that took effect around 2020, with some exemptions for small existing establishments and designated smoking rooms. Simultaneously, many municipalities ban smoking **on the street** outside designated areas - which inverts the expectation of visitors from countries where the reverse is true. Look for 喫煙所 (smoking areas). Heated tobacco products occupy a partly separate regulatory category.
- **Queueing** is orderly and universal, including for lifts, taxis and shop openings.
- **Gestures:** point at your own nose, not your chest, to indicate yourself. Beckon with the palm **down**, fingers waving toward you - palm up is for animals. Crossed arms or crossed fingers in an X means "no" or "not allowed". A hand held vertically in front of the face, waving slightly, means "no thank you" or "excuse me, coming through".
- **Pointing at people** is rude; gesture with an open hand.
- **Noise at home** matters, because walls are thin and 迷惑 is the operative concern. Late-night laundry, loud music and heavy footsteps generate complaints.

### Gift-giving

Gift exchange is a structured system, not a spontaneous gesture.

| Type | What it is |
| :--- | :--- |
| **お土産 omiyage** | Regional food souvenirs brought back from any trip, for colleagues and friends. Individually wrapped, from the place you went, and effectively obligatory if you took time off. Every station and airport has a dedicated omiyage hall for exactly this |
| **手土産 temiyage** | A small gift brought when visiting someone's home or office. Sweets, fruit, alcohol |
| **お中元 ochuugen / お歳暮 oseibo** | Mid-year and year-end gifts to people you are obliged to - clients, bosses, teachers, doctors. Declining as a practice, and some companies now prohibit it |
| **内祝い uchiwaiwai** | Return gifts after receiving congratulations for a wedding or birth |
| **香典 kouden** | Condolence money at a funeral, in a specific black-and-silver envelope, with old (not new) notes |
| **ご祝儀 goshuugi** | Wedding money, in an elaborate red-and-white envelope, with **crisp new** notes, in an odd number of units so it cannot be divided |

Rules that catch people out:

- **Avoid four and nine.** 四 (shi) sounds like death, 九 (ku) like suffering. No sets of four.
- **Presentation matters as much as content.** Department store wrapping signals that you took it seriously. The 熨斗 (noshi) paper and its knot type encode the occasion - red-and-white for happy events, black-and-white for funerals, and a knot that cannot be untied for weddings.
- **The giving formula is self-deprecating**: つまらないものですが ("it's a trifling thing, but...") or 心ばかりですが. Do not praise your own gift.
- Recipients may **demur once** before accepting, and traditionally do not open it in front of you.
- Avoid: clocks and shoes (implying "work harder" or "step on you"), combs (kushi sounds like suffering-and-death), white flowers and chrysanthemums (funerals), potted plants to a sick person (implying rooted illness), and knives.

## Chapter 6 - Work culture

Written for someone who intends to actually work there, not for someone reading a think-piece. The most important thing in this chapter is the [three-employer distinction](#the-three-employers---the-distinction-that-changes-everything), which almost all writing on Japanese work culture omits and which changes nearly every prediction.

### The traditional model, and how much of it is left

The postwar Japanese employment system, as described in every book about it, rested on three pillars:

- **終身雇用 (shuushin koyou)** - lifetime employment. You join a company after graduating and stay until retirement. The company will not fire you; in exchange, you go where you are sent and do what you are given.
- **年功序列 (nenkou joretsu)** - seniority-based pay and promotion. Advancement tracks years of service more than performance.
- **Enterprise unions** - unions organised by company rather than by trade, which negotiate cooperatively.

Three corrections, all important:

1. **It was never universal.** It applied to 正社員 (regular employees) at large firms, and largely to men. Small and medium enterprises, which employ most Japanese workers, never fully operated this way.
2. **It is weakening, unevenly.** Since the 1990s, firms have expanded 非正規雇用 (non-regular employment) - contract, dispatch (派遣), and part-time workers - who have far less security and lower pay for similar work. Non-regular employees now make up a very large share of the workforce, on the order of a third or more. This two-tier structure is the central labour-market issue in Japan and gets much less foreign attention than overtime does.
3. **It is a postwar institution, not an ancient cultural trait.** It emerged in specific conditions - labour shortage, high growth, union bargaining - and it is eroding as those conditions changed. Explaining it by national character gets the causation backwards.

What the traditional model still produces where it survives: generalist career paths with rotation across unrelated functions, internal training rather than specialist hiring, 転勤 (mandatory relocation, sometimes at short notice, sometimes resulting in 単身赴任 where the employee moves alone and the family stays), promotion by cohort, and a reluctance to dismiss that cuts both ways - it is hard to be fired and hard to be promoted fast.

### Hiring - new graduates versus mid-career

This distinction determines how you get in, and for a foreign mid-career engineer it is the good news.

**新卒一括採用 (shinsotsu ikkatsu saiyou)** - simultaneous recruiting of new graduates - is the traditional main channel. Students job-hunt (就活 shuukatsu) during their penultimate and final university years, in a nationally synchronised cycle with recruiting events, standardised aptitude tests, multiple interview rounds, and an identical recruit suit worn by everyone. Successful candidates receive a 内定 (naitei, informal offer) months or a year before graduating, attend a formal entrance ceremony on **April 1**, and go through months of group training before being assigned to a role - often a role they did not choose and that has nothing to do with their degree. The system hires for perceived potential and cultural fit rather than for skills.

If you are not a Japanese university student, this channel is essentially closed to you, and that is fine, because:

**中途採用 (chuuto saiyou)** - mid-career hiring - is the other channel, and it is normal, growing, and the one you will use. It looks much more like hiring anywhere else: you apply for a specific role, you are assessed on your actual skills and experience, you negotiate, you start when you start. Recruiters and job boards are active. For software engineering specifically, this market is comparatively healthy, and the Boston Career Forum (an annual event targeting bilingual candidates) is a known channel for bilingual hiring.

The implication: **do not model your job search on 就活 descriptions.** They describe a process you will not go through.

### Hours, overtime and karoshi

The reputation is deserved historically and the picture now is genuinely mixed.

**過労死 (karoshi)** - death from overwork - is a Japanese word that entered English because Japan recognised the phenomenon first. It covers death from cardiovascular events attributed to excessive work, and **過労自殺** covers overwork-related suicide. This is not folklore: it is a legally recognised category of industrial compensation claim with defined thresholds, and the government publishes an annual white paper on it. A prominent case in the mid-2010s, involving a young employee at the advertising giant Dentsu, generated national attention and contributed materially to the reform push.

**The reforms are real.** Legislation known as 働き方改革 (work style reform) took effect around 2019 and 2020, introducing statutory caps on overtime with penalties for violation - generally on the order of 45 hours a month and 360 a year, with higher exceptional limits under specific agreements. It also required employers to ensure employees actually take a minimum number of paid leave days, and addressed equal pay for equal work between regular and non-regular employees.

**The gap between rule and practice persists.** サービス残業 (service overtime - unpaid, unrecorded work) is the standard workaround: clocking out and continuing. 付き合い残業 is staying late because your colleagues are still there and leaving first looks bad. Whether a given company has genuinely changed or has just moved the overtime off the books varies enormously, and it is one of the main things to investigate about a specific employer.

**ブラック企業 (black company)** is the widely used term for an exploitative employer - illegal overtime, unpaid wages, bullying, high turnover. It has a recognised opposite, ホワイト企業. Screening tools exist and you should use them: employee review sites (OpenWork is the main Japanese one, plus Glassdoor), average overtime hours if the company discloses them, three-year graduate retention rates, whether paid leave usage is published, and turnover. A company that publishes its 平均残業時間 is usually a company that is not ashamed of it.

**Paid leave (有給休暇).** The statutory minimum starts at around ten days after six months of continuous service and rises with tenure to a cap. Since the work-style reforms, employers must ensure employees take at least five days. Two cultural points matter more than the numbers: **usage rates have historically been low**, because taking leave is perceived as imposing on colleagues; and **there is typically no separate sick leave** at many Japanese firms, so illness is taken out of your paid holiday. That second one surprises people badly, and it partly explains the norm of going to work while sick - which is also why the masks.

### The social layer

**飲み会 (nomikai)** - after-work drinking with colleagues. Functionally this is where the hierarchy relaxes, where honne is permitted ([Chapter 4](#tatemae-and-honne---public-position-and-true-feeling)), where information circulates, and where relationships that make work possible get built. The annual calendar includes 歓送迎会 (welcome and farewell parties), 忘年会 (year-end), 新年会 (new year), and 花見 in spring.

**How compulsory is it?** Less than it was. Younger employees decline more readily, the pandemic broke the habit in a lot of organisations, and pressuring people to attend or to drink is now viewed less favourably. But the informal cost of never attending is real, especially in a traditional firm, and the information and relationship access is real too. The pragmatic answer for a foreign employee: **go sometimes, and go to the first one.** You do not have to drink, and you do not have to stay for the 二次会.

Other social machinery: 社員旅行 (company trips, declining), 社内運動会 (company sports days, mostly gone), morning meetings (朝礼), and radio calisthenics (ラジオ体操) at some workplaces and construction sites.

### Bureaucratic survivals

Japan is simultaneously technologically advanced and administratively archaic, and the combination is genuinely disorienting.

- **判子 / 印鑑 (hanko / inkan)** - name seals used in place of signatures. A 実印 (registered seal) is needed for major transactions such as property purchase and is registered with your municipality; a 銀行印 for banking; a cheap 認印 for everyday paperwork. The ringi approval documents from [Chapter 4](#wa---group-harmony-and-how-decisions-get-made) circulate collecting these stamps. Foreign residents can and often must get one made, in katakana or romaji.
- **Fax machines** remain in use, particularly in medical, legal, government and small-business contexts. This is real and not a joke.
- **Paper forms, in triplicate, requiring a physical visit** during business hours. Municipal offices, immigration, banks.
- **Cash and paper receipts** in expense processes.

There has been a real push against this. A **デジタル庁 (Digital Agency)** was established in **2021** with an explicit mandate to modernise government IT, and an official campaign has removed hanko requirements from a large number of administrative procedures. The My Number card system is the identity backbone for this. Progress is genuine and slow. Expect improvement over the timescale of a relocation, not transformation.

### The three employers - the distinction that changes everything

Almost every article about Japanese work culture describes category 1 and presents it as "Japan". For a foreign software engineer this is close to useless, because you will almost certainly work in category 2 or 3.

| | **1. Traditional Japanese firm** | **2. Modern Japanese tech** | **3. Foreign-owned or remote** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Examples | Large manufacturers, banks, trading houses, utilities, telecoms, most SMEs | Mercari, DeNA, CyberAgent, Rakuten, LY/LINE, SmartNews, game studios, funded startups | Google, Amazon, Microsoft, Indeed, Woven, financial-sector engineering, fully remote roles for overseas employers |
| Working language | Japanese only. Business-level Japanese is a hard requirement | Mixed. Some run engineering in English by policy - Rakuten's English-language mandate is the best-known case, and Mercari's engineering organisation is substantially English-operating | English, usually |
| Japanese needed | N2 or better, plus keigo and business email conventions | Often N3-N4 functional, sometimes none for the job itself | Frequently none for the job |
| Hiring | 新卒 cycle, or 中途 with heavy Japanese screening | 中途, skills-based, recognisable process | Global-standard process |
| Pay | Seniority-weighted, lower, with allowances and bonuses | Market-ish, better than category 1 | Substantially the highest |
| Hierarchy | Strong. Titles, seniority, nemawashi, ringi | Flatter, but Japanese social norms persist informally | Mostly the parent company's culture |
| Career path | Generalist rotation, internal promotion, possible 転勤 | Specialist tracks | Specialist tracks |
| Hours | Variable, overtime norms strongest here | Generally better, startup variance | Generally best, and paid leave actually taken |
| The catch | Hardest for a foreigner, and the deepest integration | The realistic sweet spot for most foreign engineers | You may end up in an English bubble and learn little Japanese |

**Compensation, honestly.** Software engineering salaries in Japan are materially lower than in the United States, and broadly comparable to or somewhat below Western European levels, with the gap widening at senior levels. Foreign-owned firms pay far more than domestic ones for equivalent work. Offsetting factors: low cost of healthcare, cheap and excellent public transport, low crime, good food at low prices, and rents outside central Tokyo that are reasonable by global-city standards. Japan is generally not the move if maximising income is the objective, and is a strong move if quality of life and living in Japan are the objective. Be clear with yourself about which one you are optimising.

**On the language question.** There is a trap on both sides. Take a category 3 job with no Japanese requirement and you can spend three years in Tokyo operating in English, which caps both your integration and, eventually, your influence. Take a category 1 job without the language and you will be miserable and ineffective. The path that actually works for most people: enter through category 2 or 3, and deliberately build Japanese outside work until you could operate in it. See [JLPT](<../JLPT/readme.md>) for how certification maps onto hiring signals and visa points - the short version is that JLPT is a screening convenience rather than a measure of usable workplace ability, and N2 is the level most commonly named in job postings.

### Being a foreign engineer there, concretely

- **Visa sponsorship is normal** for engineering roles at established companies, and the employer's HR will usually have done it before. Startups sometimes have not. Ask early. See [Chapter 10](#chapter-10---being-a-foreigner-long-term).
- **You may be the only non-Japanese person on the team**, or one of many, and which it is changes the experience completely.
- **Expect to be outside the informal information channel** initially. Decisions will appear to arrive fully formed because the nemawashi happened in Japanese in a conversation you were not in. The fix is language plus relationships, not complaint.
- **Direct feedback lands harder than you intend.** Calibrate down. What reads as normal engineering candour in Berlin or Tel Aviv can read as an attack.
- **Your technical credibility will usually be fine.** Japanese engineering organisations are generally happy to have competent foreign engineers, and competence is legible across language barriers.
- **Promotion into management** without strong Japanese is uncommon outside category 3. If a management track matters to you, the language is not optional.
- **Documentation and code comments** may be in Japanese, which is a genuine day-one obstacle and a genuine long-term asset. Yomitan-type tooling on a browser helps enormously - see [General content](<../General content/readme.md>).

## Chapter 7 - Religion and holidays

### Practice, not belief - why "what religion are you" is the wrong question

Ask a Japanese person their religion and a common answer is **無宗教 (mushuukyou)**, "no religion". The same person may well visit a shrine at New Year, have a Buddhist family grave they tend at Obon, have had a Shinto blessing as an infant, get married in a chapel with a Christian-styled ceremony, and expect a Buddhist funeral. Surveys asking about religious *affiliation* and surveys asking about religious *practice* in Japan produce wildly different pictures, and both are accurate.

The resolution is that the question imports a Western frame. In Japan, religion is generally organised around **practice, occasion and place** rather than around exclusive membership and propositional belief. Doing a Shinto thing does not make you a Shintoist any more than carrying an umbrella makes you a meteorologist. There is no expectation of exclusivity, no conversion, no congregation you belong to, and for most people no creed. What there is: customs attached to life events, seasonal observances, local shrines with local deities, and a household relationship with ancestors.

Historically this was formalised. **神仏習合 (shinbutsu shuugou)** - the syncretic fusion of Shinto and Buddhism - was the norm for over a thousand years: kami were understood as manifestations of buddhas, shrines and temples shared sites and personnel. The Meiji government forcibly separated them (**神仏分離**), in a campaign that included a wave of destruction of Buddhist property (廃仏毀釈), in order to construct **国家神道 (State Shinto)** - Shinto reorganised as a state institution with the emperor at its centre, used to underwrite nationalism and, by the 1930s and 40s, militarism. The Occupation disestablished State Shinto in 1945, which is why the constitutional separation of religion and state is taken seriously in Japanese jurisprudence and why prime ministerial visits to Yasukuni raise constitutional as well as diplomatic objections ([Chapter 1](#contested-memory---what-a-learner-should-know-exists)).

Two other pieces worth knowing: **new religious movements (新宗教)** are numerous and some are very large, several with political arms - Soka Gakkai's relationship with the Komeito party is the most significant; and Aum Shinrikyo's 1995 subway attack produced lasting public suspicion of religious movements generally, which is part of why "religious" carries a slightly negative connotation in contemporary Japanese and why mushuukyou is such a comfortable self-description.

**Christianity** has a long and dramatic history - sixteenth-century Jesuit missions, substantial conversions, then violent suppression and the 隠れキリシタン (hidden Christians) who maintained a syncretised underground practice for two centuries - but Christians remain a small percentage of the population. Its cultural footprint is nonetheless large, mostly in the form of wedding aesthetics and Christmas.

### Shrines and temples - telling them apart and behaving at each

| | **Shrine (神社 jinja)** | **Temple (寺 tera / -ji)** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Religion | Shinto | Buddhism |
| Object | 神 kami - deities, spirits, forces, ancestors, occasionally historical people | Buddhas and bodhisattvas |
| Entrance marker | **鳥居 torii** gate - the single most reliable identifier | 山門 sanmon gate, often with guardian statues |
| Guardians | 狛犬 komainu, lion-dogs, or foxes at Inari shrines | Nio guardian figures |
| Name endings | 神社, 神宮 (jingu), 大社 (taisha), 宮 (gu) | 寺 (-ji, -dera), 院 (-in), 庵 |
| Visible features | Purification basin, shimenawa straw ropes, paper shide, ema plaques, vermilion structures | Incense burner, pagoda, large Buddha statues, cemeteries, bells |
| Associated events | New Year, weddings, births, festivals, exam-passing prayers | Funerals, memorial services, Obon, ancestors |
| Clapping | **Yes** | **No** |

**How to visit a shrine:**

1. **Bow once** at the torii before entering. Walk to the **side** of the path - the centre is for the kami.
2. At the **手水舎 (temizuya)** purification basin: take the ladle in your right hand, rinse your left; switch, rinse your right; pour into your cupped left hand and rinse your mouth (do not drink from the ladle, and spit beside the basin, not into it); rinse your left hand again; stand the ladle upright to rinse the handle.
3. At the hall, toss a coin into the **賽銭箱 (saisenbako)**. Five yen is traditional (ご縁 - a pun on "connection").
4. Ring the bell if there is one.
5. **二礼二拍手一礼** - two bows, two claps, one bow. Pray silently between the claps and the final bow. Local variations exist; Izumo Taisha uses four claps.
6. Bow again on the way out at the torii.

**How to visit a temple:** bow at the gate; purify if there is a basin; light incense at the burner and waft the smoke toward yourself if you wish; put a coin in the offering box; **put your hands together (合掌) and bow - do not clap**; bow leaving.

**Things you can buy or do at either:** **おみくじ (omikuji)** fortune slips - tie a bad one to the rack provided to leave the bad luck behind; **お守り (omamori)** protective charms for specific purposes (traffic safety, exams, childbirth, love), conventionally replaced annually; **絵馬 (ema)** wooden plaques you write a wish on and hang; **御朱印 (goshuin)** calligraphic seal-stamps collected in a dedicated book, which is a popular hobby and a genuinely good excuse to visit places.

Household altars: the **仏壇 (butsudan)** is the Buddhist altar for the family's ancestors, with memorial tablets, offerings of rice and water, and incense. The **神棚 (kamidana)** is a small Shinto shelf-shrine. A traditional home has both, which is the syncretism in physical form.

### Life events and their division of labour

The allocation is remarkably tidy and worth learning because it explains a lot of media:

- **Birth and early childhood - Shinto.** お宮参り (omiyamairi), taking a newborn to the shrine; **七五三 (shichi-go-san)** in November, when children aged three, five and seven are dressed formally and taken to a shrine.
- **Coming of age - civil, with shrine visits.** 成人の日 (Coming of Age Day) in January, with formal furisode kimono and municipal ceremonies. Note the age of majority was lowered to 18 relatively recently, which has complicated the ceremony's organisation.
- **Weddings - either, or both, or neither.** 神前式 (Shinto ceremony at a shrine, with the san-san-kudo sake ritual and traditional dress) and Christian-style chapel weddings are both extremely common, the latter usually conducted at a wedding venue by an officiant who may or may not be clergy, chosen for aesthetics rather than faith. Also 人前式, a secular ceremony before assembled guests. The reception is the main event, heavily formatted, with speeches, outfit changes, and 引出物 gifts for guests.
- **Funerals - Buddhist, overwhelmingly.** A wake (通夜), then the funeral, incense offered by each mourner, a posthumous Buddhist name (戒名) conferred, then cremation - which is close to universal in Japan - and the collection of the ashes by family members using chopsticks, which is the origin of the chopstick taboos in [Chapter 5](#chapter-5---etiquette-practically). Mourners wear black and bring 香典 condolence money. Memorial services follow at prescribed intervals, and the grave is maintained by the family. Note the growing practical problem of grave succession as families shrink, which has produced new options such as tree burials and collective graves.

The joke Japanese people make about this themselves - born Shinto, married Christian, buried Buddhist - is accurate and is not experienced as contradictory.

### The year

This calendar is worth knowing in detail, because **the vocabulary and media payoff is enormous.** Anime and drama seasons are structured around it, half of all incidental dialogue references it, and seasonal words are a formal requirement in poetry.

**正月 / お正月 - New Year.** The biggest holiday of the year by a wide margin, and the closest Japanese equivalent to Christmas in Western countries in terms of social weight. Companies close for roughly a week. Components:

- **年末 preparation** - 大掃除 (deep cleaning), 年賀状 (new year cards, sent in bulk and delivered on January 1), 忘年会 parties, 大晦日 (New Year's Eve) with 年越しそば (year-crossing soba) and NHK's 紅白歌合戦 singing contest, and 除夜の鐘, temple bells rung 108 times.
- **初詣 (hatsumoude)** - the first shrine visit of the year, made by enormous numbers of people in the first few days. Major shrines get millions of visitors.
- **おせち料理 (osechi)** - elaborate boxed New Year food, each item symbolic, prepared in advance so nobody cooks. Plus **お雑煮 (ozouni)**, a mochi soup that varies by region, and 鏡餅 decorative mochi.
- **お年玉 (otoshidama)** - money in decorated envelopes given to children by relatives. The reason children like New Year.
- Decorations: 門松 (pine gate decorations), しめ飾り, and 書き初め (first calligraphy of the year).

**節分 (Setsubun)**, early February - the eve of spring in the old calendar. **豆まき (mamemaki)**: throwing roasted soybeans while shouting **鬼は外、福は内** ("demons out, fortune in"), often with someone wearing an oni mask, then eating your age in beans. **恵方巻 (ehoumaki)** - eating an uncut sushi roll facing a specified lucky direction - is a genuinely modern commercial custom that spread nationally from the Kansai region, substantially pushed by convenience stores. A good example of how fast a "tradition" can be manufactured.

**ひな祭り (Hinamatsuri)**, March 3 - Girls' Day / Doll Festival. Elaborate tiered displays of imperial-court dolls, hishimochi and shirozake. Superstition holds you must put the dolls away promptly afterwards.

**卒業式 and 入学式** - graduation in March and school entrance in April. **The Japanese school and fiscal year begins in April**, which is a structurally important fact: it is why April is the season of new beginnings in Japanese media rather than September, why cherry blossom is associated with starting things, and why anime school-years run on a different rhythm than a Western viewer expects.

**花見 (hanami)**, late March to early April in much of Honshu - cherry blossom viewing. The **桜前線** (cherry blossom front) is forecast and tracked on the news as it moves north, arriving earliest in Kyushu and Okinawa and reaching Hokkaido around May. Hanami is a party: tarpaulins under the trees, food, a great deal of alcohol, and in a corporate setting the junior employee sent hours early to hold the spot. The cultural loading is on transience - the blossom is beautiful because it falls in a week. Also 花見団子, 桜餅, and every conceivable sakura-flavoured product.

**ゴールデンウィーク (Golden Week)**, late April into early May - a cluster of national holidays: Showa Day on April 29, Constitution Memorial Day on May 3, Greenery Day on May 4 and Children's Day on May 5. With weekends this produces the longest common holiday block of the year, and therefore the most expensive and crowded travel period. **こどもの日 / 端午の節句** on May 5 brings 鯉のぼり (carp streamers) and kabuto helmet displays.

**梅雨** in June, then **七夕 (Tanabata)**, July 7 in most places and August in some regions - the star festival, based on a legend of two separated lovers who meet once a year across the Milky Way. Wishes written on **短冊 (tanzaku)** paper strips and hung on bamboo. Sendai's Tanabata is the most famous.

**お盆 (Obon)**, mid-August in most of the country and mid-July in some areas including parts of Tokyo - the festival of the returning ancestral spirits. Families return to their home towns, clean and visit graves (お墓参り), light 迎え火 and 送り火 fires to guide the spirits home and back, float lanterns (灯籠流し), and dance the **盆踊り (bon odori)** at community gatherings. Combined with the **夏祭り (summer festival)** season - yukata, food stalls (屋台), goldfish scooping, 花火大会 fireworks displays, shrine floats and portable shrines (神輿 mikoshi) - this is the densest cultural month of the year and the single most heavily depicted period in anime. Obon is also, with New Year and Golden Week, one of the three great national travel crushes. Summer is additionally the **ghost season**, which is why Japanese horror releases cluster there and why characters tell ghost stories in August episodes.

**Autumn** brings 敬老の日 (Respect for the Aged Day), the autumnal equinox holiday, and the occasional **シルバーウィーク (Silver Week)** - a September holiday cluster that only lines up into a long run in some years, depending on where the dates fall. Plus 運動会 (school sports days), 文化祭 (school culture festivals - another anime staple), 紅葉狩り (autumn leaf viewing), and 月見 (moon viewing) with 月見団子.

**七五三 (Shichi-go-san)** in November, and 勤労感謝の日 (Labour Thanksgiving Day), which descends from an ancient harvest festival.

**Christmas** is a secular commercial event with its own local rules, and a notably **romantic** rather than familial one - Christmas Eve is a couples' date night, the equivalent of Valentine's Day elsewhere. **Christmas cake** (strawberry sponge) is near-universal, and **KFC** is a genuine Christmas tradition, the result of a successful 1970s advertising campaign, to the point that people pre-order buckets weeks ahead. December 25 is not a public holiday and people work. Illuminations are a major seasonal attraction.

**バレンタインデー (Valentine's Day)**, February 14, runs on an inversion: **women give chocolate to men.** The categories are explicit - **本命チョコ** (for someone you actually like), **義理チョコ** (obligation chocolate for male colleagues and bosses), **友チョコ** (for female friends, a newer and now dominant category), and 自分チョコ (for yourself). Note that giri-choko is in visible decline - some companies have banned it as a form of workplace pressure, and the commercial emphasis has shifted toward friend and self gifting. **ホワイトデー (White Day)**, March 14, is the reciprocal: men who received chocolate return a gift, conventionally of greater value. White Day was invented by the Japanese confectionery industry, which is exactly the kind of fact worth holding on to when you are tempted to read every custom as ancient.

**ハロウィン (Halloween)** has grown rapidly into a large costume-and-street event, particularly in Shibuya, with essentially no trick-or-treating and considerable public-order concern.

Japan has a comparatively large number of national holidays - currently around sixteen - and a **ハッピーマンデー** system that moves several to Mondays to create long weekends, plus 振替休日 substitute holidays when a holiday falls on a Sunday. The combination of many fixed holidays and low paid-leave usage produces the defining feature of Japanese travel: **everyone is off at the same time**, three times a year, which makes those periods expensive and crowded and the rest of the year pleasantly quiet.

## Chapter 8 - Food culture

Food is the highest-yield vocabulary domain in Japanese for a learner, for three reasons: the words are extremely high-frequency, they appear constantly in every genre of media, and you will use them daily from the moment you arrive. It is also the area where a small amount of cultural knowledge converts immediately into a better life.

### Principles

**和食 (washoku)** - Japanese cuisine as a category, added to UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list in 2013, which is itself a piece of cultural politics worth noticing. Its conventional structure is **一汁三菜 (ichiju-sansai)**: rice, one soup, three side dishes. Rice is not a side; it is the centre, and everything else is technically an accompaniment to it - which is why ご飯 means both "cooked rice" and "a meal", and why おかず means specifically the things you eat *with* rice.

**旬 (shun)** - seasonality, in the strong sense: the idea that each ingredient has a correct moment and is best then. This is not marketing, it is a structuring principle. Menus change, sweets change, convenience store products change, and 初物 (the first of the season) carries real value. It links directly to the seasonal-word system in [Chapter 7](#the-year).

**旨味 (umami)** and **出汁 (dashi)** - the savoury fifth taste, identified by a Japanese chemist, and the stock that delivers it. Dashi is made from **鰹節 (katsuobushi**, dried bonito flakes), **昆布 (kombu**, kelp), 煮干し (dried sardines) or dried shiitake. This matters practically: **dashi is in almost everything**, and most of it contains fish. Miso soup, simmered dishes, noodle broth, tamagoyaki, dipping sauces. A strict vegetarian or vegan in Japan has a genuinely harder time than in most developed countries, and the phrase 出汁も入っていませんか ("does it not even contain dashi?") is one you will need. Kombu-only dashi exists and 精進料理 (Buddhist temple cuisine) is properly vegetarian, but the default is not.

Other principles: an emphasis on **presentation and vessel** - the dish is chosen for the food, and lacquerware, ceramics and arrangement are part of the meal; a preference for **minimal intervention** in high-end cooking, letting the ingredient show; and **もったいない (mottainai)**, the sense of waste as something close to a moral failure, which is why you finish your rice.

### Regional cuisine, and why it matters for reading

Japanese food is strongly regional, and this is not trivia - it is how menus and place names connect.

| Region / city | Known for |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Kansai vs Kanto flavour** | A real and much-discussed split. Kansai uses lighter soy sauce (薄口醤油) and dashi-forward broth; Kanto uses darker soy (濃口) and stronger seasoning. The same dish in Osaka and Tokyo tastes and looks different. Kansai leans udon, Kanto leans soba |
| **Osaka** | 食い倒れ - "eat until you drop". Takoyaki, okonomiyaki (batter-mixed style), kushikatsu (with the famous no-double-dipping rule), kitsune udon |
| **Hiroshima** | Okonomiyaki built in layers with noodles - a genuinely different dish from the Osaka version, and a point of real regional rivalry. Oysters |
| **Kyoto** | 京料理, 懐石 (kaiseki multi-course haute cuisine), おばんざい home-style small dishes, tofu and yuba, matcha and wagashi |
| **Nagoya** | 味噌カツ (miso-sauce pork cutlet), ひつまぶし (eel over rice), 手羽先, and the red-miso palate generally |
| **Fukuoka / Hakata** | 豚骨ラーメン, mentaiko (spiced cod roe), motsunabe, and 屋台 street food stalls along the river |
| **Sapporo / Hokkaido** | Miso ramen, soup curry, seafood, dairy, Genghis Khan grilled lamb, crab |
| **Sendai / Tohoku** | 牛タン (grilled beef tongue), ずんだ (edamame paste sweets), sake |
| **Okinawa** | A distinct cuisine: ゴーヤチャンプルー (bitter melon stir-fry), 沖縄そば (wheat noodles, nothing like soba), ラフテー (braised pork belly), 海ぶどう, and awamori spirit |
| **Kagawa** | 讃岐うどん - thick chewy udon, a regional identity in its own right |

The practical payoff: **regional names on a menu are information.** 博多ラーメン tells you tonkotsu. 讃岐うどん tells you the texture. 名古屋 anything tells you red miso. And 名物 (meibutsu, local speciality) signage is everywhere, which is also why the **omiyage** system in [Chapter 5](#gift-giving) works - the gift is specifically the regional food of the place you went.

This is the actionable part. Concrete vocabulary and mechanics:

| Word | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- |
| 定食 teishoku | Set meal - main, rice, miso soup, pickles. The default lunch |
| 丼 don / どんぶり | A bowl of rice with something on it - 牛丼, 親子丼, 天丼, 海鮮丼 |
| 単品 tanpin | A la carte, as opposed to a set |

This is one of the fastest, most tangible rewards of learning to read - and a good practical reason to prioritise food kanji early ([Vocabulary](<../Vocabulary/readme.md>)).

### Venues

| Type | What it is |
| :--- | :--- |
| **居酒屋 izakaya** | The central social institution. Small dishes, lots of drinks, shared plates, loud, cheap to mid-range. Where work relationships get built ([Chapter 6](#the-social-layer)) |
| **定食屋 / 食堂** | Cheap set-meal restaurants. Unglamorous and excellent |
| **ラーメン屋** | Ticket machine, counter seating, ten minutes, no conversation. Regional and shop-specific styles, plus 替え玉 (extra noodles) at tonkotsu shops |
| **回転寿司 kaitenzushi** | Conveyor or touchscreen-ordered sushi. Cheap, fast, family-friendly, and now largely tablet-ordered rather than belt-grabbing |
| **立ち飲み tachinomi** | Standing bars. Very cheap, very quick, often excellent |

| **ファミレス famiresu** | Family restaurants - Saizeriya, Gusto. Cheap, endless, open late, drink bars, and a legitimate place to study for three hours |

### Konbini as infrastructure

Calling Japanese convenience stores "convenience stores" undersells them. A 7-Eleven, Lawson or FamilyMart is a piece of civic infrastructure, and for a new resident it is the single most useful building on your street. What you can do there:

- Buy genuinely decent food - onigiri, bento, sandwiches, oden in winter, fried chicken, and an entire rotating seasonal product line
- **Pay bills** - utilities, taxes, national health insurance, and most things that arrive as a paper slip with a barcode
- **Use an ATM** that accepts foreign cards, 24 hours
- **Send and receive parcels**, including the 宅配 delivery services, and pick up online orders
- **Print, scan, copy and fax**, and print documents from the cloud
- Buy concert, bus and theme park **tickets** from the multi-function terminal
- Buy stamps, batteries, umbrellas, shirts, chargers, phone credit
- Use a clean toilet

They are open 24 hours (though staffing shortages have pushed some to reduce this), they are everywhere, and the food quality is genuinely high by international convenience-store standards. Do not let anyone tell you eating konbini food is a failure state; it is normal for everyone.

## Chapter 9 - Pop culture

Two jobs in this chapter. The first is the actual history of manga, anime, games, music and fandom, because it is genuinely interesting and because knowing it makes you a better reader of the medium. The second is [the honest conversation](#anime-is-not-a-documentary) about what anime does and does not tell you about Japan. If you only read one part of this guide's later chapters, read that one.

### Manga

**Claimed ancestry, with a caveat.** Popular accounts trace manga to Edo-period woodblock prints, the 鳥獣戯画 animal scrolls, and 黄表紙 illustrated popular books, and the word 漫画 was used by Hokusai. This lineage is real as a lineage of Japanese pictorial narrative, and substantially overstated as a direct causal ancestry - modern manga owes at least as much to imported American and European comic strips and to cinema. Treat the ancient-origins story as national-heritage framing rather than history.

**Prewar and wartime.** Newspaper strips and satirical cartooning develop from the Meiji period; Rakuten Kitazawa and Okamoto Ippei are the significant early figures. 紙芝居 (kamishibai), travelling picture-card storytelling, is an important popular ancestor of both manga and anime narration.

**Tezuka Osamu** is the pivot. Working from the late 1940s, Tezuka established the postwar form: long-form serialised **story manga** with cinematic panel construction, dynamic camera angles, emotional interiority, and genre ambition well beyond children's humour. *New Treasure Island* and then *Astro Boy* (鉄腕アトム) are the landmarks. He is called the god of manga for a reason, and reading him is not homework - *Black Jack* and *Phoenix* hold up.

**The magazine system**, which is the structural fact that shapes everything. Manga is published serially in thick weekly or monthly anthology magazines, cheap and disposable, and collected later into 単行本 volumes. Shonen Magazine and Shonen Sunday launched in 1959; **Weekly Shonen Jump** in 1968, and Jump's reader-survey-driven model - popular series continue, unpopular series are cancelled fast - has shaped the entire industry's incentives toward strong hooks and escalating stakes. The demographic labels are magazine categories, not genres:

| Label | Nominal audience | Reality |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 少年 shonen | Boys | Read by everyone. Jump, Magazine, Sunday |
| 少女 shoujo | Girls | Romance-heavy, historically the most formally experimental in panel layout |
| 青年 seinen | Adult men | Where most of the psychologically serious and violent work lives - Young Animal, Young Jump, Big Comic, Afternoon |
| 女性 josei | Adult women | Adult relationships, work, the most realistic register in mainstream manga |

**Gekiga and the adult turn.** In the late 1950s and 60s, Tatsumi Yoshihiro and others coined 劇画 (gekiga, "dramatic pictures") to distinguish serious adult comics from children's manga - darker, socially realistic, often bleak. The magazine **ガロ (Garo)**, from 1964, became the home of avant-garde and alternative work (Tsuge Yoshiharu, Mizuki Shigeru's early work, later Maruo Suehiro). This is the lineage that makes seinen possible.

**Shoujo's revolution.** The **24年組 (Year 24 Group)** - Hagio Moto, Takemiya Keiko, Oshima Yumiko and others, from the 1970s - transformed shoujo manga from formulaic romance into work dealing with sexuality, gender, alienation and science fiction, with radically experimental page composition. Their influence on everything subsequent, including work aimed at men, is hard to overstate, and they are the origin point of the boys' love genre.

**Notable examples in the seinen/gekiga lineage:** Miura Kentaro's **Berserk** ran in Young Animal, a seinen magazine, and is the outstanding example of long-form dark fantasy in the medium. **Oshi no Ko** (Akasaka Aka and Mengo Yokoyari) ran in Weekly Young Jump. **Chainsaw Man** (Fujimoto Tatsuki) began in Weekly Shonen Jump and moved to the Jump+ digital platform - and Fujimoto is a good example of an author whose formal playfulness and film literacy come straight out of the gekiga-to-seinen lineage even while publishing in a shonen magazine. Readers drawn to that register may also want Urasawa Naoki, Asano Inio, Taiyo Matsumoto, Ito Junji, and going further back, Tatsumi and Hagio.

**The current state:** print magazine circulation has collapsed, digital and app-based serialisation (Jump+, Manga One, Comico) has partly replaced it, webtoons are a competitive pressure, and the international market is now large enough to influence what gets made. Manga sales are healthy; the magazine model is not.

### Anime

**Origins.** Japanese animation dates to the 1910s, and there is a body of prewar short work. Toei Doga (now Toei Animation), founded in the 1950s, produced the first feature-length colour animated films, aiming explicitly to be the Disney of Asia.

**Television changes everything.** *Astro Boy* (鉄腕アトム) began broadcasting in **1963**, adapted from Tezuka's manga and produced by his own studio. To make weekly television animation economically possible on a tiny budget, it pioneered **limited animation** techniques - held frames, repeated cycles, three-frame shooting, panning stills, mouths animated in isolation. This is a hugely consequential decision. It made TV anime viable and it locked in an aesthetic - the still shot with dramatic lighting, the speed-line rush, the long reaction beat - that became a *style* rather than merely a cost saving. It also established the low-budget, high-volume production economics the industry still struggles with.

**The decades, briefly:**

- **1970s** - the robot boom (Mazinger Z, Gundam), and *Mobile Suit Gundam* in 1979 proving that animation could carry a serious war story for an older audience. *Space Battleship Yamato* had already shown there was a teenage and adult market. The birth of organised fandom.
- **1980s** - bubble money, the **OVA** (original video animation) format selling directly to fans on video and enabling material too niche or too adult for broadcast, Studio Ghibli founded in **1985** after *Nausicaa* (1984), and *Akira* (1988) demonstrating what an enormous budget could do.
- **1990s** - *Sailor Moon* and *Dragon Ball Z* as global gateways, and **Neon Genesis Evangelion** (1995-96) as the watershed: a mecha show that turned into a psychological and theological interrogation of its own genre, made under famously difficult conditions, and released into a Japan still processing the Kobe earthquake and the Aum subway attack of that same year. Its influence on everything after is enormous and its cultural context is specific - it is a document of 1990s Japanese anxiety, not a timeless artefact.
- **2000s** - the international boom (Pokemon, Naruto, Bleach, One Piece), *Spirited Away* winning the Academy Award for animated feature in the early 2000s, and domestically the **late-night broadcast model**: shows aired after midnight as advertisements for DVD box sets sold to a small number of devoted buyers. That funding model is why 2000s anime is so tightly targeted at otaku taste.
- **2010s to now** - streaming money (Netflix, Crunchyroll, Chinese platforms) replacing disc sales, international audiences influencing greenlighting, simulcast subtitling killing the fansub ecosystem, an enormous increase in the number of shows per season, and record-breaking box office for theatrical releases, notably the *Demon Slayer* film in 2020 which became Japan's highest-grossing film.

**The production committee system, and why it matters.** Most anime is financed by a **製作委員会** - a consortium of a publisher, a television station, a record label, a merchandise company, a distributor and others, each buying a share of the rights in its own domain. This spreads risk and is a sensible reason the industry produces as much as it does. It also means:

- The **animation studio is usually a contractor**, not a rights holder. A studio can produce an enormous hit and see very little of the upside.
- Creative decisions are distributed across stakeholders with different interests.
- Margins at the studio level are thin, which pushes down wages and pushes work to subcontractors, increasingly overseas.

**Animator working conditions are a real and documented problem**, not a fan grievance. Industry body surveys have repeatedly found very low pay for junior animators, particularly 動画 (in-between) artists who are frequently paid per drawing at rates that produce poverty-level annual income, along with very long hours and precarious freelance status. High-profile production crunches, delayed episodes and staff burnout are regular news. There have been unionisation efforts, some studio-level reforms, a training-support scheme, and increased pay at some larger studios, and streaming money has improved things at the top end. The structural problem is not solved. This is worth knowing because "support the industry by paying for anime" is a real argument and also an incomplete one - much of the money does not reach the people drawing.

### Games

- **Arcades first.** Space Invaders and the late-70s arcade boom, and the ゲームセンター as a physical social space that still exists - now dominated by crane games (UFOキャッチャー), rhythm games, photo booths (プリクラ) and card-based arcade titles.
- **Famicom (1983)**, released as the NES abroad, rescues the home console market and makes Nintendo a global company. Then the rivalry era: Nintendo versus Sega, and from 1994 Sony's PlayStation, which shifts the industry to CD-ROM and to a broader adult audience.
- **The JRPG**, essentially a Japanese contribution as a form: *Dragon Quest* (1986) and *Final Fantasy* (1987) establishing the template, then a 1990s golden age. Its conventions - turn-based menus, party dynamics, long narrative, character art - are a distinct design tradition, and one that is genuinely good Japanese reading practice if you can handle the volume of text.
- **The 2000s decline.** Japanese studios largely missed the shift to HD development, Western-style online play and the PC market, and global commercial dominance moved to American and European publishers. This is a real and much-analysed transition, not a nationalist grievance - Tofugu's mirror has [an article on it](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japan/why-japan-no-longer-dominates-the-video-game-industry/index.html>).
- **The partial resurgence.** FromSoftware's Souls lineage, Nintendo's Switch era, Capcom's recent run, and a healthy mid-size and indie scene.
- **Mobile and gacha** now dominate domestic revenue. Free-to-play with randomised paid draws, aggressive live-service operation, and enormous sums involved. Gacha games are also, incidentally, a reliable source of dense casual Japanese text.
- **Doujin and visual novels.** An entire parallel independent scene. Visual novels in particular are one of the best Japanese reading resources that exists - text-heavy, voiced, paced by the reader - and [Reading](<../Reading/readme.md>) owns their place in the reading ladder.

### Music and idols

**The idol system.** Japanese idols (アイドル) are not simply pop stars. The model is built on **accessibility and development rather than finished excellence** - fans support a performer who is visibly amateur and improving, and the emotional core of the product is that relationship. The machinery around it includes handshake events, photo sessions, single releases with multiple variant covers to encourage multiple purchases, and in the AKB48 system, a voting "election" decided by purchased ballots.

The main lineages: the male-idol agency founded by Johnny Kitagawa, which dominated male pop for decades and which was **restructured and renamed following the public confirmation in 2023 of long-running sexual abuse by its founder and its cover-up** - a genuinely major event in the Japanese entertainment industry, and one with implications for how media handled the story for decades. On the female side, Hello! Project and Morning Musume, then AKB48 and its regional sister groups, then the current diversified landscape including Nogizaka46 and a large idol underground.

Other pieces:
- **Vocaloid** - synthesised singing software, with **初音ミク (Hatsune Miku, 2007)** as the breakout. Crucially this is a **user-generated** phenomenon: the character is a platform, tens of thousands of amateur producers wrote songs for her, and a whole generation of professional Japanese musicians came out of that scene. Culturally this is one of the most interesting things Japan has done with music.
- **City pop**, the 1980s bubble-era sound, revived internationally by algorithmic recommendation, which is a nice example of Japanese domestic pop culture being rediscovered from outside.
- **アニソン (anisong)** as its own commercially significant genre with dedicated artists and festivals.
- **The domestic orientation of the industry.** Japan has historically been a large enough music market to be self-sufficient, with unusually high physical-media sales and a long-lived CD rental system, which meant Japanese artists had little need to seek foreign audiences. That is changing - anime-tied songs by artists such as YOASOBI and Ado have found large international streaming audiences - but the industry's structures and rights practices still reflect the domestic-first era.
- **Karaoke** as a genuine social institution, in private rooms rather than in bars, and 一人カラオケ (solo karaoke) as a normal activity.

### VTubers, doujin and fandom

**VTubers.** Virtual YouTubers - performers using animated avatars with real-time motion capture. **Kizuna AI (2016)** popularised the form; the agencies **hololive** and **Nijisanji** industrialised it. The dominant format is long-form live streaming - gaming, chatting, singing - monetised through superchats and memberships. Two things are worth noting: it is a significant, genuinely new entertainment form that Japan invented, and **it is exceptional listening material** - hours of unscripted, casual, high-energy Japanese with a chat log providing context, which is very hard to source otherwise. [Listening](<../Listening/readme.md>) owns how to use it as a resource.

**同人 (doujin) and Comiket.** 同人誌 are self-published fan or original works. **コミックマーケット (Comiket)**, held twice a year at Tokyo Big Sight, is the largest such event in the world, drawing hundreds of thousands of attendees across a few days, with tens of thousands of participating circles. The ecosystem includes Pixiv for illustration, Niconico for video, and specialist retailers (Toranoana, Melonbooks) and the Booth marketplace.

The legally interesting part: **most derivative doujinshi is technically copyright infringement, and is broadly tolerated.** Rights holders generally do not enforce, because the doujin scene functions as a talent pipeline and a marketing engine - a great many professional manga artists came out of it. Some franchises publish explicit derivative-work guidelines, and *Touhou Project* is famous for a permissive stance that produced an enormous independent music, game and art economy around it. This is an informal equilibrium, not a legal right, and it occasionally breaks.

**オタク (otaku).** The word's trajectory is a small social history in itself. It derives from お宅, a formal second-person pronoun, reportedly used among fans addressing each other; a magazine columnist popularised it as a label in the early 1980s, with a mocking edge. It turned sharply negative after a 1989 murder case whose perpetrator was reported as an obsessive fan, triggering years of media hostility toward fan culture. Through the 2000s it normalised - the *Densha Otoko* phenomenon, the commercial visibility of Akihabara, the government's own Cool Japan promotion - and today 〜オタク largely just means "enthusiast", attaching freely to non-fan interests (鉄道オタク for trainspotters, 歴史オタク). It retains some negative charge for some speakers and some generations, and 推し活 ("oshi activity" - the practice of supporting a favourite) is the current, entirely positive framing.

Current fandom vocabulary, which is fun and register-marked: **推し (oshi)** - your favourite, the one you support; 推す as a verb; 沼 ("swamp") for a fandom you have fallen into; 尊い ("precious/sacred") for something overwhelming; 神回 for a great episode; 二次元 for the fictional world as against 三次元 reality; 聖地巡礼 for visiting the real locations a work is set in. *Bunny Girl Senpai* is a good example of the last - it is set in recognisable Fujisawa, Enoshima and Kamakura, and people do go.

**Places.** **Akihabara** shifted from a postwar radio-parts market to electronics to its current identity as the anime, game and idol district, with maid cafes, arcades and specialist retail - and it has gentrified and diversified considerably from its 2000s peak. **Nakano Broadway** is the collector's alternative: a cramped mall of secondhand specialist shops, generally considered better than Akihabara for actually finding things. **Ikebukuro's Otome Road** serves a predominantly female fandom. Osaka's **Den Den Town** is the Kansai equivalent. Chains: Mandarake for secondhand, Book-Off for cheap used manga, Animate for new goods.

### Anime is not a documentary

This is the part that matters, and it is worth being direct about, because being vague here would be less useful.

**Anime is a stylised, genre-bound fictional medium with strong internal conventions.** It is not reportage about Japan, and it is not even trying to be. Live-action drama is closer but also heavily conventionalised. If your model of Japanese society comes primarily from anime, you will be wrong in **specific, predictable, listable** ways - which is good news, because listable errors are fixable.

**1. Speech registers.** This is the big one, and it is the one with real social consequences. Anime dialogue is written to make characters instantly legible, so speech is used as costume:

- Rough masculine speech - 俺, 〜だぜ, 〜ぞ, てめえ, 〜やがる, お前 - marks a character type. Real men mostly do not talk like this to anyone but close friends, and never at work.
- Archaic and fictional pronouns - わし, 拙者, 我輩, 余 - do not exist in modern speech.
- Extreme feminine forms - 〜ですわ, 〜かしら, わたくし - are ojou-sama markers, largely absent from real speech.
- Heavy sentence-final particle stacking, verbal tics and catchphrases are character-design devices.
- **Real adult speech is much flatter.** Long stretches of ですます in any public or semi-public setting, heavy hedging, half-finished sentences, constant aizuchi, and far less emphatic particle use. It is, honestly, less fun and much more useful.
- **The consequence:** vocabulary and phrasing learned from anime and deployed on a stranger, a colleague or an interviewer ranges from odd to genuinely rude. This is the single most common failure mode of anime-derived Japanese, and it is why [Speaking](<../Speaking/readme.md>) has a section on exactly this trap. Mine anime for vocabulary, but **tag the register when you mine it** ([AJATT](<../AJATT/readme.md>), [Vocabulary](<../Vocabulary/readme.md>)).

**2. Names and address.** Anime characters use first names, nicknames and casual address constantly, because the audience needs to track them and because intimacy is dramatically useful. **Real Japanese adults default to surname + さん and often stay there indefinitely**, including with people they genuinely consider friends. あなた is essentially not used to address someone. Dropping honorifics is a meaningful act ([Chapter 4](#other-pieces-of-the-system)). Getting this wrong makes you look presumptuous in a way that is hard to recover from.

**3. School life.** Japanese high school in anime is a space of freedom, romance, club adventures, rooftop conversations and casual teacher relationships. Real Japanese high school is dominated by **entrance-exam pressure** for the next stage, 塾 (cram school) in the evenings, club activity that is frequently a grinding daily obligation rather than a hobby, rigid rules on appearance, and comparatively little free time or romance. Teachers work extremely long hours, partly because they run the clubs. The rooftop is usually locked. The culture festival and sports day are real; the amount of unsupervised time is not.

**4. Workplaces.** Anime offices are either comedic or heroic. Real Japanese offices are quiet, process-heavy and consensus-driven ([Chapter 6](#chapter-6---work-culture)). The shouting boss, the brilliant maverick who is forgiven because he is right, and the dramatic public confrontation that resolves a conflict are all fiction - and the last one is actively dangerous to imitate, because the real resolution channel is private and preparatory. If your model of workplace conflict comes from fiction, you will burn a relationship you need.

**5. Strangers.** Real interaction with strangers in Japan is minimal, brief and formal. **People do not strike up conversations on trains.** Nobody will ask you where you are going. The chance encounter that starts a plot is a plot device, and the general baseline of unsolicited social contact is considerably lower than in most Western countries - which is comfortable for some people and isolating for others, and is one of the main things that determines whether someone is happy living there.

**6. Gender.** Anime's treatment of gender ranges from nostalgic to fantastical to frankly fetishistic, and you cannot read any of it as description. The real situation is more complicated and worth stating carefully: **Japan consistently ranks low among wealthy democracies on international gender-gap measures**, driven especially by low female representation in management and in politics; at the same time female labour-force participation is high and rising, legal protections have expanded, and attitudes differ sharply by generation. Neither the regressive nor the progressive reading of anime gender dynamics is reportage. Same for depictions of sexuality and LGBT life, where legal recognition (notably same-sex marriage) lags substantially behind a fairly permissive popular-culture presence, and where local partnership systems and ongoing litigation are the live story.

**7. Violence and crime.** Yakuza films and delinquent manga romanticise heavily. Actual violent crime rates in Japan are low by international comparison, street violence is rare, and organised crime membership has declined substantially since anti-organised-crime legislation from the 1990s onward and municipal exclusion ordinances in the 2010s. Correspondingly, the everyday sense of physical safety is genuinely high and is one of the things foreign residents consistently mention.

**8. Emotional register.** Anime is loud about feelings because drama requires it. Everyday Japanese emotional expression is more restrained, more indirect, and more likely to be carried by what is not said ([Chapter 4](#tatemae-and-honne---public-position-and-true-feeling)). The gap between these two things is not hypocrisy on anyone's part; it is the difference between a medium and a society.

**Now the other half of this, which is equally true.** Having your exposure to Japan come through anime and manga is an **enormous asset**, not a deficiency to apologise for:

- **Motivation is the scarce resource** in language learning, not information. Years of genuine love for Japanese media is the reason you will still be doing this in eighteen months, which is the only variable that actually predicts success.
- **You already have phonology.** Thousands of hours of listening means Japanese sounds like a language to you rather than noise, and your ear for pitch and rhythm is far ahead of someone starting from a textbook.
- **You have a reference library.** When you learn a word or a construction, you have somewhere to attach it.
- **The set dressing is largely accurate.** Trains, konbini, apartment layouts, school buildings, shrine architecture, food, uniforms, street scenes, seasonal markers - background art is often meticulously researched, and this stuff is real. The unreliable layer is the *social behaviour and speech*, not the physical world.
- **Some works are genuinely informative, in a specific sense.** Not as descriptions of ordinary life, but as documents of their moment. *Evangelion* is a real artefact of 1990s Japanese anxiety and post-bubble institutional distrust. *Oshi no Ko* is unusually informative about the actual mechanics of the idol and entertainment industry, even wrapped in melodrama. *Chainsaw Man* is genre-aware in a way that tells you about the state of the medium. And it is worth noticing the reverse case: *Berserk* is European-set dark fantasy, and tells you a great deal about Miura Kentaro and nothing whatsoever about Japanese society - which is a useful reminder that "Japanese media" and "media about Japan" are different categories.

**The fix is addition, not subtraction.** Do not stop watching anime, and do not feel bad about it. Add non-fiction input - documentaries, news, vlogs, variety, streams, reality shows, interviews - until it is a meaningful fraction of your diet. Add reading that is set in ordinary life. And when you notice a behaviour you are inclined to imitate, check it against a non-fiction source first.

## Chapter 10 - Being a foreigner long-term

> **Verify everything in this chapter before acting on it.** Immigration, tax, insurance and pension rules change, and this section will go stale. It gives you the **structure** - what categories exist, what the moving parts are, what questions to ask - so that you can read the primary sources efficiently. It is not advice and it is not current. The authoritative sources are the **Immigration Services Agency of Japan (出入国在留管理庁)**, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Japanese embassies for visa applications, the National Tax Agency, the Japan Pension Service, and your own municipal office. For anything consequential, use an immigration lawyer or a 行政書士 (administrative procedures specialist). Employers with experience sponsoring foreign staff will handle most of it for you.

[JLPT](<../JLPT/readme.md>) owns how certification interacts with hiring and with the immigration points system. This chapter owns everything else about living there.

### Residence statuses, structurally

Japan does not have a general "immigration visa". You hold a **在留資格 (status of residence)** that permits a defined category of activity, and almost all work statuses are **tied to both a qualification and a sponsoring employer**. The structure is what matters:

| Category | Structure |
| :--- | :--- |
| **技術・人文知識・国際業務** (Engineer / Specialist in Humanities / International Services) | The main route for software engineers and most white-collar foreign professionals. Requires a relevant university degree or a documented period of relevant professional experience, plus a job offer in a matching field. Tied to employment, but changing employers within the same activity category is a notification rather than a new application |
| **高度専門職 (Highly Skilled Professional)** | A **points-based** status scoring education, income, age, professional qualifications and Japanese language ability. Reaching the threshold brings benefits: longer periods of stay, permission for some additional activities, easier family arrangements, and - the significant one - a much shorter qualifying period for permanent residence. There are also newer income- and education-based routes added in recent years for top-tier candidates and graduates of highly ranked universities |
| **経営・管理 (Business Manager)** | For founding or running a business. Has capital and office requirements |
| **日本人の配偶者等 (Spouse of a Japanese national)** | Not tied to an employer, permits any work, and has a much shorter path to permanent residence. Also, note, dependent on the marriage |
| **家族滞在 (Dependent)** | For the spouse and children of someone on a work status. Work is restricted; part-time work requires a separate permission to engage in activity outside the status |
| **留学 (Student)** | Study, with limited permitted working hours |
| **特定技能 (Specified Skilled Worker)** | A newer category for designated industries with labour shortages, expanded in recent years. Not the engineer route |
| **技能実習 (Technical Intern Training)** | Nominally a training programme, widely criticised domestically and internationally for exploitative conditions and treated by critics as disguised low-wage labour. Subject to ongoing reform proposals. Worth knowing about because it is central to Japan's actual immigration debate |
| **Working Holiday** | Available to nationals of countries with a bilateral arrangement, with age limits. Not available to everyone |

**永住 (Permanent Residence)** removes the employer tie and the renewal cycle, while remaining a residence status rather than citizenship. The conventionally cited requirement is a long continuous period of residence - commonly around ten years with a portion in a work status - with substantially shorter periods for highly-skilled-points holders and for spouses. Applications also assess income stability and, importantly, a **clean record of actually having paid your taxes, pension and social insurance**. That last point trips people up years later: skipping pension contributions early on can block a permanent residence application much later.

**帰化 (Naturalisation)** is separate, handled by the Ministry of Justice, and requires renouncing your other citizenship, since Japan does not generally permit dual nationality for adults. It is a genuinely different decision from permanent residence and comparatively few foreign residents take it.

**Mechanics you will deal with:**
- The **在留カード (residence card)** is issued on arrival and you are legally required to carry it.
- **Register your address at the municipal office** within a short statutory window (fourteen days) of moving in, and again every time you move. This registration is the key that unlocks health insurance, the My Number system and most other administration.
- **My Number** is the national identification number; the physical card is increasingly the gateway to online procedures and is being merged with the health insurance card.
- **Re-entry** - do not leave the country on a work status without understanding the special re-entry permit system, and check it before any trip.
- **Renewals** happen on a cycle tied to your status, and you apply before expiry. Do not let it lapse.

### Housing

This is the area where foreign residents hit the most friction, and it is worth understanding before you arrive.

**The cost structure of a standard rental** is front-loaded in a way that surprises people. Moving in typically involves several months' rent in one go:

| Item | What it is |
| :--- | :--- |
| **敷金 shikikin** | Deposit. Partly refundable, with cleaning and restoration costs deducted |
| **礼金 reikin** | "Key money" - a **non-refundable** gift to the landlord, often one to two months' rent. This is simply money you do not get back |
| Agency fee | Typically about a month's rent to the estate agent |
| Guarantor company fee | See below |
| Fire insurance | Mandatory, a modest annual sum |
| Lock exchange | Commonly charged |
| **更新料 koushinryou** | A renewal fee, often a month's rent, charged every couple of years when the contract renews |

**The guarantor problem.** Japanese rental contracts traditionally require a **保証人 (guarantor)** - a Japanese individual, usually a relative, who accepts liability for your rent. A foreign arrival does not have one. The standard solution is a **保証会社 (guarantor company)**, which charges a fee to act as guarantor; this is now the normal route and most agencies will arrange it. Some employers act as guarantor, which is worth asking about.

**Landlords who refuse foreign tenants.** This is real, common enough to be a practical planning problem, and documented in the Japanese government's own housing surveys. The stated reasons are usually about language, rubbish rules, noise, and the risk of a tenant leaving the country - which is to say a mixture of genuine practical concern and plain discrimination. The relevant point is that **Japan has no comprehensive anti-discrimination statute covering nationality in private transactions**, so there is generally no legal remedy. What actually works:

1. **Foreigner-friendly agencies** and listing services that filter for properties accepting foreign tenants.
2. **UR (Urban Renaissance) housing** - public-corporation rental housing which requires no key money, no guarantor, no agency fee and no renewal fee, and which does not discriminate on nationality. Trade-offs are location and building age, and there are income requirements. For many new arrivals this is the single best answer.
3. **Company-arranged housing** (社宅 or a company-guaranteed lease), which is the easiest path if your employer offers it.
4. **Share houses**, which are set up for exactly this situation - furnished, short contracts, no key money, no guarantor. Good for the first six months while you establish a record.
5. Having a Japanese-speaking colleague make the initial call, which changes the response rate more than it should.

**Other housing notes:** apartments come unfurnished and usually without appliances, light fittings or curtains; sizes are given in 帖 or 畳 (tatami-mat units) and layouts in codes like 1K, 1DK, 1LDK (rooms plus kitchen / dining / living); contracts are typically two years; and the earthquake standard (新耐震基準, post-1981) is a listed selling point for good reason ([Chapter 2](#earthquakes-volcanoes-and-tsunami---a-permanent-condition)). Buying is possible for foreign residents without permanent residence, though mortgage access is much easier with PR or a Japanese spouse.

### Money, phones and healthcare

**Banking.** Opening an account is easier at some institutions than others. **Japan Post Bank** and several online banks (Sony Bank, SBI Shinsei, Rakuten) are generally the most accessible to new foreign residents; some traditional banks impose a six-month residency requirement or want a hanko. You will need a domestic account before your employer can pay you. International transfers through Japanese banks are slow and expensive, and services like Wise are the standard workaround. Note that your **credit history does not transfer** - expect credit cards to be difficult for the first year, and expect some services to be awkward without one.

**Phone.** A contract requires your residence card and usually a Japanese bank account or credit card. The major carriers (docomo, au, SoftBank) are expensive; **MVNOs** (IIJmio, mineo, and the carriers' own budget brands) are cheaper and easier to sign up for. There is often a gap on arrival between landing and having a Japanese number, which is worth planning for because you need a number for almost everything else.

**Healthcare.** Enrolment in public health insurance is **mandatory**, not optional:

- **健康保険 (Employees' Health Insurance)** through your employer, with premiums shared between you and the company and deducted at source.
- **国民健康保険 (National Health Insurance)** through your municipality if you are not covered by an employer. You enrol at the ward office, and premiums are income-based.

Either way, the insured party pays a share of costs at the point of care - commonly 30 percent for working-age adults - and there is a monthly ceiling on out-of-pocket costs under the **高額療養費** high-cost medical care system. The practical experience: appointments are cheap, walk-in access to specialists is easy, prescriptions are cheap, dental is cheap, and the quality of routine care is high. The weaker areas for a foreign resident are **English-speaking providers** outside major cities and **mental healthcare**, where both availability and cultural approach differ considerably from what many arrivals expect. Find out where your nearest 内科 (general medicine) clinic is before you need it.

**Pension.** Enrolment is also mandatory - **厚生年金 (Employees' Pension)** via an employer, or **国民年金 (National Pension)** otherwise. Two things to know. First, **pay it**, because payment records are checked for visa renewals and permanent residence. Second, if you leave Japan before qualifying for a pension there is a **脱退一時金 (lump-sum withdrawal payment)** with limits on how many years it covers, and separately Japan has **social security agreements** with a number of countries that allow contribution periods to be combined or avoid double payment. Which applies to you depends on your nationality - check the Japan Pension Service and your home country's equivalent.

**Tax.** The structure:

- **National income tax**, withheld at source, with an employer-run **年末調整 (year-end adjustment)** that handles most people's reconciliation. **確定申告** (self-assessment filing) runs roughly February to March and is needed if you have other income, deductions to claim, or multiple employers.
- **住民税 (local inhabitant tax)** is the one that ambushes people. It is assessed on the **previous** year's income and billed in arrears. So your first year is cheap and your second year is not, and if you leave Japan you may owe a full year's inhabitant tax on income you earned before departing. Budget for it.
- **消費税 (consumption tax)** at 10 percent, with a reduced 8 percent rate for most food and non-alcoholic drink and for takeaway. Displayed prices are generally tax-inclusive.
- **Residency and worldwide income.** Japan's rules distinguish non-permanent residents from permanent residents for tax purposes, with the scope of taxable foreign income expanding after a threshold period of residence. This is genuinely complicated, matters a great deal if you have assets or income abroad, and is a question for an accountant rather than a guide.

**Driving.** An International Driving Permit is valid for a limited period (generally one year), some countries' licences require an official translation instead, and after that you need to convert your licence (**外免切替**) - which for some countries requires passing a practical test. Check the rules for your specific licence early if driving matters to you.

### The foreigner experience, honestly

**The vocabulary.** **外国人 (gaikokujin)** is the neutral term. **外人 (gaijin)** is the clipped form, literally "outside person", and whether it is offensive is genuinely contested - some foreign residents find it othering and dislike it, others use it about themselves without concern, and most Japanese speakers using it intend nothing by it. Official and polite usage prefers gaikokujin. **ハーフ (hafu)** refers to mixed-heritage people and carries its own complicated set of experiences, sometimes glamorised and sometimes exclusionary.

**Permanent foreignness is the core thing to understand.** Japan is an unusually easy country to live in and an unusually hard country to be *from* if you were not born there. The concrete texture of this:

- **Compliments that do not stop.** 日本語上手ですね ("your Japanese is good") and お箸使えるんですね ("you can use chopsticks!") continue to be offered after five years and after twenty. They are kindly meant and they also encode an expectation that you would not be able to.
- **Conversations switching to English**, sometimes mid-sentence, sometimes after you have spoken Japanese, which is helpfulness that functions as exclusion.
- **Being read as a tourist** indefinitely - asked how long you are visiting, complimented on your bravery, handed the English menu.
- **"When are you going home?"** asked as small talk, of someone who lives there.
- **Being asked to represent your country** and to have opinions on behalf of it.
- **Occasional refusals** - a landlord, a bar, a gym, rarely a shop. Uncommon, real, and with essentially no legal remedy given the absence of a general anti-discrimination law. A 2016 statute addresses hate speech against foreign residents but is largely declaratory and carries no penalties.
- **The experience varies enormously by appearance.** A visibly non-East-Asian foreigner is permanently marked as foreign and gets the full set of the above. An East-Asian foreigner - Chinese, Korean, Taiwanese - is often read as Japanese until they speak, which removes the othering and substitutes a different and sometimes harsher set of problems, including being held to Japanese social standards without having been raised in them.

**Fluency changes this a great deal, and not completely.** This is the honest formulation. Real Japanese ability moves you from guest to participant: you can have actual friendships with people who do not speak English, you can handle your own bureaucracy, you can work without a ceiling, you can understand what is going on around you, and the amount of accidental condescension drops sharply. What it does not do is make you read as Japanese. Some people report that high fluency actually *increases* the astonishment reaction. The realistic target is not being mistaken for Japanese; it is being treated as a competent adult resident, which fluency does achieve.

**The community tradeoff, stated as a tradeoff.** The foreign community is genuinely valuable - practical knowledge, shortcuts, emotional support from people who have had the same week you have had, and a place where you are not the only one. It is also the most reliable way to plateau at conversational Japanese forever and to live in Japan for a decade without participating in it. The pure-integration path, conversely, is slow and lonely and can leave you without anyone who understands what is hard. **The answer is to be deliberate rather than to pick a side**: use the foreign community for logistics and sanity, and construct Japanese-language social contexts on purpose, because they will not accrete by themselves ([Phase 7](#phase-7---living-it)). Hobby circles, sports, 習い事, the local 町内会, volunteering - anything with a recurring schedule and a shared activity, because shared activity carries a conversation that small talk cannot.

**Non-catastrophising bottom line.** Japan is safe, clean, extremely well-served by public transport, cheap for healthcare and food, culturally rich, and has some of the best everyday quality of life anywhere. It also has more bureaucratic friction than most Western European countries, a real ceiling on social belonging, and a working culture that ranges from excellent to genuinely harmful depending on the employer. Many foreigners build long, happy lives there. Many leave after two to five years. The variables that seem to decide it are, in rough order: **language ability, career track, and whether you built a Japanese-language social life.** Notice that all three are things you can act on, and that two of them are what this repo is for.

### Internal difference, and how Japan handles it

The "homogeneous Japan" idea is a Meiji-era and postwar construction, and it is inaccurate. Knowing the internal histories matters for two reasons: they are the context for how Japan handles difference generally, and they come up. Stated neutrally, because a learner deserves to know these exist and does not need anyone's verdict.

**The Ainu** - the indigenous people of Hokkaido and historically northern Honshu, Sakhalin and the Kurils, with an unrelated language and a distinct culture. Dispossessed and assimilated from the Meiji period; recognised as an indigenous people in statute in 2019; language critically endangered. Whether the historical treatment constitutes colonisation, and what land, resource and self-determination claims follow, are politically contested. See [Chapter 3](#not-dialects---ryukyuan-and-ainu).

**Ryukyuans / Okinawans** - the Ryukyu Kingdom's annexation in the 1870s, Meiji-era assimilation including language suppression, the catastrophic civilian death toll in the 1945 Battle of Okinawa, US administration until reversion in 1972, and the continuing concentration of US military bases on a small prefecture. Okinawan identity is strong and distinct; the base issue is bitter and unresolved, and local referendum results and central government policy have diverged.

**在日コリアン (Zainichi Koreans)** - descendants, largely, of people who came or were brought to Japan during the colonial period, plus later arrivals. Key points: many hold **Special Permanent Resident** status rather than Japanese nationality, sometimes into the third or fourth generation; the community is split by affiliation to North or South Korea, with separate school systems; many use Japanese names (通名) in daily life, which is itself a subject of debate; local voting rights for long-term foreign residents have been repeatedly proposed and not adopted; and the community has been a target of organised hate speech, which prompted the 2016 legislation. Naturalisation rates are significant and the choice is emotionally loaded in both directions.

**部落民 (burakumin)** - descendants of Edo-period outcast groups associated with occupations considered ritually polluting. The legal status was abolished in the Meiji period, and there is no visible marker of descent. Discrimination persisted, historically enforced by investigating family registry (戸籍) records to identify ancestral addresses - a practice used in marriage and employment screening, and one reason for restrictions on registry access. Government programmes addressed the historically deprived areas. The dominant contemporary treatment of the subject in Japan is **silence**: many younger Japanese people know little about it, and discussing it openly is unusual. Whether that silence constitutes resolution or avoidance is disputed.

**Nikkei returnees** - a 1990 immigration law change allowed people of Japanese descent, principally from Brazil and Peru, to live and work in Japan. Large communities formed around manufacturing centres. The outcome complicates the ethnicity-equals-belonging assumption: ethnically Japanese, culturally Brazilian, frequently not Japanese-speaking, and often treated as foreign.

**The current foreign population** is increasingly Chinese, Vietnamese, Korean, Filipino, Nepali and Indonesian, and growing. Avoid quoting figures, which change fast and are usually quoted out of date.

**The policy direction.** Demographic pressure - a shrinking working-age population and an aging society - is forcing incremental opening: the Specified Skilled Worker categories, expanded sectors, easier highly-skilled routes, and record numbers of foreign workers. What is notable is that this has happened **without a political consensus to describe it as immigration policy**. The official framing has generally been about temporary labour and specific shortages rather than settlement. Whether Japan will shift to an explicit immigration framework, and what integration policy would accompany it, is one of the more consequential open questions about the country's next twenty years - and directly relevant if you are planning to be there for them.

## Common pitfalls

### 1. Treating anime as ethnography

**The mistake:** building a model of Japanese society, and especially of Japanese speech, out of fiction.

**Why it feels right:** it is your longest, richest and most emotionally invested exposure. The emotional truth in good anime is real. And crucially, **the set dressing is accurate** - the trains, the konbini, the apartments, the school buildings, the shrines and the food are meticulously researched, so you correctly learn to trust the medium, and then over-extend that trust to the social content, which is stylised.

**The fix:** separate the layers explicitly. Physical world: mostly reliable. Social behaviour and speech register: mostly unreliable. Before imitating any behaviour or phrase, check it against a non-fiction source. And add non-fiction input until it is a real fraction of your diet. Full treatment in [Chapter 9](#anime-is-not-a-documentary).

### 2. Collecting Japan facts instead of learning Japanese

**The mistake:** spending your study time reading about Japan in English.

**Why it feels right:** it is genuinely interesting, genuinely useful, adjacent to your actual goal, and produces a warm feeling of progress. The Tofugu mirror in this repo has over 400 culture articles and they are all good, which makes this the best-camouflaged procrastination available.

**The fix:** cap it. A fixed weekly budget, taken *after* your reviews and immersion, not before. Then audit: if culture-in-English hours are within a factor of two of your Japanese-input hours, you have a problem. This is the single reason the [recommended path](#recommended-path-opinionated) puts a number on the budget.

### 3. Believing the nihonjinron model

**The mistake:** treating uchi/soto, honne/tatemae, wa and amae as a predictive theory of Japanese people.

**Why it feels right:** the frameworks are elegant, they explain real things, and they do make correct predictions often enough to feel validated. For an engineer, a clean model that compresses a lot of observation is intrinsically attractive - which is exactly the vulnerability.

**The fix:** use them to generate hypotheses about *why something happened*, never to predict *what a person will do*. Expect variation by individual, generation, region and industry to dwarf the national average. And apply the test: if you catch yourself thinking a Japanese person is being insufficiently Japanese, your model is broken. See [the critique](#the-honest-critique---nihonjinron-and-over-generalisation).

### 4. Politeness overcorrection

**The mistake:** defaulting to maximum keigo everywhere, on the theory that more polite is always safer.

**Why it feels right:** you have read that politeness matters enormously, so the conservative strategy looks obviously correct.

**The fix:** it is not correct. Keigo signals **distance**, and excessive formality with a friend or a close colleague is as marked as casual speech to a client - it says "I do not consider us close", which is a thing you may not mean to say. The workable default is **ですます with anyone new, then follow the other person's lead** and relax when they do. [Speaking](<../Speaking/readme.md>) owns live register management; [Grammar](<../Grammar/readme.md>) owns the forms.

### 5. Exoticising

**The mistake:** reaching for a cultural-essence explanation when a mundane one is available.

**Why it feels right:** the differences are genuinely interesting, the "mysterious Japan" framing is everywhere and is fun, and the exotic explanation is more satisfying than the boring one.

**The fix:** assume the boring reason first. Long working hours are labour law and promotion structure, not a spiritual disposition. Quiet trains are a norm about not inconveniencing others, not Zen. Small apartments are a land constraint. Japan is a large, wealthy, industrial democracy full of people who want roughly what everyone wants, operating under a specific set of institutions and incentives. Start there.

### 6. Flattening

**The mistake:** the equal and opposite error - deciding that cultural difference is overblown and that people are just people.

**Why it feels right:** pitfalls 3 and 5 are real errors, and having identified them it feels sophisticated to reject the whole category. It is also flattering to be above stereotypes.

**The fix:** notice that the differences that will actually affect you are **concrete and behavioural**, not mystical: who bows first, whether you can decline directly, where a decision actually gets made, whether your boss keeps his honorific when you speak to a client. Those are real, specific, learnable, and you will get them wrong. "People are people" is true at the level of human motivation and useless at the level of conduct.

### 7. Preparing for tourist Japan when you plan to live there

**The mistake:** studying temples, festivals, food and travel while skipping visas, tax, housing and work culture.

**Why it feels right:** travel content is abundant, beautiful and enjoyable. Immigration and inhabitant tax are dry, and the details go stale so studying them early feels wasteful.

**The fix:** run them as two separate curricula with different schedules. The cultural layer is continuous and enjoyable; the practical layer is a **project with a deadline**, executed twelve to eighteen months out ([Phase 6](#phase-6---the-practical-relocation-layer)). Do not let the pleasant one crowd out the consequential one.

### 8. Ignoring contested history

**The mistake:** skipping the wartime memory material because it is uncomfortable and you are here for the language.

**Why it feels right:** it is uncomfortable, it is not your fight, and "I have no opinion on this" is a genuinely defensible personal stance.

**The fix:** you do not need opinions; you need awareness. Not knowing why a Yasukuni visit is news, what 在日 means, or why a Korean colleague might react to a comment about textbooks means you will eventually say something harmful by accident and not understand what happened. Read [the contested memory table](#contested-memory---what-a-learner-should-know-exists) once. That is sufficient.

### 9. Assuming "Japanese work culture" is one thing

**The mistake:** planning your career around the monolithic picture in the general literature.

**Why it feels right:** the entire genre of writing on this subject describes a single model - the traditional large firm - and presents it as national.

**The fix:** the [three-employer distinction](#the-three-employers---the-distinction-that-changes-everything) changes almost every prediction: working language, hours, hiring process, pay, hierarchy, Japanese requirement. Research **specific companies**, not "Japan". Read employee reviews, check disclosed overtime figures, and talk to people who work there now.

### 10. Learning register from your fandom

**The mistake:** acquiring 推し, 神回, てぇてぇ, 草 and the rest, and letting them leak out of fan contexts.

**Why it feels right:** it is fun, it is current, it gets you into online fan spaces, and it is genuinely useful Japanese - in those spaces.

**The fix:** fine in fan spaces, actively harmful in an interview or a first meeting. **Tag register when you mine**, as a field on the card - casual / polite / business / fandom / fiction-only. [Vocabulary](<../Vocabulary/readme.md>) and [AJATT](<../AJATT/readme.md>) own the card-design mechanics; the discipline is yours.

## Measuring progress

Culture is the easiest topic in this repo to fool yourself about, because the feeling of understanding arrives long before the understanding. These are deliberately concrete tests. If you cannot pass one, you have not learned that part yet.

| # | Test | Standard |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | **Map test** | Name and place at least thirty of the 47 prefectures on a blank map, and all eight regions. Read all 47 names correctly |
| 2 | **Timeline test** | Sketch the period sequence with one sentence each. Then explain the Meiji Restoration and the 1945 defeat and Occupation to another person, in about five minutes each, without notes, including why each is still politically live |
| 3 | **Register test** | Watch an unfamiliar scene and work out who is senior to whom **from the language alone**, before the plot tells you. Honorific choice, sentence endings, address forms |
| 4 | **Uchi/soto test** | Given a three-party scenario (you, your boss, an outside client), correctly choose humble versus honorific forms and correctly handle your boss's title in both directions. Explain **why**, not just what |
| 5 | **Indirectness test** | Find five refusals in media or real conversation that contain no negative form at all, and identify the mechanism in each |
| 6 | **Etiquette dry run** | Talk yourself through four sequences from memory with the Japanese phrases included: a konbini transaction, ordering and paying at a restaurant, a shrine visit, an onsen visit. No looking anything up |
| 7 | **Practical test** | State which residence status you would apply under and what it requires; list what your employer must provide; produce a written first-month bureaucratic checklist |
| 8 | **Bias test** | Name three specific, concrete ways a show you personally like misrepresents Japan. "It's exaggerated" does not count - name the behaviour and what the real version is |
| 9 | **Social test** (post-arrival) | Two recurring social contexts where the language is Japanese and nobody is paid to talk to you. Plus: you have had a genuine disagreement with a Japanese friend and remained friends |
| 10 | **The real one** | When a conversation or a scene goes over your head, can you tell **whether you missed a word or missed a social cue**? |

Test 10 is the one that matters. Early on, every failure feels the same - a wall of incomprehension. The mark that this guide has actually landed is that you can classify your own failures: "I did not know that word", versus "I knew every word and missed what was happening". The second category is what this chapter exists to shrink, and being able to *see* the category at all is the real milestone. If you can diagnose it, you can fix it.

What deliberately is **not** a measure of progress: how many culture articles you have read, how many Japanese concepts you can name in English, or how confidently you can explain Japan to someone else. That last one is a warning sign rather than an achievement.
