# Sources and provenance

Everything in this folder, where it came from, and what you are allowed to do with it.
All items retrieved **2026-09-17**.

**Read this before you cite anything.** A large part of this folder is *pre-1930 primary
source material*. It is here because it is foundational, freely distributable, and
genuinely worth reading - not because it is accurate about Japan today. The
[caveats](#caveats---read-this-part) section below is not boilerplate.

### Japanese classics (Aozora Bunko)

Nine files **in Japanese** from **Aozora Bunko** (aozora.gr.jp), the Japanese equivalent of
Project Gutenberg. Every item was picked out of Aozora's own master index
(`list_person_all_extended_utf8.zip`) and every one carries 作品著作権フラグ = なし **and**
人物著作権フラグ = なし, which is Aozora certifying that both the work and its translator or
editor are out of copyright. Taken as the **XHTML** edition so that **furigana survives as
`<ruby>` tags** - 1,341 of them in *Taketori Monogatari* alone - then **converted from
Shift-JIS to UTF-8** with `iconv`, with the `charset=` meta tag and the XML `encoding=`
declaration rewritten to match. Nothing else was changed. Open them in a browser and the
ruby renders as furigana above the kanji.

**These are cultural artefacts first and reading material second, and the difficulty note in
each row is the important part.** Classical Japanese (古文) is not a hard dialect of modern
Japanese; it is a separate language variety with its own verb morphology, its own
auxiliaries, and - in the 旧仮名 editions - kana spellings that do not match modern
pronunciation. A learner at N2 cannot read *Hojoki* in the original, and should not feel bad
about it. Where Aozora carries a 現代語訳 (modern-Japanese translation) it was taken as well,
and the rows say which is which.

### Japanese folklore studies (Aozora Bunko)

Seven files **in Japanese**, same provenance, same Shift-JIS to UTF-8 conversion, same
`<ruby>` furigana. This section exists because **民俗学 (Japanese folklore studies) is the
discipline that actually documented the material Chapters 7, 8 and 9 talk about**, and its
founders died long enough ago to be public domain.

Yanagita Kunio (1875-1962) founded the field. Orikuchi Shinobu (1887-1953) was its other
major figure, working on ritual and the origins of Japanese religion. Their prose is
**genuinely difficult**: Meiji and early-Showa literary register, and *Tono Monogatari* in
particular is written in condensed 文語体 (聞きたり, せしなり, あらざれども) rather than modern
colloquial Japanese. Treat the whole section as N1 and above, and expect the essays to be
easier going than *Tono Monogatari* itself.

### Meiji thought and society (Aozora Bunko)

Two files **in Japanese**, same provenance and conversion. Fukuzawa Yukichi (1835-1901) gives
Chapter 1's Meiji section a perspective **written by an actual Meiji Japanese person**, to set
against Nitobe, who wrote in English for Americans: Fukuzawa is the most widely read Japanese
author of the period, the founder of Keio, and the man on the 10,000-yen note.

His prose is **late-nineteenth-century written Japanese** - not 古文, but not modern
colloquial either. Expect 〜なり and 〜たり alongside forms you recognise. Roughly N1, and
easier going than *Tono Monogatari*.

### Government white papers (Japanese)

Three Japanese-language 白書 and 年鑑 (government white papers and yearbooks). They are here for
two reasons at once: they are the **authoritative source** on these subjects, and they are
**real reading practice on real topics** - dense, formal, chart-heavy administrative Japanese of
exactly the kind you would have to read in a Japanese office.

The karoshi and gender-equality papers are the **概要版 (summary edition)**; the full editions
are enormous - the karoshi white paper runs to about 85 MB across three PDFs - and the summaries
carry the figures and the argument. The religious yearbook is the **full** annual publication
rather than a summary, but it is mostly statistical tables and stays under 3.5 MB regardless.
Japanese government white papers are published free for reuse under terms
compatible with CC BY (confirmed for 文化庁 via the MEXT website terms of use it defers to);
MHLW, Cabinet Office and 文化庁 all permit reproduction with attribution.

### Dialect atlas (NINJAL)

Three map-and-legend pairs from **『日本言語地図』 (the Linguistic Atlas of Japan)**, surveyed
and published 1966-74 by the **National Institute for Japanese Language and Linguistics
(国立国語研究所, NINJAL)** and released digitally as PDF. Chapter 3 is a full chapter on

**Licence, precisely:** NINJAL releases maps 101-300 of the atlas under **CC BY 4.0** and

reusable with attribution to NINJAL.

**Two honest caveats.** First, these PDFs are **raster scans of the printed atlas** - each is
a single high-resolution JPEG, roughly 4,100 x 2,900 pixels, wrapped in a PDF. They are
perfectly legible zoomed in, but there is **no text layer**: you cannot search them, copy
from them, or point a dictionary extension at them. Second, the survey recorded the speech of
people **born in the 1880s and 1890s**. That is the point - it is a snapshot of Japan before
broadcast standardisation - but it is not a map of how anyone speaks in 2026. The `_e` files
are the legends, which list every recorded form and its map symbol; you need the legend to
read the map.

## Caveats - read this part

1. **Most of this folder is not current scholarship.** Eight of the ten items under
   *Primary sources and classics* were published between 1871 and 1925. They are primary
   sources: evidence about their own period, and about how Japan was presented to the West.
   They are not descriptions of Japan in 2026.

2. **Bushido is the sharpest trap.** Nitobe's *Bushido* (1900) is a **foundational
   nihonjinron text** - the genre arguing that Japan is uniquely and essentially different.
   It is enormously influential and almost entirely unreliable as history. Nitobe was a
   Quaker-influenced Christian agriculturalist writing in English for Americans, working
   from a class that had been abolished before he was an adult. If you ever find yourself
   explaining "the samurai code" to someone using anything from this book, stop.

3. **Hearn shaped the Western imagination of Japan, including its errors.** *Glimpses* and
   *An Attempt at Interpretation* are the origin of a great deal of still-circulating
   Western folklore about Japanese spirituality and "the Japanese mind". Read them as
   literature and as period evidence. *Kwaidan* is on safer ground because it is openly a
   retelling of folk tales.

4. **The Victorian material on the Ainu is racist and is deliberately excluded.** Project
   Gutenberg has period texts on the Ainu (for example *Alone with the Hairy Ainu*, PG
   37873) whose titles indicate their contents. They were **not** downloaded. The four
   APJJF articles are here specifically to occupy that ground properly.

5. **Two licence uncertainties, flagged rather than hidden:** the `code4fukui` prefecture
   data (published as open data, no explicit licence declared) and the TokyoDev articles
   (free to read, all rights reserved). Both are described honestly in their tables above.

6. **Statistics go stale.** The Statistical Handbook is the 2025 edition and the holidays
   CSV runs to 2026. Check [stat.go.jp](https://www.stat.go.jp/english/) for newer editions
   rather than quoting these in five years. For anything about visas or residency, the
   [Immigration Services Agency](https://www.isa.go.jp/en/) overrides every other file here.

7. **Two of the Japanese files are classical Japanese, and a learner cannot read them.**
   *Hojoki* and *Tosa Nikki* are 古文 in 旧仮名 orthography. 古文 is a separate language
   variety - different verb morphology, different auxiliaries, kana spellings that do not
   match modern pronunciation - not simply harder Japanese, and no amount of JLPT level
   unlocks it. Both are on this shelf as **cultural artefacts**; where Aozora carries a
   現代語訳 it is here alongside the original. A third file, *Taketori Monogatari*, is easy
   to mistake for either: it is a **1920s retelling** in modern-ish grammar wearing
   pre-reform kana, which is neither 古文 nor current Japanese. The *Japanese classics*
   table above labels every row with its actual register - read that column before you open
   anything. **This is not a reading ladder.** [Reading](<../Reading/readme.md>) owns the
   ladder, and its Aozora selection is deliberately filtered to 新字新仮名, which this
   section is not.

8. **The Yanagita and Fukuzawa material is hard for a different reason.** It is not
   classical, but it is Meiji and early-Showa literary register - 文語体 in the case of
   *Tono Monogatari* (聞きたり, せしなり), and 〜なり / 〜たり forms throughout Fukuzawa.
   Treat the whole Japanese-language side of this folder as N1 and above, with
   『古事記物語』 and 『こども風土記』 the two realistic entry points.

9. **The NINJAL dialect maps are image scans, and they are historical.** Each is a single
   high-resolution JPEG in a PDF wrapper: **no text layer**, so not searchable, not
   copyable, and no use to a dictionary extension. They also record speakers born in the
   1880s and 1890s. That is exactly what makes them valuable - a picture of Japan before
   broadcast standardisation - and exactly why they are not a map of how anyone speaks now.

10. **The folder total at the top of this file may be stale** as more material is added.
    Recount with `du -sh` rather than trusting it for an exact figure.

## How the derived files were built

Two items in this folder were **assembled or derived** rather than downloaded whole. Both
are reproducible.

**Kojiki and Nihongi** were built from English Wikisource by enumerating the subpages of
`Kojiki (Chamberlain, 1882)/` (185 sections) and `Nihongi/` (38 books) via the MediaWiki
`action=query&generator=allpages` API, fetching each page with `action=parse`, stripping
HTML to plain text, and concatenating in reading order with a rule between sections.
Requests were throttled to roughly one per 1.3 seconds with a descriptive User-Agent, per
Wikimedia API etiquette. Each file carries its own provenance header.

**The 47-prefecture CSV** was produced by this SPARQL query against the Wikidata Query
Service, then de-duplicated to one row per ISO code (Aichi and Fukuoka return multiple rows
because they carry multiple population or area statements):

```sparql
SELECT ?iso ?prefEn ?prefJa ?prefKana ?capEn ?capJa ?population ?areaKm2 WHERE {
  ?p wdt:P300 ?iso . FILTER(STRSTARTS(?iso,"JP-"))
  ?p wdt:P31 wd:Q50337 .
  ?p rdfs:label ?prefEn . FILTER(LANG(?prefEn)="en")
  OPTIONAL { ?p rdfs:label ?prefJa . FILTER(LANG(?prefJa)="ja") }
  OPTIONAL { ?p wdt:P1814 ?prefKana }
  OPTIONAL { ?p wdt:P36 ?cap .
    ?cap rdfs:label ?capEn . FILTER(LANG(?capEn)="en")
    OPTIONAL { ?cap rdfs:label ?capJa . FILTER(LANG(?capJa)="ja") } }
  OPTIONAL { ?p wdt:P1082 ?population }
  OPTIONAL { ?p wdt:P2046 ?areaKm2 }
} ORDER BY ?iso
```

A `region` column (Kanto, Kansai and so on) was attempted and **deliberately dropped**:
Wikidata does not model the eight regions consistently enough to extract reliably, and a
half-populated column is worse than none. The regions are already covered by the
[Japanese locations Anki deck](<../Vocabulary/Japanese locations/Japanese locations.apkg>).

Note that `prefecture_kana` is empty for Hokkaido, because Wikidata carries no kana
statement for it. It is ほっかいどう.

---

**The Comiket chronology CSV** was derived from the English Wikipedia article *Comiket* by
fetching the article's wikitext through the MediaWiki `action=parse` API, isolating the
"List of Comic Markets" table, resolving its `rowspan` cells for year and venue, expanding
`{{formatnum:}}` and `{{Nihongo}}` templates, stripping references and footnote markers, and
writing one row per edition. 110 editions from C1 (December 1975) to the announced 2027 dates.
Wikipedia text is CC BY-SA 4.0, and Wikipedia in turn cites the Comic Market Preparatory
Committee's own published chronology. A provenance header is written into the top of the CSV as
comment rows, including the warning that attendance is the committee's day-sum figure rather than
a unique-visitor count. It is a tertiary source and is labelled as one.
