# Speaking

<img src="../../Assets/Listening-Speaking-logo.png" alt="Listening and Speaking" height="300"/>

> **TL;DR**
> - **What this is:** the production side of Japanese - articulation, mora timing, pitch accent, prosody, real-time retrieval, and picking the right register for the person in front of you. This guide owns **pronunciation, pitch accent, shadowing, output practice and register in speech** for the whole repo.
> - **Why it matters:** speaking is the skill most learners have the least of, and the only one that decays measurably without use. It is also the immersion community's weakest area - "input is everything" reliably produces people who can read a novel and then freeze when a barista asks a follow-up question.
> - **When to start:** **pronunciation work starts on day 1.** It is cheap, it is solo, and it is the one part of Japanese where doing nothing for two years actively costs you, because bad articulation hardens. **Free conversation** starts when you have a few hundred words and basic grammar - months, not years.
> - **The recommended path:** phonetics from day 1, shadowing from week 2, self-talk from month 3, a paid tutor from month 6-9, register work from year 1. Explicitly earlier than AJATT orthodoxy says.
> - **The one non-negotiable technique:** record yourself and A/B against a native. You cannot hear your own Japanese while you are producing it. Everything else in this guide is optional; this is not.

## What this is and why it matters

Speaking is not one skill. It is at least four, and they fail independently:

1. **Articulation** - can your mouth make the sounds? Japanese has a small, clean phoneme inventory, which makes this the *easy* part, and which is exactly why learners skip it and then keep a heavy accent for a decade.
2. **Prosody and timing** - mora-equal rhythm and pitch accent. This is what makes Japanese *sound* Japanese, and it is where nearly all of the "your Japanese is technically correct but sounds foreign" judgement lives.
3. **Real-time retrieval** - producing a grammatical sentence in under a second, without a dictionary, while listening to the other person. Input builds your *recognition* store. It does not, by itself, build the retrieval path from concept to mouth.
4. **Register** - the plain/polite/keigo dimension. Getting this wrong is not an accent problem, it is a social problem, and it is the one error a Japanese colleague will actually remember.

Two honest facts about this skill set that the rest of the internet underplays:

**Speaking decays.** Reading and listening are recognition tasks; they degrade slowly. Production is a motor and retrieval skill and it degrades fast. Three months without speaking and you will be visibly worse, in a way that three months without reading will not do to you. That means speaking cannot be a "phase" you complete - it has to become a habit you maintain.

**The immersion community is weakest here, and knows it.** The input-first tradition ([AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md), MIA, Refold) is extremely good at getting you to comprehension and extremely bad at getting you to conversation, because it treats output as something that emerges automatically once comprehension is high enough. It partly does - a 3,000-hour immersion learner who has never spoken will speak better on day one than a 300-hour textbook learner. But "better than a beginner" is not "able to hold a job interview", and the gap between the two is filled with practice you have to deliberately schedule. TheMoeWay's author says this out loud: delaying output until you are "really good" is "a product of a toxic perfectionist mindset". He is right.

**What this guide owns:** phonetics and articulation, vowel devoicing, mora timing for production, pitch accent end to end, shadowing and chorusing, self-talk, conversation practice and tutoring, and the practical use of register in live speech.

**What it does not own:** what to listen to and the comprehensible-input ladder live in [Listening](../Listening/readme.md); the kana charts and the mora system as *writing* live in [Kana](../Kana/readme.md); romanization systems live in [Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md); the keigo grammatical machinery lives in [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md); the sociology of hierarchy and honorifics lives in [Culture](../Culture/readme.md); Anki, Yomitan and dictionary setup live in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>).

## Where it fits in the journey

The single most useful thing to understand here: **speaking splits into two halves with completely different prerequisites.** Conflating them is why people either start too late or start badly.

| Half | Prerequisites | When |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Phonetics and shadowing** (articulation, mora timing, pitch awareness, imitation) | Kana, read reliably. That is it. | **Day 1 to week 2.** No vocabulary needed - you can shadow audio you do not understand. |
| **Free conversation** (spontaneous, unscripted, with a human) | ~500-1,000 words, basic grammar through the て-form and past tense, enough listening to parse a slow reply | **Month 4-9** for most people. Earlier if you are moving to Japan. |

**Prerequisites in detail:**

| Prerequisite | Why | Owner |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Kana read fluently | Every transcript, pitch dictionary and shadowing script assumes it. | [Kana](../Kana/readme.md) |
| Romaji dropped | Romaji encodes English phonology, not Japanese. Reading `fu`, `tsu`, `ra` as English letters *is* the accent. | [Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md) |
| A few hundred words | Below this, a conversation is a phrasebook recital. Shadowing still works; conversation does not. | [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) |
| Basic sentence grammar | You need です・ます, past tense, negatives and the て-form to say anything with two clauses in it. | [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md) |
| Some listening hours | You cannot have a conversation you cannot hear. Perception precedes production - see below. | [Listening](../Listening/readme.md) |

**Perception precedes production, and this is the one place where Listening and Speaking genuinely interlock.** You cannot reliably produce a distinction you cannot hear. If long and short vowels sound the same to you, your おばさん and おばあさん will be the same word. So the first pronunciation exercise is always a *listening* exercise: can you tell these two apart in a blind test? Only then does drilling your mouth make sense. [Listening](../Listening/readme.md) owns what to listen to; this guide owns what to listen *for*.

**What speaking unlocks:**

- Conversation, which is the highest-bandwidth immersion there is - interactive, personalised, and it forces output.
- The ability to live in Japan as a participant rather than a tourist. Reading gets you through a train station. Speaking gets you a lunch invitation.
- A brutal and instant feedback signal on your grammar. You discover that you never actually learned the causative-passive the first time you need it in a sentence about your landlord.
- Being taken seriously at work. The JLPT will never test speaking ([JLPT](../JLPT/readme.md) is reading and listening only), which is precisely why an employer will test it in the interview.

## The schools of thought

The central dispute in this topic is not *how* to practise speaking. It is **when to start**. Here are the five real positions, stated as strongly as their advocates state them.

### 1. Silent period / input-first (Krashen, AJATT, MIA, Refold)

Delay output for months or years. Build a very large passive base first, then let production emerge. The theoretical spine is Stephen Krashen's Input Hypothesis: language is acquired by understanding messages slightly beyond your level, and consciously "learned" knowledge does not readily convert into fluent use. The practical argument is fossilization: if you force output before you have internalised the patterns, you will invent your own broken Japanese, drill it through thousands of repetitions, and then have to unlearn it.

- **Pros:** when output does start, it is noticeably more accurate and more natural, because you are recombining patterns you have actually heard rather than translating from English. No bad habits to unlearn. No effort wasted on constructions you do not understand yet. Your accent is better, because you spent the early months listening rather than reading aloud.
- **Cons:** speaking is a motor and retrieval skill, and input does not build it - a learner with 2,000 hours of input and zero output hours speaks haltingly, then improves very fast over a few weeks of practice, which tells you the practice was doing something the input was not. The delay compounds anxiety: the longer you wait, the higher the stakes of the first conversation feel. "I will start speaking when I am ready" is, in practice, indistinguishable from never. And if you are moving to Japan on a date, the clock is not negotiable.
- **Honest caveat:** Krashen did **not** claim output is harmful. He claimed *forced early* output is unhelpful. "Never speak for two years" is a community invention layered on top of him. The Donkuri guide says this directly: the myth that output is bad "has nothing to do with Krashen's input hypothesis".
- **Best for:** learners with no deadline, whose goal is reading or media consumption, and who are genuinely putting in high-volume immersion. Also: people who will be embarrassed into quitting by early failure.

### 2. Output from day one (conversation-first, tutor-led, Pimsleur-adjacent)

Speak from the first week with a patient partner - a tutor, a teacher, a friend. Communication is the skill; train the skill.

- **Pros:** builds the actual target skill directly, which nothing else does. Exposes gaps instantly - you find out in ten seconds that you cannot say "I have been living here for three years", and now you have a reason to learn it. Enormously motivating: a successful conversation on week three is worth more to compliance than a month of flashcards. Builds tolerance for being imperfect in public, which is a real and trainable skill.
- **Cons:** early output is cognitively exhausting and heavily error-ridden. Without correction you can drill in mistakes - and a polite conversation partner will not correct you. A true beginner conversation is mostly memorised formulas with nothing behind them, which can produce a convincing illusion of ability. It is also slow per hour: an hour of tutoring buys far less raw vocabulary than an hour of Anki plus immersion.
- **Best for:** people with an imminent deadline (moving, a job, a trip), extroverts who are energised by interaction, and anyone whose motivation is fragile and needs visible wins.

### 3. Shadowing and chorusing (the pronunciation-first position)

Do not converse yet. Instead, imitate native audio intensively - chorus along with it, then shadow just behind it, then record and compare. Build the mouth before you build the sentences.

- **Pros:** the highest-value technique per hour that exists for accent and prosody. It trains articulation, mora timing and pitch contour simultaneously, which no amount of isolated phoneme drilling does. Entirely solo-practicable, so there is no scheduling excuse. It is also the only output practice that is genuinely safe during a silent period - you are not generating your own Japanese, so you cannot fossilize your own errors. And it works at zero comprehension, so it can start on day 1.
- **Cons:** it is not spontaneous production. Perfect shadowing of a clip does not mean you can say anything new. It is monotonous - the technique demands many repetitions of the *same* short clip, which most learners refuse to do. And there is no feedback loop on meaning or grammar.
- **Best for:** everyone, honestly. This is the position with the least to argue against. Especially: learners in a silent period who want to do *something* productive with their mouth, and anyone who already has intermediate comprehension and a heavy accent.

### 4. Pimsleur / audio-course method

Graded audio lessons that prompt you in English and force you to produce Japanese out loud from memory before giving you the answer. Spaced recall, hands-free, no reading.

- **Pros:** it forces *retrieval*, which is the exact mechanism input-only study skips. Genuinely effective for a small set of travel-ready and courtesy phrases with good pronunciation. Hands-free, so it converts commute and gym time into output practice. Excellent for the first two weeks of an absolute beginner who wants to be able to say something real.
- **Cons:** the vocabulary ceiling is tiny - you finish a full course with a few hundred words. The register is stilted and formal, tuned to a 1970s businessman: polite forms and hotel scenarios, almost no casual speech. It is translation-based, which builds an English-to-Japanese pipeline you later have to dismantle. Commercial courses of this type are expensive relative to what they deliver. The three *Teach Yourself* courses in this folder are all in this family and are all available to you offline, which removes the cost objection entirely.
- **Best for:** commutes, absolute beginners who need immediate speakable phrases, and imminent travel. Not a core method.

### 5. Self-talk / monologuing

Narrate your day to yourself in Japanese. Out loud, in the shower, walking to work, describing what you are cooking.

- **Pros:** free, infinite, and zero social anxiety, which means you will actually do it. It surfaces *your* vocabulary gaps rather than a textbook's - you discover you cannot say "the deployment failed" or "I need to renew my visa", which are the words your life actually requires. It trains the concept-to-mouth path with no listener-pressure. It scales from one sentence on day 10 to a fifteen-minute monologue on day 400.
- **Cons:** no correction whatsoever, so it can entrench errors - this is the real risk, and it is the same risk position 1 warns about. Nobody pushes back, so you naturally avoid every structure you find hard and keep circling the same 200 words. It also does not train *listening under pressure*, which is half of conversation.
- **Best for:** the daily-driver output habit between tutoring sessions, and for anyone whose speaking is bottlenecked on retrieval speed rather than knowledge.

### The positions side by side

| Position | Starts | Trains | Main risk | Cost |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Silent period | Year 1-2 | Nothing directly; protects accuracy | Never starting | Free |
| Output day one | Week 1 | Retrieval, comfort, real-time | Fossilization without correction | Tutor fees |
| Shadowing | Day 1 | Articulation, prosody, pitch | Does not transfer to spontaneity | Free |
| Audio course | Day 1 | Forced recall, set phrases | Tiny vocabulary, stilted register | Usually paid (free in this folder) |
| Self-talk | Month 2-3 | Retrieval speed, personal vocabulary | Entrenching errors, avoidance | Free |

## Recommended path (opinionated)

**Pick a staged synthesis, weighted by what each technique costs you.**

The reasoning is a cost asymmetry, not a compromise:

- **Shadowing and phonetics are nearly free and the damage from skipping them is permanent-ish.** Accent hardens. A learner who spends 15 minutes a day on articulation for the first six months ends up sounding better than a learner who does 3,000 hours of input and never opens their mouth. So do this from day 1 regardless of which camp you nominally belong to. There is no counter-argument; the silent-period camp's own objection (fossilizing your own errors) does not apply, because in shadowing you are not producing your own Japanese.
- **Early free conversation carries a real fossilization cost, and the cost scales with how uncorrected it is.** So delay *unstructured* conversation until you have a base, and when you do start, start with someone paid to correct you rather than a friend who will nod politely.
- **Self-talk sits in the middle:** cheap, high-volume, uncorrected. Start it once you have enough grammar to build a sentence, and deliberately alternate it with corrected practice so the errors get caught.

Concretely:

| Phase | When | What |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Phonetics | Day 1 | Learn the articulation of every mora properly. One week of study, then maintenance. |
| Shadowing | Week 2 onward | 10-15 min/day, forever. The single highest-leverage habit in this guide. |
| Self-talk | Month 3 onward | Narrate your day. Free, unlimited, no anxiety. |
| Tutored conversation | Month 6-9 | Paid tutor, weekly. Earlier if you have a deadline. |
| Register / keigo | Year 1-2 | Recognize-first; produce when a job requires it. |
| Pitch accent, deliberate | Month 6+ (awareness from day 1) | Install the tools on day 1; do focused study once you have a vocabulary to attach it to. |

**Ignore this if:** your goal is reading - novels, manga, visual novels, research papers - and you have no intention of living in or working with Japan. In that case the input-first camp is straightforwardly correct for you: put those 15 minutes a day into more reading, keep a rough awareness that pitch accent exists so your internal reading voice is not actively wrong, and skip the rest of this guide until your goals change. Also ignore the tutoring recommendation if the money is a genuine problem - self-talk plus shadowing plus a language exchange partner gets you most of the way, just slower and with more entrenched errors.

## Pronunciation reference

This is the canonical pronunciation chapter for this repo. It is deliberately technical, because "practise your pronunciation" is useless advice and "the ら-row is an alveolar tap, here is how to make one" is not.

Sources for the phonetics below are the in-repo [IMABI](<../Grammar/imabi.org blog offline copy/offline copy/imabi.org/imabi.org/japanese-phonology/index.html>) phonology lessons and the in-repo Tatsumoto [pitch accent primer](<../General content/Tatsumoto Blog offline copy/tatsumoto.neocities.org/blog/japanese-pitch-accents.html>). Where this guide summarises rather than quotes, it says so.

### The five vowels

Japanese has five vowel phonemes: あ い う え お. Two properties matter enormously and neither is on any beginner chart:

**1. They are monophthongs and they do not drift.** An English vowel usually moves during its own production - "eye" starts at one vowel and glides to another, "no" glides, "day" glides. Japanese forbids this. As IMABI puts it, diphthongization "is obligatory for many vowels in English but it is forbidden for all vowels in Japanese". The Japanese あ is roughly the *onset* of English "eye" and nothing after it. This is the single most common English-accent giveaway: え drifting toward "ay", お drifting toward "ow".

- **Drill:** hold each vowel for three seconds. あーーー. If the sound changes while you hold it, you are gliding. Record it and listen.

**2. う is not English "oo".** In Standard (Tokyo-area) Japanese the high back vowel is **unrounded**; any lip movement is *compression*, not the protruding rounding of English "food". IPA writes it `[ɯᵝ]`. Outside East Japan it is closer to a genuine `[u]`. Also: Japanese い has no lip spreading and no tense/lax distinction, so Japanese い can sound to an English ear somewhere between "beat" and "bit" - it is neither.

- **Drill:** say English "oo" and then, without moving your tongue, relax your lips flat. That is closer to う.

**3. Long vowels are two morae, not one long sound.** This is a timing matter, covered below.

### Vowel devoicing

The high vowels /i/ and /u/ are frequently **devoiced** - the mouth still forms the vowel but the vocal cords do not vibrate - when they occur between or after the voiceless consonants /k/, /s/, /t/, /p/. It is especially common in the morae し, ち, ひ, す, つ, ふ, and especially in low-pitched morae and fast speech. Per IMABI: it does not occur in single-mora words, it is more prevalent in male speech, and it is characteristic of Eastern Japanese dialects including Standard Japanese while being almost non-existent elsewhere in Japan.

In practice:

| Word | Naive reading | What you actually hear |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| です | "de-su" | "des" |
| 好き (すき) | "su-ki" | "ski" |
| ました | "ma-shi-ta" | "mash-ta" |
| 靴 (くつ) | "ku-tsu" | "kts" |
| ～しています | "shi-te-i-ma-su" | "shte-imas" |

**The critical nuance that learners get wrong:** the mora still occupies its beat. Devoicing removes the voicing, not the *time*. If you delete the vowel entirely you compress the rhythm and it stops sounding Japanese. Keep the mouth shape, keep the duration, drop the voice.

This is also why pronouncing です as a fully voiced "DE-SUU" is one of the fastest ways to mark yourself as a learner, and it is free to fix.

### The Japanese /r/ - ら り る れ ろ

This is an **alveolar tap** `[ɾ]`. IMABI is explicit that it is neither the English `[l]` nor the English `[ɻ]`, that it is fully voiced, and that it is produced "by merely tapping the alveolar ridge". It is often described as sounding like an English `[d]` - which is the most useful practical hint available.

**How to produce it:**

1. Say the English word "ladder" or "butter" at speed. In most American English the middle consonant is already a tap - the tongue tip hits the ridge behind your upper teeth once and leaves immediately.
2. Isolate that tap. That is very nearly ら.
3. Two things not to do: do **not** curl the tongue back and hold it (English R), and do **not** press the tongue tip up and hold contact while the sound continues (L). The defining feature is that it is a *single quick contact*, not a sustained posture.

IMABI notes allophonic variation: before `[i]` and `[j]` it is usually a clean tap; before `[o]` it often shifts toward the lateral approximant `[ɺ]`; in vulgar or casual speech it can become a trill `[r]` - which is why an angry anime character's ら sounds different from a newsreader's.

- **Drill words:** ら り る れ ろ / ありがとう / わかる / ください / 頑張る / 練習.

### ふ, ひ, つ, ん and the other traps

**ふ is not English F.** The consonant in ふ is `[ɸ]`, a **bilabial** fricative - IMABI describes it as an allophone of /h/ before the back unrounded vowel. English F is *labiodental*: lower lip against upper teeth. For ふ, the teeth are not involved at all. Bring the lips close together, almost as if softly blowing out a candle, and let air pass between them.

**ひ is not English H either.** Before `[i]`, /h/ becomes the palatal `[ç]` in Standard Japanese - a more forward, more friction-y sound than English "h". This is why ひ and し are confusable to learners and why 人 (ひと) is hard to say cleanly.

**つ is an affricate `[ts]`.** English has this cluster only word-medially and word-finally - "cats", "its", "pizza" - and never at the start of a word, so English speakers substitute either `[t]` ("tsu" becomes "too") or `[s]` ("tsu" becomes "sue"). Both are wrong and both are very audible.

- **Fix:** say "cats", hold the final "ts", then say "cats-u" and chop off "ca". What remains is つ. Drill: つくえ / いつ / 一つ / 靴 / 津.
- Related: ち is `[tɕ]` and し is `[ɕ]`, alveolo-palatal sounds made with **no lip rounding**. English "sh" is usually produced with rounded lips. Unround them.

**ん is a moraic nasal, and it is a chameleon.** IMABI counts at least seven allophones in complementary distribution, assimilating to whatever follows:

| Environment | Realisation | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Before bilabials (b, p, m) | like `[m]` | さんぽ, こんばんは, しんぶん |
| Before alveolars (t, d, n) | like `[n]` | みんな, かんたん, あんない |
| Before velars (k, g) | like `[ŋ]`, English "-ng" | りんご, まんが, げんき |
| Before palatals | `[ɲ]`-ish | こんにゃく |
| Before vowels/approximants, or at word end | a nasalized vowel | 日本 (にほん), でんわ, 恋愛 (れんあい) |

Two consequences. First: do not try to pronounce a crisp English "n" everywhere - さんぽ genuinely sounds like "sampo" and that is correct, not sloppy. Second, and more important: **ん is a full mora.** It gets the same beat as any other kana. IMABI flags precisely this - students mispronounce it as `[n]`, which "ignores this property of ん along with the fact that it is moraic". 日本 is に-ほ-ん, three beats, not two.

**Minor extras, for completeness:** Japanese voiceless stops are aspirated less strongly than English ones, so Japanese か is softer than English "ka". And /g/ has a nasal allophone `[ŋ]` word-medially for many speakers, which is the `カ゚` notation you occasionally see in dictionaries - not something to study, just something not to be confused by.

### Mora timing - the thing that decides whether you are understood

Japanese rhythm is mora-based: each mora is *ideally* given the same duration. A mora is one full-size kana, optionally plus a small ゃ/ゅ/ょ. Long vowel marks and small っ each count as their own mora. So:

- 東京 (とうきょう) = と / う / きょ / う = **4 morae**
- 借金 (しゃっきん) = しゃ / っ / き / ん = **4 morae**
- 日本 (にほん) = に / ほ / ん = **3 morae**

(The mora system *as a writing system* is [Kana](../Kana/readme.md)'s territory. What follows is production.)

English is stress-timed: we compress unstressed syllables and stretch stressed ones. Transferring that habit to Japanese destroys the rhythm, and it is a comprehension problem, not just an accent problem. Two specific killers:

**Killer 1: long vs short vowels.** These are different words, and getting it wrong does not produce a slight accent, it produces the wrong word.

| Short | Long | Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| おばさん (aunt, middle-aged woman) | おばあさん (grandmother) | The classic. Getting this backwards in person is memorable. |
| おじさん (uncle) | おじいさん (grandfather) | Same pattern. |
| びょういん 病院 (hospital) | びよういん 美容院 (beauty salon) | 4 morae vs 5 - びょ is one mora, びよ is two. |
| しゅじん 主人 (husband) | しゅうじん 囚人 (prisoner) | Worth getting right. |

**Killer 2: the geminate っ.** IMABI describes it as a moraic obstruent that "disrupts" even timing, causing a pause and an intensification of the following sound. It is a *beat of silence* with the mouth already closed on the next consonant - not a doubled letter you pronounce faster.

| Pair | |
| :--- | :--- |
| きて (来て, come) / きって (切って, cut) / きいて (聞いて, listen) | Three different words, three different rhythms: 2 / 3 / 3 morae |
| かた (肩, shoulder) / かった (買った, bought) | |
| いた (居た, was there) / いった (行った, went) | |

- **Drill:** tap a finger once per mora at about one tap per half-second while you say the word. If you cannot fit the word to the taps, your timing is wrong. Then speed up. This feels childish and it works.
- **Realism check:** IMABI also notes that perfectly equal mora duration is an idealisation - real speech has vowel length effects, moraic obstruents, and some diphthongization. Aim for the ideal; native speech will deviate from it and that is fine.

## Pitch accent

This is the most over-discussed and most badly-explained topic in Japanese learning. What follows sticks to what the in-repo [pitch accent primer](<../General content/Tatsumoto Blog offline copy/tatsumoto.neocities.org/blog/japanese-pitch-accents.html>) states, because pitch accent is exactly the sort of topic where confident-sounding wrong explanations circulate freely.

### What it actually is

Japanese is a **pitch-accent** language. Words carry a high/low pitch pattern assigned per **mora**. This is different from English **stress** accent, where an emphasized syllable gets changes in pitch *and* duration *and* loudness. In Japanese, the duration of each mora stays consistent and only the pitch varies.

Because the rhythm stays flat, it is sometimes claimed that Japanese has no accent at all. This is false, and you will meet the claim in this very folder: *Speak Japanese with Confidence* tells the reader on page 16 that "Japanese, unlike English, has no stress accent so every syllable has equal stress", and that ひろしま should be "Hi-ro-shi-ma" rather than "Hi-RO-shi-ma". The first half is correct and the conclusion is misleading - the *stress* is even, but the *pitch* is not.

The correct Japanese term is **高低アクセント**. Casually, Japanese speakers often call it イントネーション. So when a non-linguist native tells you your "intonation" is off, they usually mean your pitch accent.

### The notation

Dictionaries express accent as a single number: **the mora after which the pitch drops from high to low.** `1` means the drop comes after the first mora. `0` means there is no drop at all.

That is the entire notation. Once you know the number and the mora count, you know the pattern.

### The four patterns

Standard Japanese words fall into four patterns. The primer makes a point worth internalising: the most unnatural-sounding Japanese comes not from using the *wrong* pattern but from using a pattern that **does not exist in Japanese at all** - a mistake no native would make. Getting into the set of four is most of the benefit.

| Pattern | Number | Shape | Following particle | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **平板 (heiban)** - "flat board" | 0 | First mora low, everything after it high, no drop | **High** | 時間 じかん -> じかんが |
| **頭高 (atamadaka)** - "head high" | 1 | First mora high, everything after it low | Low | 天気 てんき -> てんきが |
| **中高 (nakadaka)** - "middle high" | 2 .. n-1 | Starts low, rises, drops somewhere *inside* the word | Low | 一つ ひとつ (2), 工場 こうじょう (3) |
| **尾高 (odaka)** - "tail high" | n (= mora count) | Starts low, rises, stays high to the end - the drop lands on what follows | Low | 川 かわ -> かわが |

Notes that make this usable:

- **中高 words have at least three morae**, and the accent number can never equal the mora count - that case is 尾高 by definition.
- **The difference between 平板 and 尾高 is invisible in isolation** and only shows up on the following particle. かわ (尾高) and じかん (平板) both end high; add が and one drops, one does not. This is why pitch drills use particles.
- **起伏式 (kifuku)** is an umbrella term covering 頭高 + 中高 + 尾高 - i.e. everything that has a downstep. It is not a fifth pattern.
- Two structural rules from the primer: the first and second morae always differ in pitch (it is impossible for a standalone word to be all-high or all-low), and once the pitch drops it stays down for the rest of the word.
- **If you have to guess, guess 平板.** Per the primer, among the 10,000 most common words 平板 is about 55%, 頭高 about 25%, 中高 about 15%. Treat those as community figures from that source rather than as a measurement you can cite.
- Accent is not fixed under composition: two 平板 nouns compounding usually give a 中高 result - 携帯 (0) + 電話 (0) -> 携帯電話 (5). And ます overrides a verb's own accent entirely (drop after ま: いきます, みます, たべます).

### Minimal pairs that are only distinguished by pitch

These are the cases where wrong pitch is not an accent, it is the wrong word. Accent numbers below are as given in the in-repo primer.

| Reading | Words | Accents |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| はし | 橋 (bridge) / 箸 (chopsticks) / 端 (edge) | 2 / 1 / 0 - all three patterns on one reading |
| かみ | 神 (god) / 紙 (paper) / 髪 (hair) | 1 / 2 / 2 - note 紙 and 髪 are the *same* |
| はな | 花 (flower) / 鼻 (nose) | 2 / 0 |
| き | 木 (tree) / 気 (spirit, mind) | 1 / 0 |
| ひ | 火 (fire) / 日 (day) | 1 / 0 |
| かう | 飼う (keep a pet) / 買う (buy) | 1 / 0 |
| おもい | 思い (thought) / 重い (heavy) | 2 / 0 |

Two cautions. First, on the famous 雨 / 飴 (rain / candy) pair: 雨 is 頭高 - the accent sits on the first mora, so it runs HIGH-low. 飴 is 平板 - accentless, so it runs low-HIGH and stays high onto a following particle (飴が). Confirm it in a pitch dictionary if you like, and build the habit of checking every new pair there rather than trusting any guide, including this one. Second, some words legitimately have **more than one** accepted accent in Standard Japanese (ちょっと is listed as both 1 and 0); dictionaries list the common one first, but which is actually dominant in speech is a question for your ears, not the dictionary.

### Should you study pitch accent deliberately?

Both camps have a real case.

**The "study it explicitly, from early on" camp** (Dogen and the pitch-serious part of the community, MIA historically):

- Pitch is the largest single component of "sounds foreign" once your phonemes are correct. You can have perfect grammar and vocabulary and still sound unmistakably non-native purely from pitch.
- It is much harder to fix later than to learn right. Once you have said a word 5,000 times with the wrong pattern, the motor habit is set.
- It is cheap to *be aware* of: installing a pitch dictionary in Yomitan costs ten minutes once, and then every word you ever look up shows its accent. [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) makes the same argument from a different angle - install it on day one, because retrofitting pitch onto three thousand existing cards is miserable.
- In some cases it is load-bearing for comprehension, and some Japanese jokes are built on it.

**The "acquire it from listening" camp** (much of the AJATT-descended community):

- Native children acquire it with zero explicit study. High-volume listening does transfer a large fraction of it.
- Beginner attention is scarce and pitch competes with vocabulary, grammar and kanji - all of which have far higher immediate returns. A learner with 800 words and perfect pitch cannot have a conversation.
- Explicit pitch study without a large vocabulary is memorising numbers attached to words you cannot use.
- Wrong pitch almost never blocks communication. Context resolves 橋 vs 箸 instantly.

The in-repo primer, which is written from inside the immersion community, is unusually honest about where this lands: its authors' observation is that even dedicated immersion learners typically do **not** reach native-level pitch from immersion alone, ending around 50-60% correct, and that the heavy use of reading, text flashcards and dictionaries - the things that make immersion learning fast - is part of what damages pitch and pronunciation. Treat that percentage as a community observation, not a study.

**What the research actually says, and what it does not.** Read the scope carefully, because it is narrower than it looks.

Matsuzaki et al. (1994) ran a controlled comparison with twelve Korean learners and found that instruction using a *visualized* prosody representation produced better pronunciation than instruction using bare accent-nucleus notation. Kanamura (2020) surveys how teachers actually handle pitch in the classroom, and Kori (2013) covers sentence-level intonation - the question rise without か - which word-level pitch data says nothing about. Kohno (1998) is the scholarship behind mora timing.

The important caveat: **Matsuzaki compares two ways of teaching pitch explicitly. It does not compare explicit study against pure immersion.** So it is evidence that *if* you study pitch deliberately, a visual representation beats a notation you have to decode - which is a real and useful finding, and an argument for tools like kotu.io over memorising accent numbers. It is not evidence that the explicit camp is right and the immersion camp is wrong. That comparison does not appear to have been run on adult learners of Japanese in a form that would settle it. Twelve learners in 1994 is also a small, dated study; treat it as a data point, not a verdict.

### Regional reality

**Standard Tokyo pitch is a choice, not the truth.** The four patterns above describe 標準語 - the variety taught in schools and used by NHK announcers. Accent varies substantially across Japan; one word can carry different patterns in different regions. The primer notes that even actual Tokyo speech differs from textbook 標準語 despite 標準語 often being called "Tokyo dialect".

Kansai, in particular, has a genuinely different accent *system* - not a sloppier version of Tokyo, a different assignment of patterns. Many words carry an accent in Kansai where Tokyo has none, and the reverse, so the two systems often strike the ear as mirror images of one another - but treat that as a loose impression rather than a conversion rule, because Kansai accent is a system in its own right and not a transformation applied to Tokyo. If you learn Japanese primarily from Kansai-heavy media, or from a partner from Osaka, you will pick some of that up. That is not an error; it is a dialect. Just be aware of which one you are speaking, and do not "correct" a native speaker's accent to match your dictionary.

Dialects as a cultural and social phenomenon belong to [Culture](../Culture/readme.md). For Kyoto specifically, the in-repo Tatsumoto resource list points at [akenotsuki.com/kyookotoba](http://www.akenotsuki.com/kyookotoba/).

## Shadowing and chorusing, practically

This is the technique. If you take one procedure away from this guide, take this one.

**Terminology:** **chorusing** is speaking *simultaneously* with the audio, matching it in real time. **Shadowing** is speaking a beat *behind* it, roughly half a second to a second, like a simultaneous interpreter. Chorusing is easier and trains matching; shadowing is harder and trains production under load. Do both, in that order.

### The procedure

**Material requirements:** a **15-30 second** clip, clean single-speaker audio, no music, with a **transcript**. Short is essential. Beginners try to shadow a whole three-minute scene and get nothing out of it. You want a clip short enough that you will say it twenty times without wanting to die.

The audio in this folder is close to ideal for this - studio-recorded, slow, clearly articulated, with full transcripts in the accompanying PDFs. See [Resources](#resources).

1. **Listen 5 times with no text.** Eyes closed. You are not trying to understand; you are mapping the rhythm and the melody. Notice where the pitch rises and falls and where the speaker pauses.
2. **Read the transcript while listening, twice.** Now attach the sounds to the words. Mark the places where what you *hear* differs from what you would have *guessed* from the text - this is where devoicing, mora timing and pitch live. These marks are the actual lesson.
3. **Chorus 10 times.** Play the audio and speak along with it, out loud, at full volume, matching timing exactly. You will fall behind at the hard parts. Do not stop the audio - let yourself fall behind and catch up. Falling behind at the same place every time tells you where your articulation is slow.
4. **Shadow 5 times.** Now start about half a second behind the speaker and stay there. Harder than it sounds, because you are listening and producing at the same time. This is the rep that builds real-time production.
5. **Record yourself saying the clip solo, with no audio playing.** Phone voice memo is fine.
6. **A/B the recording against the original.** Play three seconds of the original, then three seconds of yourself, then the original again. Do not listen to whole takes; the differences vanish over long stretches.
7. **Pick exactly ONE thing to fix.** Not five. One: "my つ is a t", or "I am rushing the っ in 行った", or "my です is fully voiced". Redo steps 3-6 with only that in mind.

### How much, how often

- **10-15 minutes a day.** It is intense; more than 20 minutes and quality collapses.
- **Stay on one clip for 3-7 days**, then replace it. Switching clips daily is the most common way to make this technique useless - the gains come from the tenth repetition, not the first.
- **Keep your recordings**, dated. This is your progress instrumentation (see [Measuring progress](#measuring-progress)).

### Why recording yourself is non-negotiable

You cannot hear your own speech accurately while producing it. Two reasons: a large part of what you hear when you talk arrives through bone conduction rather than through the air, so your voice literally sounds different to you than to anyone else; and while you are speaking, your attention is on production, not evaluation. The result is that learners are systematically unable to hear their own errors in real time and are routinely astonished by their first recording.

It is uncomfortable. Everyone hates the sound of their own recorded voice, and hearing yourself speak bad Japanese is worse. Do it anyway - and notice that the discomfort is itself information: it means you can hear the gap, which means you can close it. The learners who never record are not the ones with no gap; they are the ones who do not know the size of theirs.

## Register - the practical layer

Getting register wrong is the error with the highest social cost and the lowest learning cost. It is worth an hour.

### The one distinction you must get right early

**Plain form (だ / る) vs です・ます.** Everything else in the politeness system is refinement; this is the load-bearing split.

The practical rule for a learner: **default to です・ます with everyone except people you met as peers in an explicitly casual setting.** Strangers, shop staff, colleagues, anyone older, anyone senior, anyone whose relationship to you you are unsure about - です・ます. It can read as slightly stiff among friends. Stiff is recoverable; presumptuous is not.

The mirror-image error is real too: sticking rigidly to です・ます with close friends for years sounds distant and can quietly keep people at arm's length. But that is a year-two problem. Get the default right first.

The grammar itself - how the forms are built, how keigo is constructed - belongs to [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md). The social structure underneath it, and why the hierarchy works the way it does, belongs to [Culture](../Culture/readme.md).

### The anime trap

If your immersion is anime and manga - and for many learners using this repo, it is - you are absorbing a register that is casual, frequently masculine, frequently blunt, and sometimes not a register real people use at all. Anime dialogue is *written*, by adults, for dramatic effect, with genre conventions attached.

Things that feel normal after 500 episodes and are not normal in a Tokyo office:

| From anime | What it actually is |
| :--- | :--- |
| お前 | Blunt second-person. Fine between close friends or downward; rude to a stranger, insulting to a superior. Use the person's name plus さん instead. |
| 貴様 | Archaic and hostile. A fiction word. Never use it. |
| ～だぜ, ～だぞ | Strongly masculine, casual, assertive. Marked even among Japanese men; very marked from a foreigner. |
| ～だろ / ～だろう (as a challenge) | Casual and confrontational depending on delivery. |
| ～のだ / ～のである | Written, declarative, explanatory register. Reads as a narrator or a villain monologue, not speech. |
| ～かしら, ～わ (as a sentence-final particle) | Stylized "feminine" speech; old-fashioned or character-voice in most real contexts. |
| ぼく / おれ / あたし / わたくし | All real, all carrying different signals about gender, age, formality and self-presentation. Not interchangeable. わたし is the safe default for anyone. |

**None of this means stop watching anime.** Anime immersion is a fantastic input source and it is the reason many of us are learning at all. It means: treat what you absorb as **recognition** knowledge by default, and promote a phrase to **production** only when you have heard a real person in a comparable social position say it. That is the whole discipline - a one-way valve with a manual override.

### The gendered-speech question, honestly

Japanese does have gendered speech patterns, and they are much weaker and more fluid in reality than in fiction. A great deal of anime "female speech" - the sentence-final わ, かしら, heavy use of あたし - is a stylistic convention that signals a character type, not a transcript of how women in Tokyo speak in 2026. Likewise the ぜ / ぞ / だ-heavy male register. Real differences exist, tend to be subtler, vary by age and region, and are shifting.

Practical guidance: わたし and です・ます are unmarked and safe for anyone of any gender. Beyond that, copy real people you have actually heard, not characters. And treat prescriptive "men say X, women say Y" tables with suspicion - [Culture](../Culture/readme.md) is the place for that discussion.

### Keigo is a recognize-first skill

You need to *understand* 敬語 long before you need to *produce* it. On arriving in Japan you will immediately be spoken to in it - by shop staff, station staff, receptionists, doctors and every phone system - and understanding いらっしゃいます / お待ちしております / かしこまりました matters on day one. Producing it correctly matters when you have a job, and at that point it arrives as a small set of templates for your specific role, usually taught to you by a colleague or a manual.

So: learn to parse it early, learn to produce it when you have a concrete reason. The exception worth pre-learning is the set of stock business phrases you will need constantly, which is exactly what [*Teach Yourself Phone Japanese*](<./Teach Yourself Phone Japanese/book.pdf>) in this folder drills - お願いいたします, 少々お待ちください, お電話変わりました, 失礼ですが、どちらさまでしょうか. Phone calls are the hardest register situation for a learner, because there is no context, no face and no gesture, and it is the one this folder happens to cover well.

## Getting real practice

Once you need a human, there are four categories and they are not substitutes for each other.

### 1. Language exchange apps - HelloTalk, Tandem

Reciprocal: you help someone with your native language, they help you with Japanese.

- **Good:** free, large user base, low barrier, and you can be corrected by natives at scale. Good for text and voice-note practice while you are still too nervous for live calls.
- **Bad:** in practice it drifts into text rather than voice. A large fraction of partners want English more than they want to teach Japanese, and you end up as an unpaid English teacher. Quality is wildly variable, the app can feel like a social network, and there is no accountability - your partner will ghost.
- **Links:** [hellotalk.com](https://hellotalk.com/), [tandem.net](https://www.tandem.net/). There is an offline Tofugu review of HelloTalk in this repo at [hellotalk](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japanese-learning-resources-database/hellotalk/index.html>).

### 2. Tutoring marketplaces - iTalki, Preply

You pay a teacher or a community tutor by the lesson.

- **Good:** accountability (a booked lesson happens), someone whose job is to correct you, and the ability to ask "why is this wrong" and get an answer. Rates vary enormously by tutor - community tutors are substantially cheaper than certified teachers, and the cheapest tutor who will correct you is better value than the best tutor who will not. Check rates on the platform rather than trusting any number you read in a guide, including this one.
- **Bad:** it costs money, quality varies, and a bad tutor will run you through a textbook you could have read yourself. Also the confidence trap: a tutor is professionally encouraging and will tell you your Japanese is 上手 when it is not.
- **Links:** [italki.com](https://www.italki.com/), [preply.com](https://preply.com/).

**The key argument for paying:** a friend will not correct you. Correction is socially expensive - interrupting someone to fix their particle is rude, and your Japanese friends will smile and move on. A paid tutor has correction as the *product*. That is the thing you cannot get for free, and it is the thing that stops self-talk and immersion from entrenching your errors.

**The underrated option:** once you have a base, book a tutor for **free conversation only** - no curriculum, no textbook, just 50 minutes of talking with someone who takes notes and gives you a correction list at the end. For an immersion learner this is usually far more valuable than structured lessons, because your input is already handling vocabulary and grammar; what you are missing is corrected output. Ask explicitly for the correction list. Many tutors will not volunteer it.

### 3. Community and in-person

Local Japanese-language meetups, university Japanese clubs, city international-exchange centres, Japanese cultural associations, conversation cafes. In-person practice adds the things a video call strips out: gesture, interruption, background noise, and a group where you are not the centre of attention.

For the relocation case specifically: once you are in Japan, the highest-value practice is the unglamorous kind - talking to your colleagues at lunch, the neighbour, the konbini, the city ward office. Living in Japan does not automatically teach you Japanese; plenty of foreign engineers spend five years in Tokyo inside an English-speaking bubble. The bubble is the default outcome, not the exception, and avoiding it is a deliberate decision you make repeatedly.

### 4. Solo practice, which is most of your hours

Shadowing (above), self-talk, and read-aloud.

- **Self-talk:** narrate your day out loud. Commute, cooking, walking. Start at the level of 「今、コーヒーを作っています」and grow. Deliberately try to say the thing you *want* to say, notice the exact word you are missing, and look it up afterwards. Those lookups are the best vocabulary cards you will ever make - see [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md) for mining and [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) for card formats.
- **Read-aloud:** read your Anki sentences out loud rather than silently. This is nearly free - you are doing the reviews anyway - and it converts recognition reps into articulation reps. It is also the single easiest habit to add on day 1.
- **Recorded diary:** two minutes of spoken Japanese a day, recorded, about your actual day. Keep them all.

### Conversation-exchange etiquette

- **Split the time explicitly and use a timer.** 25 minutes Japanese, 25 minutes English. Without a timer, exchanges collapse into whichever language is easier, which is English.
- **Bring three topics.** "What shall we talk about" in a language you cannot speak is a dead call.
- **Ask for the correction style you want, up front.** "Please correct my particles, but let me finish my sentence first" is a request natives are happy to honour and will never make on their own.
- **Do not fix their English mid-sentence** if you want the same courtesy.
- **Reliability is the whole currency.** Show up. Exchange partners vanish mostly because the other person flaked first.

## Phase-by-phase plan

### Phase 0 - Build the mouth (week 1-4)

- **Goal:** correct articulation of every mora, and awareness of what pitch accent is.
- **Time:** 15-20 min/day for the first week, then 10 min/day maintenance.
- **Daily routine:** read one section of the [pronunciation reference](#pronunciation-reference) above per day and drill it out loud. Look up five words a day on [Forvo](https://forvo.com/) and imitate them. Read your Anki sentence cards aloud instead of silently. Install a pitch-accent dictionary in Yomitan ([General content](<../General content/readme.md>)).
- **Materials:** this guide. [Kana](../Kana/readme.md)'s audio deck for per-mora native audio. [Forvo](https://forvo.com/). Optionally the offline [pitch accent primer](<../General content/Tatsumoto Blog offline copy/tatsumoto.neocities.org/blog/japanese-pitch-accents.html>).
- **Done when:** you can (a) hold each of the five vowels for three seconds without the sound drifting, on a recording you have listened back to; (b) produce ら り る れ ろ as taps and hear the difference from your own English R and L on a recording; (c) say つくえ, ふとん, 一つ, 日本, さんぽ, りんご correctly and explain what is happening in each; (d) pass a blind 10-pair listening test on おばさん/おばあさん and きて/きって/きいて at 9 out of 10; (e) define 平板, 頭高, 中高 and 尾高 from memory.

### Phase 1 - Shadowing (week 2 - month 6, then forever)

- **Goal:** native-like rhythm and prosody in connected speech; a mouth that can keep up.
- **Time:** 10-15 min/day.
- **Daily routine:** the seven-step procedure above, on one clip, for a week per clip.
- **Materials:** [*Speak Japanese with Confidence*](<./Speak Japanese with Confidence/Speak Japanese with confidence.pdf>) CD1 and CD2 audio are ideal starter clips - studio-clean, slow, with a printed transcript. **Use the audio and ignore the romaji text**: the book is entirely romaji, which is exactly the habit [Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md) tells you to break. Transcribe the line into kana yourself, or read along only after you have heard it. Later, move to clips from your own immersion (see [Listening](../Listening/readme.md)) or the natural-speed dialogues in [*Nihongo Nama Chuukei*](<./Speaking Skills Learned through Listening Japanese Live/Nihongo Nama Chuukei.pdf>).
- **Done when:** on a blind A/B of your latest recording against the original, **you can identify your own three biggest differences** without being told what to listen for. Not "you sound identical" - that is a multi-year target. The milestone is that your ear works.

### Phase 2 - Self-talk and read-aloud (month 3 - 9)

- **Goal:** retrieval. Getting from concept to mouth without a dictionary.
- **Time:** 10 min of deliberate monologue, plus reading all Anki sentences aloud.
- **Daily routine:** narrate one real activity out loud in Japanese. Record a two-minute spoken diary. Note every word you reached for and did not have, and mine those.
- **Materials:** your own life. The role-play (ロールプレイ) prompts in [*Nihongo Nama Chuukei*](<./Speaking Skills Learned through Listening Japanese Live/Nihongo Nama Chuukei.pdf>) once you are past beginner grammar - it is built around functions like 誘う (inviting), 申し出る (offering), 伝言をする (leaving a message), which is exactly the vocabulary a monologue does not force.
- **Done when:** you can produce **three continuous minutes** about your day, recorded, with no English, no long silences, and no more than three switches to "how do I say...". The recording is the proof; do not grade this from memory.

### Phase 3 - First real conversations (month 6 - 12; earlier if you are moving sooner)

- **Goal:** real-time comprehension plus production plus recovery from failure.
- **Time:** one 45-60 min tutored session a week, plus everything above.
- **Daily routine:** unchanged, plus weekly lesson prep - three topics and five sentences you want to try.
- **Materials:** a community tutor on [italki.com](https://www.italki.com/) or [preply.com](https://preply.com/); a partner on [hellotalk.com](https://hellotalk.com/) or [tandem.net](https://www.tandem.net/). Ask explicitly for an end-of-lesson correction list.
- **Done when:** a 30-minute conversation with a native who is not a teacher, in which you switch to English fewer than five times and are asked to repeat yourself fewer than three times.

### Phase 4 - Register and working Japanese (year 1 - 2)

- **Goal:** speak appropriately to a superior, a stranger and a friend, and survive a phone call.
- **Time:** absorbed into existing practice, plus one focused study block on keigo.
- **Daily routine:** as Phase 3, plus deliberate register switching - re-say the same sentence in plain and in です・ます form; shadow keigo audio specifically.
- **Materials:** [*Teach Yourself Phone Japanese*](<./Teach Yourself Phone Japanese/book.pdf>) and its [46 audio tracks](<./Teach Yourself Phone Japanese/Audio/flac>) - business phone keigo with full Japanese script, romaji and English side by side, plus a "using the telephone in Japan" section. [*Nihongo Nama Chuukei*](<./Speaking Skills Learned through Listening Japanese Live/Nihongo Nama Chuukei.pdf>), whose entire design principle is showing the *same function* across different social relationships. [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md) for the keigo machinery, [Culture](../Culture/readme.md) for the hierarchy it encodes.
- **Done when:** you can make a phone call to book something and leave a message, unscripted; and you can switch register mid-conversation when a manager joins the table without stopping to think.

### Phase 5 - De-fossilization and maintenance (ongoing)

- **Goal:** fix what has hardened; stop decay.
- **Time:** the monthly benchmark, plus a pitch pass when you feel ready.
- **Daily routine:** keep shadowing. Keep a weekly conversation on the calendar even when you feel fluent - **this is the phase where people silently regress.**
- **Materials:** [kotu.io](https://kotu.io/) minimal-pair drills, [Dogen's phonetics series](https://www.patreon.com/dogen), [OJAD](https://www.gavo.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/ojad/) for verb-form accent, your archive of recordings.
- **Done when:** never. This is the steady state.

## Common pitfalls

| The mistake | Why it feels right | The fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Waiting until you are "ready" to speak** | It genuinely is true that more input makes output better, and there is always more input to get. It feels like patience. | It is avoidance with a theory attached. Set a *date*, not a threshold - "first tutored lesson on month 6, day 1" - and book it in advance. Notice that you will feel exactly as unready on that day. |
| **Practising without ever recording yourself** | You are speaking; speaking is practice; it feels productive. | You cannot hear your own errors live. Record weekly, minimum. If you have never listened to yourself speak Japanese, you do not know how you sound. |
| **Ignoring pitch accent entirely** | It seems like an advanced concern, and communication works without it. | Two hours to learn the four patterns plus a Yomitan pitch dictionary. That is the whole minimum, and it buys you the ability to notice pitch for free forever. |
| **Obsessing over pitch accent** | It is rule-based, measurable, and feels like precision engineering. Very attractive to technical learners. | Cap it. At under ~2,000 words, vocabulary beats pitch by a wide margin. Revisit when real conversation starts. |
| **Katakana-English pronunciation bleeding both ways** | コンピューター really is the Japanese word, and saying it "correctly" feels like showing off. | Both directions are errors: saying "computer" in an English accent in a Japanese sentence is wrong, and letting コンピューター leak into your English is a separate and funnier problem. Treat loanwords as Japanese words with Japanese mora structure - コ・ン・ピュー・ター. |
| **Flat, robotic prosody from reading-only study** | Each word is individually correct, so the sentence must be correct. | Sentences have contours, not just words. Shadowing is the only fix - you cannot get prosody from text. |
| **Memorising phrases you cannot vary** | Phrasebook sentences produce instant successful interactions. It feels like fluency. | For every memorised phrase, produce three variations - change the subject, the tense, the politeness level. If you cannot, you have memorised a sound, not a sentence. |
| **Speaking only with other learners** | It is comfortable, cheap and available, and you understand each other perfectly. | You understand each other *because* you share the same errors. Learner Japanese is a mutually intelligible dialect of wrong. Keep it, but make sure at least one regular partner is a native. |
| **Letting anime register leak out** | 500 hours of hearing お前 and ～だぜ makes them feel like normal words. They are in your head as available options. | Recognition by default, production on evidence. Promote a phrase only after hearing a real person in a comparable social role use it. Default to です・ます and わたし. |
| **The confidence trap** | Your tutor says 上手ですね, your exchange partner understands everything, so you must be good. | Both are being kind, and a tutor is trained to understand learner Japanese - they are the worst possible measure of intelligibility. Test on a stranger who has no stake in your feelings: a shop, a station, a taxi. |
| **Practising in your head instead of out loud** | Silent rehearsal feels like the same thing and is far less embarrassing. | It is not the same thing. Speaking is motor. Silent practice trains none of the muscles or the coordination. Out loud, at volume, or it does not count. |
| **Never letting the conversation get hard** | Steering onto familiar topics produces smooth, successful conversations. | Smooth conversation at your comfort level teaches nothing. Bring one topic per session that you know you cannot handle yet. The point is to fail informatively. |
| **Skipping the monthly benchmark because progress feels invisible** | Month to month you genuinely cannot hear a difference, so it feels pointless. | That is exactly why you file the recordings instead of trusting your memory. Compare across three months, not one. The gap is obvious and it is the best motivation available. |

## Measuring progress

Speaking is the skill where self-assessment is least reliable, because the only person who never hears your Japanese properly is you. So instrument it. All of these are concrete, repeatable and unflattering.

**1. The monthly benchmark recording.** Pick one fixed passage - 60-90 seconds, with native audio available. Read it aloud and record it on the same day every month, forever. Compare across three-month gaps, never one-month. This is the single best progress signal in this guide and it costs two minutes a month.

**2. The stranger test.** Can a native who is *not* your teacher, *not* your exchange partner, and has no investment in encouraging you understand you on the first try? Count the repeats. Teachers and exchange partners develop an ear for learner Japanese and become useless as instruments. Shop staff, station staff and taxi drivers do not.

**3. Sustained-speech duration.** How many minutes can you talk continuously with no English and no long stalls? Time it. Track the number. 30 seconds, then 3 minutes, then 10. It is a single number that moves, which is rare in language learning.

**4. Self-correction rate.** Can you catch and repair your own error *mid-sentence* without stopping? This is the most underrated milestone in this guide. It means your internal monitor now runs in Japanese and in real time. Count self-repairs per conversation from your recordings - it should go **up** first (you are noticing more) and then down (you are making fewer).

**5. The shadowing A/B test.** Record the clip you have been shadowing, then blind-compare three-second segments against the original. Score yourself on three axes: rhythm (mora timing), melody (pitch contour), and phonemes (specific sounds). Honestly, with numbers out of 10. Track the three separately - they improve at different rates, and knowing which one is lagging tells you what to drill.

**6. Pitch perception score.** A minimal-pair test like [kotu.io](https://kotu.io/) gives you a percentage. It measures your *ear*, not your mouth, but the ear is the prerequisite - a rising perception score predicts a rising production score. Re-test quarterly.

**7. Register accuracy.** From a recorded conversation, count the number of times you used the wrong politeness level. This one goes to zero long before your grammar does, and it is the one with real social consequences.

**What not to use as a measure:** a JLPT level (it does not test speaking at all - see [JLPT](../JLPT/readme.md)), how much anime you understand (recognition, not production), or how confident you feel. Confidence and ability decouple badly in both directions.
