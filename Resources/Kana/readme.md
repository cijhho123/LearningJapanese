> **TL;DR**
> - Kana are the two phonetic scripts of Japanese - **hiragana** (ひらがな, the grammatical glue and native words) and **katakana** (カタカナ, loanwords, onomatopoeia, emphasis). Each has 46 base signs; counting dakuten and digraphs it is about 104 signs per script.
> - They are **moraic**, not alphabetic: one sign = one beat. This is where Japanese rhythm comes from, and getting it wrong early costs you months of mishearing.
> - Start on **day 1**, before grammar, vocabulary or kanji. This is the only part of Japanese that is genuinely finishable in a week, and everything else is gated behind it.
> - Recommended path: **audio-first recognition SRS for both scripts inside one week** ([Recognizing kana.apkg](<./Anki Decks/Recognizing kana.apkg>), 232 cards with native audio), mnemonics only as a rescue for the handful that will not stick, writing postponed, then let real reading finish the job.
> - The real failure mode is not hiragana. It is **half-learning katakana** and paying for it for a year. Learn both together, on purpose.

## What this is and why it matters

"Kana" (仮名) is the umbrella term for hiragana and katakana. Both encode exactly the same sounds - あ and ア are both `a` - and differ only in shape and in what they are used for. They are usually called syllabaries, but the precise term is **moraic scripts**: each sign represents one *mora*, a timing unit. That distinction is not pedantry; it is the single most useful thing in this guide (see [Mora vs syllable](#mora-vs-syllable-why-japanese-rhythm-feels-off)).

Unlike kanji, kana are (almost) perfectly regular. A kana sign has one reading, forever. There are exactly three irregular cases and four obsolete characters, all listed below. That regularity is why kana study is a bounded task with a finish line - a rare and precious thing in Japanese. Treat it as a sprint, not a semester.

### The three-script system

Modern Japanese writes with all three scripts interleaved, and the script itself carries information. Look at where the boundaries fall:

| Sentence | 私 | は | コンピューター | で | アニメ | を | 見 | た |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Script | kanji | hiragana | katakana | hiragana | katakana | hiragana | kanji | hiragana |
| Role | word stem | topic particle | loanword | particle | loanword | object particle | verb stem | verb ending |

`私はコンピューターでアニメを見た。` - "I watched anime on the computer." Kanji carry the content, hiragana carry the grammar, katakana flag the foreign words. Because Japanese does not use spaces, the script changes *are* the word boundaries. This is why a reader who only half-reads katakana loses the ability to parse sentences, not just words.

### What hiragana does

- **Particles**: は, が, を, に, で, と, も, から, まで, の. Every sentence, forever.
- **Okurigana** - the inflecting tails of verbs and adjectives: 食**べる**, 食**べた**, 高**い**, 高**かった**. Kanji gives the stem, hiragana gives the tense and politeness.
- **Native words with no common kanji or deliberately written in kana**: こと, もの, ある, する, きれい, ちょっと, ありがとう.
- **Furigana** - small kana printed above kanji to give the reading. Manga aimed at younger readers often has furigana over every kanji, which makes it readable the moment you know hiragana.
- **Grammatical scaffolding you will meet in week 1**: です, ます, ください, でしょう.

### What katakana does

The obvious uses:

- **Loanwords** (外来語): コンピューター (computer), ホテル (hotel), コーヒー (coffee).
- **Foreign names and places**: マイケル, イスラエル, ニューヨーク.
- **Onomatopoeia and mimetics**: ドキドキ (heartbeat), ゴロゴロ (rumbling/lounging), ワクワク (excitement), ニャー (meow). Anime and manga are saturated with these.
- **Emphasis** - the functional equivalent of *italics*: ヤバイ, スゴイ, ダメ. A word normally written in kana or kanji gets switched to katakana to make it pop.

The less obvious uses, which is where learners get ambushed:

- **Plant, animal and species names** in casual or scientific writing: ネコ (cat), イヌ (dog), バラ (rose), サル (monkey). You learned 猫 and then a nature documentary hits you with ネコ.
- **Medical and technical terms** where the kanji is obscure: ガン (cancer, 癌), レントゲン (X-ray, from Roentgen).
- **Non-human or non-standard speech**: robots, aliens, computers, and monsters are often rendered entirely in katakana. Also used for broken/foreigner-accented Japanese and for characters who are shouting or in shock.
- **Historical official style**: pre-war legal documents, government paperwork and telegrams were written in kanji + katakana rather than kanji + hiragana. If you ever read an old statute or a wartime document, hiragana is the script that looks missing.
- **Company and product branding**: トヨタ, ソニー, カップヌードル.
- **Telegram and signboard style**: all-katakana text for compactness and legibility.

## Where it fits in the journey

**Prerequisites:** none. This is genuinely step one, day one.

**What it unlocks:**

- Every beginner grammar resource. Tae Kim's guide uses kana from the first lesson and does not hold your hand with romaji.
- Dictionary lookups and pop-up dictionaries. You cannot type a word you cannot spell in kana.
- Anki decks worth using. Good vocabulary and sentence decks show kana, not romaji.
- Furigana, and therefore a huge amount of manga and children's material.
- Japanese IME typing, which is kana-based (see [Writing](../Writing/readme.md)).
- JLPT N5, where the vocabulary and reading sections are kana-heavy by design.

**What it does NOT unlock:** comprehension. Kana is a *decoding* skill. After a week you will be able to pronounce a sentence you do not understand at all - which is real progress, but it is not reading. Decoding katakana loanwords into English words is a further separate skill with its own section below.

**Three boundaries worth stating up front:**

- **Romaji**: use kana from the start and let romaji die. It exists to help non-learners pronounce place names, not to help you read. See [Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md) for the romanization systems and why to drop them.
- **Pitch accent**: kana do not encode it. はし can be はꜛし or はꜜし and the writing is identical. That is a phonetics topic - see [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md).
- **Stroke order and handwriting as a discipline**: owned by [Writing](../Writing/readme.md). The kana-specific tracing sheets live in this folder, but the "how to hold a pen and why stroke order exists" material belongs there.

**Do not wait for perfection before moving on.** Start grammar and vocabulary once you can read most kana with effort - roughly when you are at 90% recognition. The last 10% and all of your speed will come from reading actual sentences, not from more chart drilling.

## Basic terms and the shape of the system

The chapter below explains every part of the kana system properly. What it does not do - what almost no kana resource does - is hand you the **names**. Japanese textbooks, dictionary tags, IME settings, grammar explanations and the datasets in this folder all use 拗音, 促音, 撥音, 濁点 and ラ行 without glossing them, and you will meet all of them before anyone defines them.

So this section is the index and the vocabulary for the chapter that follows it, not a second copy of it. **Nothing is taught here. Every row points at the section that does the teaching.** Read it once for orientation and come back when a term ambushes you somewhere else.

Read that table as one claim: **46 is the chart, 104 is the reading task, and the extras are three special signs plus a historical footnote.** The jump from 46 to 104 is not decoration - it is [where the grammar lives](#dakuten-and-handakuten).

**Why the extended katakana do not move the count.** ヴァ, ファ, ティ, ウィ, シェ and the rest *look* like another 20 characters to learn and are not. They are combinations of signs already inside the 104 plus a 小書き仮名, assembled exactly the way きゃ is assembled from き and ゃ. Learning them is learning a spelling convention, which is why they cost an afternoon and not a week. The single genuine new sign is ヴ (ウ with a dakuten), and even Japanese treats it as optional - バイオリン and ヴァイオリン are both current.

**How the numbers above were checked, since you should not take them on trust.** They are not a second opinion: this guide's own [youon table](#youon-digraphs) and the folder's [`kana.json`](<./Kana and Mora Inventory (JSON)/kana.json>) agree cell for cell - 46 base, 20 dakuten, 5 handakuten, 33 digraphs per script, with the digraphs splitting 21 / 9 / 3 across 清音, 濁音 and 半濁音 bases in both sources independently. One consequence worth knowing before you query the dataset: 215 entries means it is the **core** system only. It does not carry the small vowel kana, ヴ, the extended katakana combinations, or rare digraphs such as ぢゃ. For those, the [katakana chart](<./Katakana/katakana-chart.webp>) at the top of this page is the more complete reference.

### Where the shapes came from, and the other ordering

You do not need any of this to read Japanese. You need it to stop the historical terms above from being trivia.

**Both scripts descend from the same ancestor, which is why they encode identical sounds.** Before kana, Japanese was written in 万葉仮名 - kanji chosen for their sound alone. 平仮名 is what happened when scribes wrote those kanji in ever more cursive 草書 forms until the shapes broke free and became signs in their own right; that is why hiragana is round and flowing. 片仮名 came from the opposite motive: from the 9th century, Buddhist scholars annotating Chinese texts needed fast, small phonetic marks, so they clipped a *fragment* of the source kanji - 伊 gives イ, 呂 gives ロ - and occasionally took a simple kanji whole (二 gives ニ, 八 gives ハ). That is why katakana is angular. One ancestor, two abbreviation strategies, one phonology. It also explains 変体仮名: for most of history several shapes per sound were all correct, and it took the 1900 elementary-school ordinance (小学校令施行規則) to fix one shape per mora and retire the rest - along with the 合略仮名 ligatures.

**And there is a second enumeration order still in circulation.** いろは順 comes from the いろは歌, a 47-kana poem that uses every kana of its day exactly once, and it was the standard way to enumerate anything in Japanese until 五十音順 displaced it. You still meet it as イ, ロ, ハ used where English writes a, b, c - on old indexes, in traditional arts, and in things numbered before the war. Two practical payoffs: the poem contains ゐ and ゑ but not ん, which is a compact argument for [spending four minutes on the obsolete pair](#obsolete-and-extended-kana); and when you find a reference work whose index is not in あいうえお order, this is why.

## The kana sound system

This is the part that beginner guides compress into a chart and a shrug. Everything below is high-frequency and will hit you in your first week of real material.

### Reading the grid

The 五十音 (gojuuon, "fifty sounds") is a 5 x 10 grid plus one extra sign:

- **5 vowels**, always in this order: `a i u e o` (あ い う え お). Japanese orders everything this way - dictionaries, verb conjugation tables, name lists. Learn the order, not just the characters.
- **10 rows**: the bare vowel row, then the 9 consonant rows `k s t n h m y r w`.
- **Plus ん** (`n`), which belongs to no row.

That is 50 cells, five of which are empty in modern Japanese (`yi`, `ye`, `wi`, `wu`, `we`), leaving 45 filled - so you get **46 signs** per script including ん.

Vowel values, once, correctly:

| Kana | Sound | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| あ / ア | `a` | as in "father", short and open |
| い / イ | `i` | as in "machine", never as in "sit" |
| う / ウ | `u` | like "oo" in "boot" but with **unrounded** lips |
| え / エ | `e` | as in "bed", never the English "ay" diphthong |
| お / オ | `o` | as in "for", pure, no glide to "ow" |

There are no diphthongs and no schwa. Every vowel is crisp and equal in length - which is exactly why long vowels are a separate phenomenon rather than "just holding it a bit".

The bolded cells are the irregular ones, and they are irregular in *sound*, not in usage:

- **し shi, ち chi, つ tsu** - the `s`, `t` rows palatalize before `i` and `u`. Never `si`, `ti`, `tu`.
- **ふ fu** - not an English `f`. No teeth involved; it is a soft bilabial puff, halfway to `hu`.
- **ら り る れ ろ** - not an English `r` and not an `l`. A single quick tap of the tongue behind the teeth, closest to the `tt` in American "butter".
- **を** - pronounced `o`, not `wo`. It only ever appears as the object particle in modern Japanese.

### Dakuten and handakuten

Two diacritics turn voiceless rows into voiced ones. **Dakuten** (濁点) is the two-stroke mark ゛; **handakuten** (半濁点) is the small circle ゜and applies only to the `h` row.

| Base row | Becomes | Hiragana | Katakana |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| k | **g** | が ぎ ぐ げ ご | ガ ギ グ ゲ ゴ |
| s | **z** | ざ じ ず ぜ ぞ | ザ ジ ズ ゼ ゾ |
| t | **d** | だ ぢ づ で ど | ダ ヂ ヅ デ ド |
| h | **b** | ば び ぶ べ ぼ | バ ビ ブ ベ ボ |
| h | **p** (handakuten) | ぱ ぴ ぷ ぺ ぽ | パ ピ プ ペ ポ |

Notes that matter:

- The `s`/`z` row is irregular in the same places as its base: じ is `ji` (not `zi`) and ず is `zu`.
- `h -> b` and `h -> p` looks arbitrary because it is historical - old Japanese `h` was closer to `p`. Just memorize は/ば/ぱ as three separate signs.
- These are **not optional extras**. が, で, ば and だ are in the top handful of most frequent kana in the language because they are grammar. Anyone who "finished kana" without them has not finished kana.

### Youon (digraphs)

A `-i` column kana plus a small ゃ, ゅ or ょ produces a single palatalized mora. The small kana is written at roughly half size and sits on the baseline - compare きゃ (one mora, `kya`) with きや (two mora, `ki-ya`).

| | `-ya` | `-yu` | `-yo` |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **k** | きゃ / キャ kya | きゅ / キュ kyu | きょ / キョ kyo |
| **s** | しゃ / シャ sha | しゅ / シュ shu | しょ / ショ sho |
| **t** | ちゃ / チャ cha | ちゅ / チュ chu | ちょ / チョ cho |
| **n** | にゃ / ニャ nya | にゅ / ニュ nyu | にょ / ニョ nyo |
| **h** | ひゃ / ヒャ hya | ひゅ / ヒュ hyu | ひょ / ヒョ hyo |
| **m** | みゃ / ミャ mya | みゅ / ミュ myu | みょ / ミョ myo |
| **r** | りゃ / リャ rya | りゅ / リュ ryu | りょ / リョ ryo |
| **g** | ぎゃ / ギャ gya | ぎゅ / ギュ gyu | ぎょ / ギョ gyo |
| **j** | じゃ / ジャ ja | じゅ / ジュ ju | じょ / ジョ jo |
| **b** | びゃ / ビャ bya | びゅ / ビュ byu | びょ / ビョ byo |
| **p** | ぴゃ / ピャ pya | ぴゅ / ピュ pyu | ぴょ / ピョ pyo |

11 rows x 3 = 33 digraphs per script. Add those 33 to the 46 base signs and 25 diacritic signs and you get **104 signs per script** - which is the honest number, not 46. (The kana deck in this folder has 232 cards across both scripts, including the obsolete pair and a few extras.)

High-frequency words that need these immediately: 東京 (とうきょう), 会社 (かいしゃ), 病院 (びょういん), 牛乳 (ぎゅうにゅう), ジャム, シャツ, チョコ.

### Long vowels

Vowel length is **phonemic** in Japanese - it changes the word, the way `b` and `p` do in English. A long vowel is exactly two mora: two beats on the same vowel, not a "stressed" vowel.

Hiragana marks length by writing a second vowel kana:

| Written | Sound | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| あ + あ | `aa` | おかあさん (mother) |
| い + い | `ii` | おいしい (delicious) |
| う + う | `uu` | ふうせん (balloon) |
| え + い | `ee` (usually) | せんせい (teacher), えいご (English) |
| え + え | `ee` (rare) | ええ (yeah), おねえさん (older sister) |
| お + う | `oo` (usually) | ありがとう, とうきょう, がっこう |
| お + お | `oo` (rare, fixed list) | おおきい (big), おおい (many), とおい (far), とお (ten), おおさか (Osaka) |

The えい/ええ and おう/おお pairs are the only real spelling traps in hiragana, and they are a leftover of the 1946 spelling reform (現代仮名遣い), which kept some historical spellings. Practical rule: **assume おう and えい; the おお and ええ words are a short memorizable list.**

Katakana does it differently - a long dash called **chouonpu** (長音符) ー, written in the direction of the text:

`ラーメン` (ramen), `コーヒー` (coffee), `コンピューター` (computer), `ケーキ` (cake), `スーパー` (supermarket)

Minimal pairs to drill until they are automatic:

| Short | Long |
| :--- | :--- |
| ビル (building) | ビール (beer) |
| おじさん (uncle) | おじいさん (grandfather) |
| おばさん (aunt) | おばあさん (grandmother) |
| ここ (here) | こうこう (high school) |

If you cannot hear these, you cannot yet hear Japanese. That is not a kana problem, it is a mora problem - see below.

### The small tsu (sokuon)

っ (hiragana) / ッ (katakana) is a half-size つ that makes **no sound of its own**. It doubles the following consonant and, more importantly, **takes up a full mora of time** - a held beat of silence before the consonant releases.

| Word | Reading | Contrast |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| きって (stamp) | ki-**t**-te (4 mora) | きて (come) - ki-te (2 mora) |
| さっか (author) | sa-**k**-ka | さか (slope) |
| がっこう (school) | ga-**k**-ko-u (4 mora) | - |
| ざっし (magazine) | za-**s**-shi | - |
| いっぱい (full / a lot) | i-**p**-pa-i | - |
| サッカー (soccer) | sa-**k**-ka-a | - |
| バッグ (bag) | ba-**g**-gu | バグ (bug) |

Rules: it never starts a word, and it never precedes a vowel or ん. When it *does* appear at the end of a word - common in manga - it marks an abrupt glottal stop: `あっ!`, `えっ?`.

English speakers systematically under-hold this. Say "book case", not "bookase" - the silence is the sound.

### ん is a mora, not a consonant

ん / ン is its own beat, and its actual pronunciation is decided by whatever follows it:

| Following sound | Realized as | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| t, d, n, r, z | `n` | かんたん (easy), みんな (everyone) |
| p, b, m | `m` | せんぱい (sounds like "sempai"), こんばんは, さんま |
| k, g | `ng` | りんご (apple), げんき |
| nothing (word end) or a vowel | nasal, uvular | ほん (book), でんわ |

Three consequences:

1. **にほん is three beats**: に-ほ-ん. Not two. This is the single most common rhythm error beginners make.
2. **ん never starts a word** - which is exactly why the word game しりとり ends when someone says a word ending in ん.
3. **You do not need to "pronounce" the variants.** Say ん as a nasal and let the following consonant pull it into place, the way it does naturally in "input" -> "imput".

### The particle irregulars

Three kana are read differently when they function as particles. This is historical spelling, preserved deliberately:

| Kana | Normal reading | As a particle | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| は | `ha` | **`wa`** | 私**は** (topic) - but はな (flower) is still `hana` |
| へ | `he` | **`e`** | 日本**へ** (direction) - but へや (room) is still `heya` |
| を | - | **`o`** | ごはん**を** (object) - only ever a particle |

The most common over-correction: learners decide は is "always wa". It is not. Only the particle. Note also that こんにちは and こんばんは end in the particle は and are therefore `konnichiwa` and `konbanwa`, and that では is `dewa` (and contracts to じゃ in speech).

### Duplicate sounds: じ/ぢ and ず/づ

In standard Tokyo Japanese, じ and ぢ are both `ji`, and ず and づ are both `zu`. Historically they were distinct; the merger is called 四つ仮名 (yotsugana). Modern spelling rules mean **you default to じ and ず**, and ぢ/づ only survive in two situations:

- **Within a repeated mora**: つづく (続く, to continue), ちぢむ (縮む, to shrink).
- **Voicing at a compound boundary** (連濁): 三日月 みか**づ**き (crescent moon, from つき), 鼻血 はな**ぢ** (nosebleed, from ち).

Do not lose sleep on this - just know that seeing づ does not mean a new sound. It does matter for typing: a Japanese IME maps `du` to づ and `zu` to ず, so 続く is typed `tsuduku`, not `tsuzuku`. IME behaviour in general belongs to [Writing](../Writing/readme.md).

### Obsolete and extended kana

**Obsolete (pre-1946):** ゐ / ヰ (`wi`) and ゑ / ヱ (`we`). Removed by the spelling reform, but they survive in pre-war texts, deliberately archaic writing, and brand names - the most famous being ヱビス (Yebisu) beer. The [Recognizing kana deck](<./Anki Decks/Recognizing kana.apkg>) in this folder includes all four; most other resources skip them. Learning them costs about four minutes, so learn them.

**Extended katakana** - combinations invented to approximate foreign sounds. These are not optional if you consume modern media:

| | | | | |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| ヴァ va | ヴィ vi | ヴ vu | ヴェ ve | ヴォ vo |
| ファ fa | フィ fi | | フェ fe | フォ fo |
| ティ ti | トゥ tu | ディ di | ドゥ du | |
| ウィ wi | ウェ we | ウォ wo | | |
| シェ she | ジェ je | チェ che | | |

Examples: ヴァイオリン / バイオリン (violin - both spellings exist), フィルム (film), パーティー (party), チェック (check), シェフ (chef), ウィスキー (whisky). The katakana chart at the top of this page lists the full extended set.

**Small vowel kana** ぁ ぃ ぅ ぇ ぉ / ァ ィ ゥ ェ ォ are the building blocks of those combinations, and in hiragana they also appear for stretched speech and dialect: あぁ, ちっちゃい, すげぇ.

### Mora vs syllable (why Japanese rhythm feels off)

A syllable is a vowel and its surrounding consonants. A **mora** is a unit of *timing*. Japanese counts mora, and each of the following is exactly one mora:

- any kana sign (か, し, ん)
- a digraph (きゃ counts as one, not two)
- the second half of a long vowel (the う in とう, the ー in カー)
- っ, the held silence
- ん

So:

| Word | Mora | Count | Syllables (English intuition) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| にほん | に-ほ-ん | 3 | 2 ("ni-hon") |
| とうきょう | と-う-きょ-う | 4 | 2 ("to-kyo") |
| がっこう | が-っ-こ-う | 4 | 2 ("gak-ko") |
| コンピューター | コ-ン-ピュ-ー-タ-ー | 6 | 3 ("com-pu-ter") |

Why you should care in week one:

1. **Japanese is mora-timed.** Every mora gets roughly equal duration. English is stress-timed and squashes unstressed syllables. If you read カー as one English beat you are producing a word no listener will parse cleanly, and worse, you will not *hear* the difference in native speech either.
2. **Pitch accent is assigned per mora**, so you cannot even begin on pitch until you count mora correctly. See [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md).
3. **Haiku counts mora, not syllables** - that famous 5-7-5 is mora.
4. Vowel length, っ and ん all stop being "weird spelling rules" and become obvious once you think in beats.

**Actionable drill:** when you read kana aloud during your first two weeks, tap the table once per kana. If your tapping hand and your mouth disagree, your mouth is wrong.

One caveat in the other direction: kana show you the *spelling*, not the *speech*. です is said closer to "des" and すき closer to "ski" because Japanese devoices `u` and `i` between voiceless consonants. That is phonetics, not kana - [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md) owns it. Just do not be alarmed when the audio does not match the chart.

## Look-alikes and how to separate them

Most kana pain after week one is visual discrimination, not memory. Attack these deliberately, in pairs, rather than hoping they resolve.

| Confusion | Discriminator |
| :--- | :--- |
| **ね / れ / わ** | Identical left stem; the answer is always in the bottom-right. Loop that curls back = **ね**. Straight kick outward = **れ**. Round closed bowl = **わ**. |
| **は / ほ** | Count the horizontal bars on the right: one = **は**, two = **ほ**. |
| **る / ろ** | Same stroke, but **る** finishes with a closed loop. Loop = `ru`. |
| **さ / き** | Count crossbars: one = **さ**, two = **き**. |
| **ぬ / め** | **ぬ** ends in a loop, **め** does not. (Same rule as る/ろ - loops are load-bearing in hiragana.) |
| **あ / お** | **あ** has a closed loop in the middle; **お** has a detached tick on the right. |
| **シ / ツ** | The reliable rule: **シ** has its short strokes entering from the **left** and its long stroke sweeps up to the right - just like し. **ツ** has its short strokes hanging from the **top** and its long stroke falls to the lower left - just like つ. |
| **ソ / ン** | Same rule, same families. **ソ** = strokes from the **top**, falling stroke (the そ/ツ family). **ン** = stroke from the **left**, rising stroke (the ん/シ family). |
| **ク / ワ** | **ク** has a sharp pointed corner and a straight diagonal cut. **ワ** is wide and open with a rounded shoulder and no cut. Also compare **タ** = ク with an extra internal stroke. |
| **ノ / メ** | **ノ** is one single stroke. **メ** is two strokes that cross. |
| **コ / ユ** | **コ** is a bracket, both strokes turning the same way. **ユ** has a long base bar running off to the right. |
| **ウ / ラ** | **ウ** has a tick on the roof; **ラ** has a bare top. |
| **ス / ヌ** | **ヌ** has a crossing stroke to the left; **ス** hangs its tail from the corner. |
| **チ / テ** | **チ** has a vertical stroke crossing through the bars; **テ**'s stroke hangs below them. |
| **マ / ム** | **マ** has a bent top with a diagonal inside; **ム** is a wide `<` with a separate tick. |

Three strategies that actually work:

1. **Drill confusing pairs adjacently, not randomly.** Random review lets you answer ね correctly by elimination. Put ね/れ/わ on screen together and force the discrimination.
2. **Vary the typeface.** Kana in a manga font, an anime subtitle font and a textbook font look meaningfully different. [Real Kana](https://realkana.com/) can quiz you across typefaces; the [Just Kana deck](<./Anki Decks/Just Kana.apkg>) in this folder ships the FG Kyokasho textbook-style kana font. Font choice in general is covered in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>).
3. **Write the pair once, by hand.** Not a handwriting program - just the confusable pair. Motor memory separates shapes that visual memory blurs. The [writing practice sheets](<./Hiragana/hiragana_writing_practice_sheets.pdf>) here are numbered per stroke and are the fastest way to do this.

## Reading katakana loanwords

You can know every katakana sign and still be unable to read カウンセリング. Decoding katakana into the word it is borrowing is a **second skill**, and it is the one that actually takes months. Front-load it.

### The transformation rules

Japanese has a small sound inventory, so every borrowed word gets reshaped predictably:

| Rule | Example |
| :--- | :--- |
| Every consonant needs a vowel (default `u`) | strike -> ス-ト-ラ-イ-ク (ストライク), milk -> ミルク |
| After `t`/`d`, the inserted vowel is `o` | test -> テスト, hand -> ハンド, bed -> ベッド |
| `l` and `r` both become ラ行 | light / right -> ライト (genuinely ambiguous) |
| `v` becomes バ行 (or ヴ) | video -> ビデオ, violin -> バイオリン / ヴァイオリン |
| `th` becomes サ行 / ザ行 | thank you -> サンキュー, marathon -> マラソン |
| Final `-t`, `-k`, `-p`, `-g` double with ッ | net -> ネット, book -> ブック, up -> アップ, bag -> バッグ |
| `-er` / `-or` / `-ar` become ー | computer -> コンピューター, elevator -> エレベーター, doctor -> ドクター, car -> カー |
| Stressed English vowels often lengthen | coffee -> コーヒー, cake -> ケーキ |

**Truncation** is the second half of the puzzle. Japanese clips long borrowings aggressively:

アパート (apartment), デパート (department store), テレビ (television), アニメ (animation), パソコン (personal computer), コンビニ (convenience store), リモコン (remote control), スマホ (smartphone), エアコン (air conditioner), ネット (internet).

### Not everything comes from English

This is the trap that makes learners think their decoding is broken:

| Word | Source | Meaning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| アルバイト | German *Arbeit* | part-time job |
| エネルギー | German *Energie* | energy |
| カルテ | German *Karte* | medical record |
| パン | Portuguese *pão* | bread |
| タバコ | Portuguese *tabaco* | cigarettes |
| イクラ | Russian *ikra* | salmon roe |
| ズボン | French *jupon* | trousers |
| アンケート | French *enquête* | survey |
| ピーマン | French *piment* | bell pepper |
| ランドセル | Dutch *ransel* | school backpack |

### False friends (和製英語)

Words that look English, decode cleanly, and mean something else:

| Katakana | Looks like | Actually means |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| マンション | mansion | apartment / condo |
| サラリーマン | salaryman | white-collar office worker |
| コンセント | consent | electrical outlet |
| ホッチキス | Hotchkiss | stapler |
| ガソリンスタンド | gasoline stand | gas station |
| バイキング | viking | buffet |
| ワンピース | one piece | a dress |
| カンニング | cunning | cheating on a test |
| スマート | smart | slim, trim |
| ナイーブ | naive | sensitive, delicate |
| ハイテンション | high tension | hyper, excited |
| クレーム | claim | a complaint |
| ダンボール | (nothing) | cardboard |
| サービス | service | free of charge, on the house |

### How to actually train it

- **Read it aloud.** Your ear recognizes the word far faster than your eye. サーヴィス looks like nonsense and sounds like "service". Reading katakana silently is the slow path.
- **Say it with Japanese rhythm.** Count the mora. If you compress コンピューター into English stress you lose the ー and the ン and the word stops being findable in your own memory.
- **Chunk at ー and ッ.** ショッピングモール -> ショッ / ピング / モール.
- **Use high-density sources.** Menus, convenience store shelves, game UI and character/attack names in anime are almost pure katakana. A restaurant menu is a 50-word katakana exam.
- **Match density to the target domain.** Katakana density varies sharply by field, and professional or technical domains are often the heaviest of all - Japanese tech work, for instance, runs on サーバー, データベース, リリース, テスト, デプロイ constantly. If a specific field is the eventual goal, its katakana vocabulary is domain vocabulary worth prioritising deliberately, not a beginner-level chore to get past.
- **Drill the real exercises**, not just characters. The [japanesepod101 practice worksheet](<./Practice Worksheets/Hiragana & Katakana PRACTICE WORKSHEET - japanesepod101.pdf>) in this folder ends with applied prompts ("how do you say and write *television* in Japanese?"), which is exactly this skill in the production direction.
- Tofugu's [Strange Katakana Words](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japanese/strange-katakana-words/index.html>) (offline in this repo) is a good tour of the weird end of the spectrum.

## Recognition, recall, and production

Three different skills, often conflated, with wildly different value:

| Skill | The task | Value in month 1 |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Recognition** | see き -> know it is `ki` | **Critical.** This is reading. |
| **Recall (sound -> sign)** | hear `ki` -> know it is き | **Useful.** Needed to type, to look words up, and to take notes. |
| **Production (handwriting)** | write き from memory with correct strokes | **Low.** Nice to have, costs 3-4x the time. |

Recognition does not imply recall. You will be able to read き weeks before you can produce it on demand - and that is fine, because typing gives you recall for free: the IME converts `ki` to き, so every sentence you type is a recall rep with instant feedback.

Production is a genuinely separate discipline and this guide deliberately deprioritizes it. The reason is simple: in the first year you are trying to build an input engine. Time spent perfecting your わ is time not spent reading. The counter-argument is real - handwriting is the strongest way to burn in the look-alike pairs, and if you sit a paper exam or study in a classroom you have no choice. Split the difference: hand-write the ~15 characters you keep confusing, skip the other 90. When you do want to do it properly, [Writing](../Writing/readme.md) owns stroke order, posture and practice regimes, and the tracing sheets in this folder are the kana-specific material.

## The schools of thought

### 1. Mnemonic-heavy (Tofugu style)

Every kana gets a picture-story: き is a key, ふ is Mount Fuji, ソ is a sewing needle. You learn the story, then the story fades and the character remains.

- **Pros:** fastest path to day-one recall with no prior exposure to non-Latin scripts. Free, and fully offline in this repo (four PDFs, ~240 pages combined). Handles the look-alikes head-on because the mnemonics are built to contrast. Includes the reading exercises that most chart-based resources omit.
- **Cons:** you are building scaffolding you must later discard, and the discard has a cost - a mnemonic detour is an extra hop between sign and sound. Some mnemonics are a stretch and you will end up remembering the story but not which character it belongs to. Slower per character than brute force.
- **Best for:** absolute beginners, people who bounce off rote repetition, anyone who has never learned a non-Latin script.

### 2. Brute-force SRS (Tatsumoto's two-day method)

Import a kana deck, crank the new-cards limit, and grind. No mnemonics by design. Tatsumoto's [Learning Kana In Two Days](<../General content/Tatsumoto Blog offline copy/tatsumoto.neocities.org/blog/learning-kana-in-two-days.html>) is explicit about it: he "specifically [does] not provide nor recommend using any mnemonics", on the grounds that kana is easy enough that effort beats cleverness.

- **Pros:** no scaffolding to unlearn - you build sign -> sound directly. It is the same tool (Anki) you will use for years, so the setup cost is paid once. With audio on the cards you learn the sound system and the signs simultaneously. Realistically 2-5 days.
- **Cons:** high early error rate feels demoralizing, and some people genuinely stall. Requires Anki installed and understood on day 1. Teaches nothing about writing. If your deck is romaji-backed rather than audio-backed, you can end up with a romaji habit instead of a kana habit.
- **Best for:** adults with SRS experience, self-directed learners, anyone who is already committed to an immersion approach.

### 3. Writing-based drilling

Tracing sheets, stroke by stroke, five characters per page, until your hand knows them.

- **Pros:** motor memory is extremely durable and is the single best cure for the look-alike pairs. You learn stroke order correctly the first time, which is nearly impossible to retrofit. Screen-free.
- **Cons:** slow - two to four weeks rather than one. You are investing heavily in the lowest-value of the three skills. Easy to mistake neat handwriting for reading fluency; plenty of people can write beautiful かな and still read at a crawl.
- **Best for:** classroom learners, people who will sit handwritten exams, and anyone who simply enjoys it enough that it keeps them studying.

### 4. Gamified apps and browser drills

Duolingo, Dr. Moku, [Real Kana](https://realkana.com/), [DJT Kana](<../General content/djtguide.neocities.org/kana/index.html>), [Kakugo](https://www.f-droid.org/en/packages/org.kaqui/), [The Kana Quiz](https://f-droid.org/packages/com.noprestige.kanaquiz/), [Tofugu's kana quiz](https://kana-quiz.tofugu.com).

- **Pros:** zero setup, phone-friendly, free, and they turn dead time into reps. The good ones (DJT Kana, Real Kana) let you select exactly which rows to drill, which is the right granularity for attacking your weak spots.
- **Cons:** multiple-choice recognition in a narrow, familiar context is much easier than reading in the wild - the score flatters you. Gamification optimizes streaks over coverage, and Duolingo in particular drip-feeds kana across weeks when it is a one-week job. Most of them never touch loanword decoding.
- **Best for:** supplementary drilling (excellent), and specifically for targeted look-alike work. Not as your primary path.

### 5. Incidental / learn-it-from-words

Skip isolated kana study. Start on vocabulary or sentences with furigana and audio, and let the kana come along for the ride.

- **Pros:** no "pre-study" phase, and every rep is a real word, so it is never wasted. It is roughly how Japanese children do it.
- **Cons:** by far the slowest to fluency. It leaves systematic holes - rare digraphs, ぢ/づ, the whole katakana script - because frequency does not cover everything. You spend months reading slowly, which slows down *all* your other study.
- **Best for:** nobody as a primary method. **Excellent as a consolidation method** once you are at ~90% recognition, which is exactly what phase 5 below does.

### The two-day speedrun vs the two-week pace

The disagreement is less real than it looks. Both camps arrive at the same place in about a month. What differs is where the work sits.

The speedrun claim is true but oversold: after two days you will recognize most kana **with visible effort**, not fluently. Speed is not included. The two-week camp is not slower to fluency, it is slower to the *starting line* - and it carries a specific danger: people who take two weeks tend to treat the end of the chart as the end of the task, stop drilling, and never reach automaticity.

## Recommended path (opinionated)

**Audio-first recognition SRS, both scripts inside one week, no writing, then retire the deck and let reading finish the job.**

Concretely: import [Recognizing kana.apkg](<./Anki Decks/Recognizing kana.apkg>) from this folder (232 cards, native audio from TAAS and NHK recordings, front = the kana, back = script + romaji + audio). Run hiragana for three days, then unsuspend katakana. Keep a printed [chart](<./Hiragana/hiragana_chart.pdf>) nearby for the first week. Use [DJT Kana](<../General content/djtguide.neocities.org/kana/index.html>) for ten minutes a day of row-targeted drilling. Reach for Tofugu's mnemonics **only** for the handful of characters that refuse to stick after three days. Write nothing except the look-alike pairs that keep getting missed. Retire the deck at the end of week 3 and let sentence reading do the rest.

**Why this and not the alternatives:**
- **Audio on every card** means building sign -> sound directly. Romaji-backed decks build sign -> romaji -> sound, and that middle hop is sticky and hard to remove later. This is the single highest-leverage detail in the whole plan.
- **Both scripts in the same week** because the katakana trap is the actual failure mode, and because the interference between シ/ツ and ソ/ン is a *feature* - it forces discrimination now rather than in month six.
- **Mnemonics as a rescue tool, not a system.** For ~90 of the 104 signs per script, raw exposure is faster than building and then dismantling a story. For シ/ツ/ソ/ン and ね/れ/わ, a mnemonic earns its keep.
- **No writing**, because production is the low-value skill (see above), and time spent on it competes directly with immersion for the same 60-90 minutes. A kana writing deck stacked on top of an existing reading, vocabulary and kanji rotation is one more queue competing for that time, and it loses that competition easily.
- **Retire the deck deliberately.** Tatsumoto's advice to delete the kana deck once you move to sentences is correct. Reading sentences *is* kana review, and a permanent kana deck is a permanent tax.

**This does not apply if:**
- **SRS itself is the obstacle.** If a screen full of unknown characters and a 40% fail rate is enough to make someone quit, the mnemonic route ([Tofugu's guide](<./Hiragana/Learn Hiragana_ Tofugu's Ultimate Guide.pdf>) + the [workbook](<./Hiragana/tofugu-learn-hiragana-book.pdf>)) gets there just as well and is the better plan, because it is the plan that actually gets finished.
- **A classroom demands handwriting in week 1.** Then do the [writing sheets](<./Hiragana/hiragana_writing_practice_sheets.pdf>) - there is no choice there, and the motor memory is a real consolation prize.
- **This is a first non-Latin script.** Mnemonics genuinely help more when every shape is equally alien.
- **The learner is a child, or being taught by one.** Mnemonics and games, obviously.

## Phase-by-phase plan

### Phase 0 - Setup

- **Goal:** remove every reason to not start tomorrow.
- **Time:** 30-45 minutes, once.
- **Do:**
  1. Install Anki and import both decks from this folder. Anki installation, FSRS and deck settings are covered in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) - do not improvise them here.
  2. Set the kana deck to ~40 new cards/day and unlimited reviews. This deck is a sprint; normal SRS restraint does not apply.
  3. In the Anki browser, search the deck for `tag:katakana` and suspend those notes. You will unsuspend them on day 4.
  4. Print [hiragana_chart.pdf](<./Hiragana/hiragana_chart.pdf>) and [katakana_chart.pdf](<./Katakana/katakana_chart.pdf>). Physically. Put them where you eat.
  5. Bookmark [DJT Kana](<../General content/djtguide.neocities.org/kana/index.html>) (works offline from this repo).
  6. Decide, out loud, that you are done with romaji. See [Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md).
- **Done when:** the kana deck shows ~108 unsuspended cards due and a chart is on your wall.

### Phase 1 - Hiragana recognition

- **Goal:** every hiragana sign, including dakuten and digraphs, recognized with effort.
- **Time:** days 1-3, about 40 minutes/day.
- **Daily routine:** 40 new cards + all reviews in Anki (~25 min), then 10 min of DJT Kana with only the rows you have seen checked, then one timed chart read-through aloud.
- **Materials:** [Recognizing kana.apkg](<./Anki Decks/Recognizing kana.apkg>), [hiragana_chart.pdf](<./Hiragana/hiragana_chart.pdf>), [DJT Kana](<../General content/djtguide.neocities.org/kana/index.html>). Optional 64-minute video walkthrough: [Learn ALL Hiragana in 1 Hour](<./Hiragana/Learn ALL Hiragana in 1 Hour - How to Write and Read Japanese/video.mp4>). Stuck characters: [Tofugu hiragana guide](<./Hiragana/Learn Hiragana_ Tofugu's Ultimate Guide.pdf>).
- **Done when:** you can read the full hiragana chart aloud - gojuuon, dakuten, digraphs - in **under 90 seconds with at most 2 errors**, and hit **>=90%** on a 50-item DJT Kana hiragana round.

### Phase 2 - Katakana recognition

- **Goal:** the same, for katakana, while hiragana is still fresh enough to cause productive interference.
- **Time:** days 4-7, about 45 minutes/day.
- **Daily routine:** unsuspend the katakana notes on day 4. 40 new + all reviews (~30 min). 10 min DJT Kana with **katakana rows only**. One timed katakana chart read.
- **Materials:** same deck, [katakana_chart.pdf](<./Katakana/katakana_chart.pdf>), [Learn ALL Katakana in 1 Hour](<./Katakana/Learn ALL Katakana in 1 Hour - How to Write and Read Japanese/video.mp4>) (~61 min), [Tofugu katakana guide](<./Katakana/Learn Katakana_ The Ultimate Guide.pdf>) for the シ/ツ/ソ/ン mnemonics.
- **Done when:** full katakana chart aloud in **under 120 seconds with at most 2 errors** (katakana is legitimately slower - do not panic), and you can correctly separate シ/ツ and ソ/ン **10 times out of 10** in a shuffled set.

### Phase 3 - The parts everyone skips

- **Goal:** digraphs, long vowels, っ and ん stop being "rules" and become sounds you hear.
- **Time:** week 2, 20-25 min/day on top of reviews.
- **Daily routine:** reviews only in Anki (no new cards - the deck is done). Then 10 minutes reading mixed kana words aloud while tapping one beat per mora, and 5 minutes of minimal-pair listening (ビル/ビール, きて/きって, おじさん/おじいさん).
- **Materials:** the tables in this guide; [hiragana & katakana practice worksheet](<./Practice Worksheets/Hiragana & Katakana PRACTICE WORKSHEET - japanesepod101.pdf>) (52 pages, both scripts, ending in applied exercises); the [Tofugu workbooks](<./Hiragana/tofugu-learn-hiragana-book.pdf>) if you want the cumulative reading quizzes at the back.
- **Done when:** you can read these aloud cold, with correct beat counts, no hesitation over 2 seconds each: ぎゅうにゅう、しゅっぱつ、きっぷ、でんわ、おおきい、せんせい、サッカー、コーヒー、ジャージ、コンピューター.

### Phase 4 - Loanword decoding

- **Goal:** katakana word -> meaning, not just katakana sign -> sound.
- **Time:** weeks 2-4, 10-15 min/day, then ongoing forever.
- **Daily routine:** 20 katakana words a day from a real source - a menu, a game UI, a product label, anime character and attack names. Read aloud, guess the source word, then check in a dictionary. Log every miss.
- **Materials:** the [transformation rules and false-friend tables](#reading-katakana-loanwords) above; [Strange Katakana Words](<../General content/Tofugu Website offline copy/www.tofugu.com/japanese/strange-katakana-words/index.html>); any Japanese menu or storefront.
- **Done when:** given 25 unseen katakana words including at least 3 和製英語, you get **20 or more** right within 5 seconds each.

### Phase 5 - Automaticity through reading

- **Goal:** stop decoding, start reading. This is the phase that actually matters and the one people skip.
- **Time:** weeks 3-8, folded into your normal study - no separate kana time.
- **Daily routine:** retire the kana deck (suspend or delete it - reading sentences is now your kana review). Read kana-heavy material aloud daily: your sentence deck, furigana manga, NHK Easy, graded readers. Grammar study starts here in parallel - see [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md).
- **Materials:** [Reading](../Reading/readme.md) for graded material, [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) for deck choice, [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md) for immersion scheduling and sentence mining.
- **Done when:** you read a page of easy Japanese aloud and stall only on **kanji**, never on kana - and afterwards you can say what the page was about, which means you were reading rather than decoding.

### Phase 6 (optional, much later) - Production

- **Goal:** write both scripts from memory with correct stroke order.
- **Time:** whenever you actually need it - month 6, year 2, or never.
- **Do:** the [hiragana](<./Hiragana/hiragana_writing_practice_sheets.pdf>) and [katakana](<./Katakana/katakana_writing_practice_sheets.pdf>) practice sheets (10 pages each, 5 characters per page, numbered strokes, plus a link to the source site's stroke-order animations). Tatsumoto's [kana Anki decks](<../General content/Tatsumoto Blog offline copy/tatsumoto.neocities.org/blog/kana-anki-decks.html>) page describes the matching production deck if you want it SRS'd.
- **Done when:** you can write all 46 base signs of both scripts from dictation without a chart.
- **Note:** handwriting as a discipline belongs to [Writing](../Writing/readme.md). Do not build a handwriting habit from this page alone.

## Common pitfalls

**1. The katakana trap.** You learn hiragana properly, then half-learn katakana, then spend a year sounding out ス-ト-ラ-イ-ク one sign at a time.
*Why it feels right:* every beginner resource is hiragana-heavy, so for the first month the cost is invisible. Katakana genuinely feels less urgent because it is less frequent in textbook sentences.
*The fix:* both scripts in the same week, non-negotiable. Then run katakana-only drill rounds (DJT Kana lets you uncheck all hiragana rows). Then treat loanword decoding as its own phase. And when you immerse, read every katakana word aloud instead of skipping it - skipping is how the hole gets dug.

**2. The romaji crutch.** Your cards say `ki`, your notes say `ki`, and six weeks in you are still translating き -> `ki` -> sound.
*Why it feels right:* romaji makes the first three days dramatically more comfortable.
*The fix:* audio-backed cards from day 1. Romaji as an *input* method (typing answers into DJT Kana) is fine; romaji as a *reading* representation is not. See [Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md).

**3. Reciting the chart and calling it recognition.** You can read あいうえお かきくけこ fluently because the *order* is cueing you, not the shapes.
*Why it feels right:* it produces the feeling of fluency and a nice fast time.
*The fix:* always test in random order. Shuffled app rounds, not grid recitation. If your chart time is great and your shuffled accuracy is bad, you have memorized a song.

**4. Treating the chart as the finish line.** "Finished kana" on day 7, drilling stops, and by week 4 the rare digraphs and half the katakana are gone.
*Why it feels right:* it is the only finishable task in your first year and finishing it feels great.
*The fix:* the kana deck is retired *by reading*, not by completion. Phase 5 is the real end of kana study.

**5. Ignoring vowel length, っ and ん.** You read おじさん and おじいさん as the same word and wonder why listening is impossible.
*Why it feels right:* your native-language ear normalizes duration away - it is not phonemic in English, so your brain discards it.
*The fix:* tap one beat per mora while reading aloud, for two weeks, until it is automatic. Drill minimal pairs explicitly. Count にほん as three.

**6. Writing before reading.** Four weeks of beautiful tracing sheets and you still read at a crawl.
*Why it feels right:* school taught you that writing something out *is* learning it, and tracing feels productive and calm.
*The fix:* postpone production to phase 6. Hand-write only the look-alike pairs you actually fail. [Writing](../Writing/readme.md) when you are ready.

**7. Over-correcting the particle irregulars.** You learn は = `wa` and start reading はな as `wana`.
*Why it feels right:* the rule was presented as a property of the character rather than of its grammatical role.
*The fix:* memorize it as "the topic particle は is `wa`", not "は is `wa`". Same for へ. を is the only one that is always the particle.

**8. Living in an app because the app says you are 68% done.** Gamified progress bars measure engagement, not coverage.
*Why it feels right:* the bar goes up daily and streaks are addictive.
*The fix:* measure with an external test (see below). If your streak is 30 days and you cannot read a menu, the streak is not measuring anything you want.

**9. Skipping dakuten and digraphs as "not real kana".** が, で, ば, しょ and きゅ are not edge cases; they are grammar and core vocabulary.
*Why it feels right:* the 46-character chart looks complete, and the extras look like accessories.
*The fix:* count 104 per script, not 46, and make sure your deck includes them. The one in this folder does.

**10. Waiting for kana mastery before starting anything else.** Six weeks of pure kana study while grammar and vocabulary wait.
*Why it feels right:* sequential learning feels tidy and safe.
*The fix:* start [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md) and [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) at ~90% recognition, around day 8. Your kana will improve *faster* because you will be reading real sentences.

## Measuring progress

Self-tests with numbers, run at day 3, day 7, day 14 and day 30. Log the results - the trend is the point.

| Test | How to run it | Target |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Chart sprint** | Read a full chart aloud, timed, then repeat in shuffled order | Hiragana <90s, katakana <120s, mixed <150s, <=2 errors |
| **Shuffled recognition** | [DJT Kana](<../General content/djtguide.neocities.org/kana/index.html>) with all rows checked, 100 items | >=95% accuracy, <2s per item |
| **Look-alike gauntlet** | 30 items drawn only from シ/ツ, ソ/ン, ク/ワ/タ, ノ/メ, コ/ユ, ね/れ/わ, は/ほ, る/ろ, さ/き | 30/30 |
| **Transcription (sound -> sign)** | Have a native audio source or TTS say 20 random words; write them in kana | >=18/20, length and っ correct |
| **Mora count** | Say 10 words aloud while tapping; check against the written kana | 10/10 beat counts correct |
| **Loanword decode** | 25 unseen katakana words, >=3 of them 和製英語 | >=20 correct in <5s each |
| **Anki retention** | Deck stats on the kana deck | >=90% true retention before you retire it |
| **The reading test** | Read a page of NHK Easy or a furigana manga page aloud | Zero kana stalls; you can summarize the page afterwards |

The last one is the only test that matters in the end, and it is qualitative in one specific way: **notice whether you retained meaning or only sound.** If you finish a paragraph knowing what it said, you were reading. If you finish it having pronounced 200 mora and remembering none of them, you were still decoding - keep going, that is normal at week 3, and it is the exact reason phase 5 exists.

## Where to go next

Kana is a one-week task with a three-week tail. Start these the moment you hit ~90% recognition - around day 8, not day 30:

- **[Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md)** - the immediate next step. Tae Kim's guide is readable the day you finish hiragana.
- **[Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md)** - your first real Anki deck, and the thing that will quietly finish your kana training for you.
- **[Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md)** - radicals, readings, and the third script. Kana first, always.
- **[Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md)** - the romanization systems, and why you are now done with them.
- **[Writing](../Writing/readme.md)** - stroke order as a discipline, handwriting practice, and IME typing (which is kana-based, so this is where the ぢ/づ typing question gets answered).
- **[Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md)** - pitch accent, which kana do not encode, plus vowel devoicing and the rest of the phonetics your chart hid from you.
- **[Reading](../Reading/readme.md)** - graded readers, NHK Easy and furigana manga: the material that turns decoding into reading.
- **[Listening](../Listening/readme.md)** - where your new mora sense gets tested against real speech.
- **[AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md)** - immersion scheduling, sentence mining, and media sourcing.
- **[General content](<../General content/readme.md>)** - Anki setup, FSRS, add-ons, dictionaries, Yomitan and fonts. Read the Anki section before phase 0.
