# AJATT - Immersion, Sentence Mining and Environment Engineering

<img src="../../Assets/AJATT-logo.jpg" alt="AJATT" height="320"/>

> **TL;DR**
>
> - **What it is:** AJATT ("All Japanese All The Time") is the immersion-first school of Japanese learning - you acquire the language by consuming enormous amounts of native Japanese, and you use SRS flashcards only as a crutch to make that consumption comprehensible.
> - **Why it matters:** every modern Japanese guide in this folder (TheMoeWay, Animecards, Donkuri/saegusa, Perdition, DJT) is a descendant of it. If you understand AJATT and its critiques, you understand the whole map.
> - **When to start:** immersion starts on day 1 - badly, uncomprehendingly, and that is fine. **Sentence mining** starts much later: after kana, after ~1,000-1,500 core words, after basic grammar. Mining too early is the single most common beginner mistake.
> - **Recommended path:** a _hybrid_ - Donkuri/saegusa's tool setup + TheMoeWay's mindset + Animecards-style word cards, deliberately breaking AJATT's "no English ever" rule for the first 6 months, and doing output early instead of postponing it for years.
> - **Read the critiques section before you commit.** AJATT works, but it is expensive in hours and has a real burnout and "10,000 hours and still can't speak" failure mode.

## What this is and why it matters

**AJATT** stands for _All Japanese All The Time_. It is the method and, more importantly, the mindset that came out of Khatzumoto's blog [All Japanese All The Time](https://learnjapanese.link/ajatt), written starting in the mid-2000s. Khatzumoto was an American engineer who claimed to have taken himself from zero to a professional working level of Japanese in roughly 18 months while living in the United States, and then got hired into a Japanese company. That claim is the origin story the entire community is built on. Treat it as a claim, not a measurement - see [The honest critiques](#the-honest-critiques).

The method rests on five core assertions:

1. **You do not study a language, you acquire it.** Explicit knowledge about Japanese (conjugation tables, grammar terminology, JLPT vocabulary lists) is not the same thing as the ability to understand and produce Japanese. You get the second one from _contact with the language_, not from knowing facts about it.
2. **Volume beats method.** Given two learners, the one with 4,000 hours of contact beats the one with 400 hours, almost regardless of what technique either used. So the highest-leverage question is not "what is optimal?" but "what will you actually do for thousands of hours?"
3. **Environment engineering.** Willpower is unreliable; environments are reliable. So you _change your environment_ rather than relying on discipline: phone and OS in Japanese, Japanese audio playing by default, Japanese media as your default entertainment, English media made inconvenient. You do not "find time to study Japanese" - you arrange your life so that doing nothing produces Japanese input.
4. **No English** (the dogmatic version: _never_ English about Japanese; no English subtitles, no English translations, no English grammar explanations after the first few weeks, monolingual J-J dictionaries as soon as possible).
5. **Fun is a technical requirement, not a luxury.** Because compliance over thousands of hours is the binding constraint, enjoyment is not soft - it is the mechanism that makes the hours happen. This is why the community is built on anime, manga and visual novels rather than on NHK news.

Historically, AJATT arrived at the moment when three things became available at once: cheap spaced-repetition software (Anki, 2006), unlimited access to native media over the internet, and a community that shared subtitle files and Anki decks. It was the first widely-read articulation of "throw away the textbook, consume native media, use an SRS to plug the gaps". Everything in this folder descends from that.

The reason this guide sits at the top of the folder rather than buried in it: AJATT is not a _resource_, it is the **operating system** the other guides run on. Kanji, Vocabulary, Grammar, Reading and Listening each own a skill. AJATT owns the schedule, the media pipeline and the card-creation loop that feeds all of them.

**What this guide owns:** immersion strategy and scheduling, active vs passive immersion, media sourcing and selection, sentence mining end to end, and progress tracking.

**What it does not own:** how to configure Anki, FSRS, add-ons, Yomitan and dictionaries lives in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>); pitch accent and output technique live in [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md); reading and listening content ladders live in [Reading](../Reading/readme.md) and [Listening](../Listening/readme.md).

## Where it fits in the journey

**Prerequisites before immersion becomes useful (not before it starts):**

| Prerequisite                            | Why                                                                          | Owner                                             |
| :-------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------ |
| Kana, read fluently                     | Every lookup tool, subtitle and dictionary entry assumes it. Non-negotiable. | [Kana](../Kana/readme.md)                         |
| Drop romaji entirely                    | Romaji actively blocks reading development.                                  | [Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md)                     |
| Anki + Yomitan installed and configured | Mining is impossible without them.                                           | [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) |
| ~1,000-1,500 core words                 | Below this, native media is noise, not input.                                | [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md)             |
| Basic grammar read through once         | You need to recognize particles and conjugations to parse a sentence at all. | [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md)                   |

**Note the split.** _Passive and low-comprehension active immersion starts on day 1_ - it costs nothing, builds the habit, and trains your ear on Japanese phonology before you can parse a word. _Sentence mining_ requires the whole prerequisite stack above. Conflating these two is where most beginners go wrong.

**What AJATT unlocks:**

- The ability to learn Japanese _from_ Japanese, which removes the ceiling that textbook-bound learners hit around N3.
- A self-feeding loop: your immersion generates your flashcards, your flashcards raise your comprehension, higher comprehension makes immersion more enjoyable, which produces more immersion. This is the whole engine.
- Entertainment and study become the same activity, which is the only reason thousands of hours is survivable for a working adult.
- Eventually: the monolingual transition (using J-J dictionaries), which is where "learning Japanese" stops being a project and becomes a normal part of life.

## The schools of thought

AJATT is a family tree, not a single method. Here is the lineage, with the honest caveat that community history is poorly documented and partly oral - uncertain points are flagged as such below.

### The theory underneath: Krashen's comprehensible input

Before the family tree, the academic root. Stephen Krashen's Input Hypothesis (1980s) argues that language is acquired by understanding messages slightly above your current level - the famous **i+1** - and that conscious grammar study produces "learned" knowledge which does not readily convert into fluent use. AJATT is, in effect, Krashen taken literally and pushed to its practical extreme.

This matters in both directions. It gives immersion learning a real theoretical spine. It is also frequently over-claimed: Krashen's hypotheses are contested in second-language-acquisition research, the "affective filter" and "monitor" constructs are hard to test, and Krashen himself did **not** claim that output is harmful - only that _forced_ early output is unhelpful. The Donkuri guide is unusually honest about this, pointing out that "the myth that output is bad has nothing to do with Krashen's input hypothesis". Anyone who tells you "Krashen proved you should never speak for two years" is misrepresenting him.

### 1. Classic AJATT (Khatzumoto, mid-2000s onward)

The original. Kanji first via Heisig's _Remembering the Kanji_, then a target of **10,000 sentences** in an SRS, then all-Japanese everything, forever. Heavy on mindset writing, timeboxing, and "make it easy, make it fun" psychology. The blog's real lasting contribution is not its curriculum - it is the attitude reset. TheMoeWay's own resource sheet puts it exactly this way: AJATT "is still popular to this day... because of the mindset building that it was able to achieve."

- **Pros:** the mindset content is still unmatched; environment engineering is a genuinely powerful idea; it correctly identified volume as the dominant variable years before anyone else.
- **Cons:** curriculum is dated (full RTK, 10,000 sentence cards, pre-Yomitan tooling); the "no English ever" rule is stated dogmatically and is actively harmful for a day-1 beginner; the site later became entangled with paid products; the success story is a single unreplicated case.
- **Best for:** reading for motivation and philosophy. Not for following as a syllabus today.

### 2. MIA - Mass Immersion Approach (Matt Bonder / "Matt vs Japan", circa 2018-2020)

MIA was the first serious attempt to turn AJATT's blog posts into a structured, staged program. It systematized the stages, pushed **Recognition RTK** (kanji meanings only, no writing), formalized the **monolingual transition**, produced Anki add-ons and tooling, and - its most distinctive contribution - put **pitch accent** at the center of the method. MIA also popularized **condensed audio** (an anime episode's dialogue with silence and music stripped out, for passive listening). MIA's own site is effectively defunct; you can still find its fingerprints in the community, for instance the "MIA_Condensed_Audio" archives linked from TheMoeWay's resource sheet.

- **Pros:** real structure where AJATT had vibes; pitch accent awareness was a genuine advance; excellent tooling for its era.
- **Cons:** maximalist and brittle - full Recognition RTK plus pitch-accent perfectionism plus monolingual-ASAP is a lot of gates before you get to read anything fun; the emphasis on never "damaging" your Japanese with early output produced a generation of learners terrified to speak.
- **Best for:** historical understanding, and the specific ideas (Recognition RTK, condensed audio, monolingual transition) that survived it.

### 3. Refold (2020 onward)

Refold ([refold.la](https://refold.la/)) is the language-agnostic rebuild of MIA, with a staged roadmap (Stage 0 through Stage 4) and a concept vocabulary the community still uses - most usefully **domains** (narrow your immersion to one content area, like slice-of-life anime, so vocabulary repeats and comprehension rises fast). Perdition's guide cites [Refold's domain concept](https://refold.la/roadmap/stage-2/a/domains) directly.

- **Pros:** the clearest, most readable staged roadmap in the family; domain theory is genuinely useful; language-agnostic, so it generalizes.
- **Cons:** generic by design, so it is thin on Japanese-specific tooling; commercial layer (paid tiers, Discord ecosystem); the roadmap's hour estimates per stage are community estimates presented with more confidence than the underlying data supports.
- **Best for:** understanding the _shape_ of the journey, and for learners who need a named stage to tell them where they are.

### 4. Tatsumoto / Animecards (circa 2020 onward)

Two related Japanese-specific projects. Tatsumoto Ren's site ([tatsumoto.neocities.org](https://tatsumoto.neocities.org/)) pushes aggressive speedrun-style guides ("learning kana in two days", "learning kanji in two weeks") and a heavily Linux-flavoured tooling stack. [animecards.site](https://animecards.site/) - mirrored in this folder and currently maintained by [friedrich-de](https://github.com/friedrich-de/AnimecardsWebsite) - carries the family's most important _technical_ argument: **word cards beat sentence cards**.

The Animecards argument, from `docs/ankicards.md` in the mirror, is worth stating precisely, because it is the single most actionable idea in this entire folder:

- A **sentence card** shows the full sentence on the front. A **word card** (or "anime card") shows only the target word on the front, with reading, definition, audio, screenshot and the context sentence on the back.
- Word cards review **2 to 4 times faster** by their review data, which converts directly into more immersion time.
- Sentence cards are often _too easy_: you memorize the context, recognize the scene, and pass the card without actually knowing the word.
- Sentence cards are also _too complex_: you are simultaneously testing your reading, your parsing, your grammar and your vocabulary, which causes decision fatigue about whether to press Good or Again.

- **Pros:** the card-format argument is empirically grounded and persuasive; excellent concrete mining workflows (mpv scripts, asbplayer, ShareX, subtitle resyncing); free, no upsell.
- **Cons:** terse and assumes technical competence; historically Linux-centric in places; the tone can be dismissive of alternatives.
- **Best for:** anyone who has decided to mine and needs the actual pipeline. This is the most useful _technical_ member of the family.

### 5. TheMoeWay (shoui, circa 2020 onward)

[learnjapanese.moe](https://learnjapanese.moe/) is the big friendly community guide: a full beginner guide, UsagiSpoon's day-by-day [30 Day Japanese](https://learnjapanese.moe/routine/) routine, a monolingual-transition guide, a resource sheet, and a roughly 33,000-character FAQ answering about 120 real learner questions. Its most valuable trait is that it argues _against_ parts of its own lineage. shoui explicitly rejects output-delay dogma: "I do not believe it is a smart idea to delay your output until you are 'really good'... All of this is a product of a toxic perfectionist mindset that hurts immersion learning."

- **Pros:** most beginner-friendly entry point in the family; UsagiSpoon's 30-day routine is the best "what do I literally do tomorrow" document that exists; the FAQ is a genuine encyclopedia of failure modes; intellectually honest about passive immersion being weak and about sleep-learning being nonsense ("It's BS.").
- **Cons:** sprawling and occasionally self-contradictory across pages; some pages are stale (Yomichan rather than Yomitan); strong opinions stated as fact - the 7:3 listening-to-reading ratio, for example, is a personal heuristic, not a finding.
- **Best for:** beginners, and for anyone who needs a second opinion on a specific question.

### 6. DJT / Itazuraneko

Grown out of the _Daily Japanese Thread_ imageboard community rather than from a single author. Its legacy is infrastructure rather than method: the [DJT guide](https://djtguide.github.io/learn/guide.html), the [Itazuraneko library](https://djtguide.github.io/library/librarymain.html) of Japanese books, the [DJT kana game](https://djtguide.neocities.org/kana/), difficulty-ordered VN charts, and the Core2.3k deck that was eventually absorbed into Kaishi 1.5k. Domains and ownership have moved repeatedly over the years, so treat any specific URL as potentially stale.

- **Pros:** unmatched library and tooling; anonymous, free, no product.
- **Cons:** no editorial coherence; hostile tone; constant link rot; you have to already know what you are looking for.
- **Best for:** finding _material_ once you know what you want.

### 7. The current mainstream: Donkuri / saegusa, and Perdition

[Donkuri's Immersion-Based Japanese Learning](https://donkuri.github.io/learn-japanese/) (by "kuri", who also writes as saegusa - the two sites cross-reference each other and share authorship of the tooling) is, as of now, the most polished and least dogmatic guide in the family. The same author co-created the **Kaishi 1.5k** core deck and the **Lapis** mining note type, which makes this guide's recommendations and its tools one coherent stack. [Perdition](https://perdition-japanese.github.io/about/) adds the other missing piece - structure and measurement - via a curriculum of concrete named steps and a **character-count ladder** with Discord roles from 100k up to 100M characters consumed.

- **Pros:** Donkuri is calm, current, well-written, and explicitly anti-dogma ("it is not 'cheating' to use textbooks, or to study kanji in isolation"); the Kaishi + Yomitan + Lapis stack is the current community default and it works; Perdition's character ladder turns "am I making progress?" into a number.
- **Cons:** Donkuri has a personal visual-novel bias that may not match your taste; Perdition's linear-characters-to-ability model is a useful fiction, not a fact, and it ignores listening; Perdition's curriculum is prescriptive about which anime to watch, which fights the enjoyment principle.
- **Best for:** Donkuri as your primary syllabus and setup guide. Perdition as your tracking and structure layer.

## The honest critiques

This section is mandatory reading, written so that you can reasonably decide **against** AJATT.

**1. The time cost is enormous and usually understated.** The honest community answer to "how long?" is TheMoeWay's: "As much as you can. The more Japanese you do, the more gains you get." That is not a plan, it is an admission. Realistically you are looking at multiple thousands of hours of contact to reach a level where you can work in Japanese. At 90 minutes of active study plus 2 hours of genuine passive per day, that is _years_. Every big hour total you see quoted - 10,000 hours, 2,000 hours to N1, and so on - is a community estimate derived from self-reports, not a measured figure. Nobody has run the study.

**2. Burnout is the normal failure mode, not the exceptional one.** The method asks you to spend most of your discretionary time on an activity that is, for the first six months, genuinely unpleasant - you understand almost nothing, Anki reviews compound, and progress is invisible. Anki fatigue is so common that the Donkuri guide devotes a section to it. Most people who start AJATT quit. The survivors write the guides.

**3. "No English ever" is counterproductive for beginners.** This is the dogma most confidently rejected here. A beginner who refuses an English grammar explanation will spend three weeks confused about the て form to preserve an ideological purity that buys them nothing. Reading Tae Kim or [Yokubi](https://yoku.bi/) in English for an afternoon costs you one afternoon and saves you months. The rule has a legitimate core - _do not read translations instead of Japanese, do not lean on English subtitles, transition to J-J dictionaries eventually_ - but the maximalist version is cargo-culting.

**4. Survivorship bias is structural, not incidental.** Every AJATT success story you read was written by someone for whom it worked. There is no blog called "I did AJATT for three years and plateaued". The method's evidence base is a collection of self-reported anecdotes from unusually obsessive people, many of whom were already talented language learners, several of whom later monetized the story. That does not make it false. It does mean the confidence level in the community vastly exceeds the evidence.

**5. The output-neglect problem is real and severe.** This is the most damaging inherited flaw. The MIA-era claim that early output "damages" your Japanese produced learners with strong comprehension and no ability to speak. shoui, after four-plus years running TheMoeWay, reports: "I haven't seen many that are confident in output. Why is that? Well for most of them, they don't even want to try outputting at all because they're scared of saying something broken." If your goal involves _talking to people_, input-only is a direct path to failure. See [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md).

**6. The "10,000 hours and still can't hold a conversation" failure mode.** This is a specific, predictable outcome of the above. You can reach the point where you read novels comfortably and understand anime without subtitles and still freeze in a conversation, because comprehension and production are different skills built by different activities. Passive knowledge does not spontaneously convert to active production - it converts _faster_ with a strong input base, but conversion requires actual production practice. Immersion is necessary and not sufficient.

**7. Comprehensible input theory is the foundation and is also over-claimed.** See the Krashen note above. "Input is the primary driver" is well supported. "Input is the _only_ thing that matters and explicit study is useless" is not, and it is the version that circulates.

**8. Measurement is weak, so the community substitutes proxies.** Characters read, hours immersed, cards mined - all easy to count, all only loosely coupled to ability. This creates a specific pathology: optimizing the number instead of the skill.

> **If you cannot arrange 2+ hours of daily contact with Japanese for years, or you need conversational ability in under a year, or you actively dislike anime, manga, novels and games, AJATT is the wrong method for you.** Take a structured course, hire a tutor, and use immersion as a supplement. That is not a lesser path - it is a different constraint set.

## Recommended path (opinionated)

> **The pick: Donkuri/saegusa's guide as the syllabus, Animecards' card format for mining, TheMoeWay's FAQ as the troubleshooting manual, Perdition's character counter as the progress meter - and neither the "no English" nor the "no output" dogma.**
>
> The reasoning:
>
> - **Donkuri for the syllabus**, because it is current, coherent with its own tooling (Kaishi 1.5k, Lapis, Yomitan), explicitly anti-dogma, and it does not sell anything. The competing choice is TheMoeWay, which is friendlier but more scattered and partly stale.
> - **Animecards word cards**, because the argument is concrete and testable: faster reviews, no context crutch, one thing tested per card - the same information reviewed 2-4x faster is not a close call, and it supersedes older AJATT and MIA sentence-card orthodoxy.
> - **TheMoeWay's FAQ**, because almost every problem you will hit in year one has a direct answer in it, written by someone who has heard the question 500 times.
> - **Perdition's character ladder**, because "am I improving?" needs a number or you will quit during the plateau. It is an imperfect proxy, but a useful one.
> - **English allowed, output early**, for the reasons in the critiques section.
>
> **Ignore this recommendation if:** you need external accountability and deadlines, in which case anchor to [JLPT](../JLPT/readme.md) exam dates and use immersion as the engine underneath rather than as the structure itself. Also ignore it if you genuinely enjoy textbooks - a learner who happily does 90 minutes of Genki a day beats a learner who does 20 resentful minutes of "optimal" immersion.

## Phase-by-phase plan

The phases below are calibrated to **60-90 minutes of active study per day plus passive immersion during work** - a common constraint for a working adult. Scale the calendar, not the ratios, if your available time differs.

### Phase 0 - Foundation (weeks 1-3)

- **Goal:** kana fluent, tools installed, and the _habit_ of daily contact with Japanese established before any of it is useful.
- **Time:** 45-60 min/day active.
- **Daily routine:** kana drilling until it is automatic; install Anki and Yomitan; start a core vocabulary deck at 5-10 new cards/day; put on Japanese audio while doing literally anything else.
- **Materials:** [Kana guide](../Kana/readme.md); Anki and Yomitan setup in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>); [`Japanese Resources.pdf`](<./Community Guides/Japanese Resources.pdf>) for the 10-step overview; UsagiSpoon's 30-day routine inside [`TheMoeWay/shoui520.github.io-master.zip`](./TheMoeWay/shoui520.github.io-master.zip) (`docs/routine.md`).
- **Done when:** you can read a random kana string aloud without hesitating, Anki and Yomitan both work, and you have had Japanese audio or video in your day for 14 consecutive days. Comprehension at this stage should be approximately zero. That is expected.

### Phase 1 - Comprehensible-input floor (months 1-6)

- **Goal:** get from "Japanese is noise" to "I can pick individual words out of a sentence". This phase is a grind and it is the one people quit in.
- **Time:** 60-90 min/day active study, plus 1-3 hours/day passive.
- **Daily routine:** 5-10 new core vocabulary cards + reviews (~25-35 min); read one grammar guide section (~15 min); 30+ min of active immersion with Japanese subtitles on, no mining; passive audio all day.
- **Materials:** Kaishi 1.5k via [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md); Tae Kim or Yokubi via [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md); Japanese subtitles from [jimaku.cc](https://jimaku.cc/) or [kitsunekko](https://kitsunekko.net/); beginner podcasts from [`100+ Podcasts.xlsx`](<./Reference Spreadsheets/100+ Podcasts.xlsx>) (start with the "Starter Pack" block at the top of the sheet); easy-rated media from the [saegusa media recommendations](<./saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations>).
- **Done when:** you finish your core deck (or reach ~1,200 mature words), you have read a grammar guide front to back once, and in a random anime episode you can identify the boundaries of most sentences and catch several words per line. **Do not mine before this point.**

### Phase 2 - Mining begins (months 6-14)

- **Goal:** switch from consuming someone else's deck to generating your own. This is the phase where the loop starts feeding itself.
- **Time:** 60-90 min/day active, passive unchanged.
- **Daily routine:** ~20 min Anki reviews; 5-10 new _mined_ cards/day; 40-60 min active immersion in one primary reading medium and one primary listening medium; mine as you go.
- **Materials:** [Sentence mining](#sentence-mining) below; the mining pipeline in [`AnimecardsWebsite-master.zip`](./AnimecardsWebsite/AnimecardsWebsite-master.zip) (`docs/minefromanime.md`, `docs/media.md`); Lapis or Lapis-modified note types; Donkuri's mining page in [`learn-japanese-main.zip`](./donkuri.github.io/learn-japanese-main.zip) (`mining/index.html`).
- **Done when:** you can go from "unknown word in an episode" to "finished card in Anki with word audio, sentence, screenshot and definition" in under 30 seconds, and you have 500+ self-mined cards. Also: you have finished one complete piece of native media - a full manga volume, a full anime season, or a short VN route - in Japanese.

### Phase 3 - Volume (months 14-30)

- **Goal:** stop optimizing and accumulate. Comprehension rises roughly with characters consumed, and nothing else you do in this phase matters as much as the raw count.
- **Time:** 60-90 min active, but shift the balance - less Anki, more immersion.
- **Daily routine:** ~15-20 min Anki; 5 new cards/day, or fewer - mining yield naturally drops as comprehension rises; the rest is reading and listening; add deliberate output 2-3x/week.
- **Materials:** whatever you actually want to consume. Media sources per [Reading](../Reading/readme.md) and [Listening](../Listening/readme.md); the [Itazuraneko library](https://djtguide.github.io/library/librarymain.html); difficulty estimates from [jpdb.io](https://jpdb.io/) or [jiten.moe](https://jiten.moe/).
- **Done when:** you have logged 2-5 million characters of reading (Perdition's 伯爵 to 公爵 range), you can watch a slice-of-life show without subtitles and follow the plot, and you have read one full novel or completed one full VN.

### Phase 4 - Monolingual transition and output (months 24-48, overlapping Phase 3)

- **Goal:** stop learning Japanese through English, and convert comprehension into production.
- **Time:** same active budget; the composition changes.
- **Daily routine:** J-J dictionary first, J-E only as fallback; mine monolingual definitions; regular speaking practice with correction; deliberate pitch-accent work.
- **Materials:** `docs/monolingual.md` in the TheMoeWay mirror - a full 21,000-character guide to the transition, including which dictionaries in which order; [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md) for shadowing, chorusing and pitch; [`Japanese Resources.pdf`](<./Community Guides/Japanese Resources.pdf>) steps 6-8.
- **Done when:** you can read a J-J definition of an unknown word and understand it without a J-E fallback, and you can hold a 30-minute conversation with a native speaker on a familiar topic without switching to English.

### Phase 5 - Domain expansion (indefinite)

- **Goal:** Japanese is no longer a project. Now you push into the domains your actual life needs.
- **Time:** whatever is natural.
- **Daily routine:** live in Japanese; deliberately attack new domains - whatever your work or interests actually require: technical or professional vocabulary in your field, 敬語 for the workplace, formal documentation, interview Japanese.
- **Materials:** [Culture](../Culture/readme.md) for work culture and etiquette; [JLPT](../JLPT/readme.md) if you need a credential for visa or hiring purposes; [`Japanese Resources.pdf`](<./Community Guides/Japanese Resources.pdf>) step 9 lists the areas that still need dedicated practice - handwriting (漢字検定), 敬語, dialects (関西弁), and academic or professional subjects.
- **Done when:** the question stops making sense. You are not finishing a curriculum any more, you are using a language.

## Active vs passive immersion

This distinction is the most abused idea in the whole method, so let me be blunt about it.

**Active immersion** means Japanese is the only thing you are doing. You are looking at the screen, parsing sentences, noticing words you do not know, looking some of them up. It is tiring. It is where essentially all of your measurable gains come from.

**Passive immersion** means Japanese audio is playing while you do something else - working, commuting, cooking, cleaning.

### What passive immersion actually buys you

Be honest about a weak effect rather than dishonest about a strong one. Here is what the sources in this folder actually say:

- Donkuri: "It is not entirely clear to what extent passive listening helps you learn languages. It is however pretty clear that active listening does help... you should do passive listening if you like it, but you probably shouldn't expect to get massive listening gains from it."
- shoui (TheMoeWay): "If you can't understand like 90% of your immersion yet then it won't get you gains. If you understand over 90% then you can make gains when you listen in on it every now and then, but much less efficient than active immersion overall."
- Both are unequivocal that sleep-listening does nothing. shoui's complete answer on immersing while sleeping: "It's BS."

So, what passive immersion plausibly does:

- **Keeps phonology warm.** Continued exposure to Japanese prosody, rhythm and phoneme boundaries.
- **Consolidates the already-familiar.** Words you have mined and half-know get extra low-quality reps.
- **Maintains identity and habit.** This is underrated. Having Japanese in your ears all day keeps you _being a person who is learning Japanese_, which sustains the active hours.

What it does **not** do:

- Teach you new words. You cannot acquire vocabulary from audio you do not comprehend.
- Substitute for active hours at any ratio. Ten passive hours do not equal one active hour; if comprehension is low they equal something much closer to zero.

**Condensed audio** is the one technique that makes passive immersion meaningfully better. You take an anime episode you have already watched actively, strip out the silence and the non-dialogue, and get roughly 8-12 minutes of pure dialogue out of a 24-minute episode. Because you already know the plot and have mined the vocabulary, comprehension is high - which is exactly the condition under which passive listening is not useless. Tools: [Condenser](https://ercanserteli.com/condenser/) to make your own, or the [condensed audio catalog](https://condensedaudiocatalog.com/) and the collections linked in TheMoeWay's `docs/resources.md`.

### The honest math on hours

Take a representative schedule as a worked example, because the arithmetic _is_ the argument:

| Component                     | Hours/day | Hours/year | Quality weight (illustrative estimate) | Effective hours/year |
| :---------------------------- | :-------- | :--------- | :------------------------------------- | :------------------- |
| Active study (Anki + grammar) | 0.5       | ~180       | 1.0                                    | ~180                 |
| Active immersion              | 0.75      | ~270       | 1.0                                    | ~270                 |
| Passive while working         | 3.0       | ~1,100     | 0.1 - 0.2                              | ~110-220             |
| **Total**                     | **4.25**  | **~1,550** |                                        | **~560-670**         |

Two conclusions fall straight out of this table. First, **passive immersion contributes real value precisely because the raw hour count is so large** - even at a 10% weight, 1,100 hours becomes 110 effective hours, comparable to a whole year of active study. That is a genuine argument for passive immersion for a working adult. Second, **the weight is a guess and it dominates the result.** If the true weight is 0.02 rather than 0.15, passive is nearly worthless. Nobody knows this number. Anyone quoting you a total immersion hour count without disclosing their active/passive weighting is publishing a number that means nothing.

## Sentence mining

This is the canonical chapter for mining in this repo. Everything else links here.

### The i+1 principle

**i+1** means: a sentence where you understand everything except _one_ thing. That one thing is what you mine.

Why one, and not two or five:

- **A sentence with zero unknowns** teaches you nothing new; it is review, not acquisition.
- **A sentence with one unknown** is learnable from context. The surrounding Japanese carries the meaning, and the card has a real chance of sticking because your brain has somewhere to attach it.
- **A sentence with three unknowns** is not a card, it is a homework assignment. You will fail it repeatedly, it will eat your review time, and you will start to hate Anki.

Practical consequence: **i+1 is a property of the pair (sentence, you), not of the sentence.** As you improve, sentences graduate into being i+1 and then out of it again. This means you cannot mine efficiently from material far above your level, because almost nothing in it is i+1. Match the material to your level - that is the whole reason the difficulty ladders in [Reading](../Reading/readme.md) and [Listening](../Listening/readme.md) exist.

### What makes a good mining sentence

| Criterion       | Good                                                                  | Bad                                                       |
| :-------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------- |
| Unknown words   | Exactly 1                                                             | 0, or 3+                                                  |
| Length          | Short enough to read at a glance - roughly 5-20 characters of context | A three-clause monologue                                  |
| Grammar         | All of it already familiar                                            | Contains a grammar point you also do not know             |
| The target word | Reasonably common; you can imagine meeting it again                   | Archaic, hyper-specific, a proper noun, or a one-off joke |
| Context         | The sentence actually disambiguates the meaning                       | The word could mean anything here                         |
| Audio           | Available - from the episode, the VN, or a dictionary                 | Text-only, if avoidable                                   |
| Source          | Media you chose to consume anyway                                     | A frequency list you are grinding out of duty             |

Additional rules worth following:

- **Do not mine proper nouns.** 田中 is not vocabulary.
- **Do not mine a word you cannot imagine using or hearing again.** Berserk has magnificent archaic vocabulary that will never appear in a Tokyo office.
- **If the grammar is the unknown, do not make a vocabulary card.** Go read the grammar point in [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md) instead. Mining will not fix a grammar gap.

### Card formats: sentence vs word vs plain vocab

| Format                                                       | Front                                | Back                                                                         | Review speed                             | Main risk                                                                                               |
| :----------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :--------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Sentence card** (classic AJATT/MIA)                        | Full sentence, target word marked    | Reading, definition, audio                                                   | Slowest                                  | Context becomes a crutch - you pass by recognizing the scene, not the word                              |
| **Word card / "anime card"** (Animecards, Lapis)             | Target word alone, optional hint     | Reading, definition, word audio, sentence, sentence audio, screenshot, pitch | 2-4x faster, per Animecards' review data | Loses front-side context, so ambiguous words need a hint field                                          |
| **Plain vocab card** (word to English, no context, no audio) | Word                                 | English gloss                                                                | Fastest                                  | No audio means no listening transfer; no context means no usage knowledge; brittle for polysemous words |
| **Cloze card**                                               | Sentence with the target blanked out | The missing piece                                                            | Medium                                   | Good for grammar points, poor as your main vocabulary format                                            |

> **Recommended: word cards (Animecards/Lapis style) for the bulk of your mining.** The case is empirical and cheap to verify for yourself: same information, dramatically faster reviews, and the sentence-card failure mode - passing a card because you remember the scene rather than knowing the word - is real. Every minute saved in Anki is a minute available for immersion, and immersion is what actually teaches you Japanese.
>
> A separate, optional technique is a **sentence bank**: a deck of whole sentences (from a grammar guide, from anime, or similar) reviewed at a light, fixed daily pace, pressing only "Good", with no grading and no failing. This is not a contradiction with word-card mining: it is not a memorization deck, it is a _reps machine_ for grammar patterns and prosody, and the sentence format is right for that job because the sentence _is_ the content. The distinction that matters: **mining decks test one word and should be word cards; exposure decks show you whole sentences and should be sentence cards.** Do not confuse the two jobs, and do not grade an exposure deck.

### The pipeline, end to end

This is the full loop for anime. Manga, VNs and novels differ only in the capture tool.

1. **Set up once.** Yomitan with dictionaries, Anki with a mining note type ([Lapis](https://github.com/donkuri/lapis), [Lapis-modified](https://github.com/friedrich-de/lapis-modified), or Senren), and a media capture path. Setup mechanics live in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>); the capture tools - mpv scripts, asbplayer, ShareX, AMES - are documented in `docs/minefromanime.md` and `docs/media.md` inside the [Animecards mirror](./AnimecardsWebsite/AnimecardsWebsite-master.zip).
2. **Get Japanese subtitles** for your show, from [jimaku.cc](https://jimaku.cc/) or [kitsunekko](https://kitsunekko.net/). If they are out of sync, `docs/subtitles.md` in the Animecards mirror covers automatic resyncing with alass.
3. **Watch actively.** Japanese subtitles on, English subtitles off. Watch to enjoy it, not to harvest it.
4. **Notice an unknown word.** Pause.
5. **Judge whether it is i+1.** Is this the _only_ thing you do not understand in this line? If no: look it up if you want, and keep watching. Do not make a card.
6. **Look it up in Yomitan.** Check the reading and the definition. Check the frequency ranking if your dictionary setup shows it - a word ranked well beyond the top ~30,000 is usually not worth a card yet.
7. **Create the card in one keystroke.** A properly configured mining setup captures target word, reading, definition, word audio, the sentence, sentence audio, and a screenshot in a single action. If this takes you more than a few seconds, your setup is wrong and you will stop mining. Fix the setup; do not power through it.
8. **Keep watching immediately.** Do not edit the card now. Mining must not break the flow of the media, or the media stops being enjoyable and the whole engine stalls.
9. **Review the next day.** Word on the front; decide whether you know reading and meaning; flip. Fail generously - failing a card is cheap, and a card you "sort of" know is a card you do not know.
10. **Prune ruthlessly.** Any card you have failed five or more times is a bad card, not a hard word. Delete it. You will meet that word again in immersion and mine a better card for it.

A useful discipline from the Animecards guide: mining a whole show you have already watched is the _third_ stage of learning, not the first. And a useful one from shoui: batch your mining separately from your enjoyment - see below.

### How many cards per day is sustainable

| New cards/day | Reviews/day at steady state (rough) | Anki time/day | Verdict                                                                             |
| :------------ | :---------------------------------- | :------------ | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 5             | ~50-75                              | 10-20 min     | Sustainable indefinitely.                                                           |
| 10            | ~100-150                            | 20-35 min     | The community default, and the ceiling for most working adults.                     |
| 20            | ~200-300                            | 45-75 min     | Sustainable for months, not years. This is where Anki starts to displace immersion. |
| 30+           | 300+                                | 90+ min       | Anki has become your hobby and Japanese has become your Anki. Stop.                 |

The review counts are rough - actual load depends on your retention settings and scheduler, which [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) covers. The shape is what matters: **new cards compound into reviews, and reviews are the thing that eventually kills people.** shoui's answer: "Just do 10. If you feel like it is too less, do 15 or 20. Please bear in mind that the more new cards you do, the more reviews you'll get."

> **A workable approach:** a low single-digit number of new cards a day on each deck you are running - a core vocabulary deck, a kanji deck if you study one separately, and an optional sentence-exposure deck - deliberately conservative, within whatever daily budget you have set aside. Raise the number once the load has proven sustainable for a few weeks. **Every minute Anki takes is a minute stolen from immersion.** Anki is the crutch; immersion is the walking. A learner doing 30 new cards a day and 20 minutes of immersion has inverted the method.

### When to mine and when to just keep watching

Mine when:

- The word is i+1 and looks reasonably common.
- You are in a phase where mining is the point (Phase 2 onward).
- Your review load has room.

Just look it up and keep going when:

- There are several unknowns in the line.
- You are behind on reviews.
- The word is niche, archaic, or a proper noun.
- You are enjoying the show and stopping would ruin it. **This one overrides everything.**

There is a useful alternative discipline from shoui worth stealing: **mine from material you have already consumed.** Watch or read for enjoyment with lookups, then go back through the same episode or chapter later and mine what did not stick. This cleanly separates enjoyment from harvesting, and it acts as a natural filter - whatever you forgot within a day is exactly what needs a card.

### The beginner mistake of mining too early

This is the single most common way beginners waste months.

- **The mistake:** you install Yomitan in week three, start mining your first anime episode, and generate 60 cards from 24 minutes of video.
- **Why it feels right:** you are doing the advanced thing the guides describe. It feels like real progress. Every card feels earned.
- **Why it fails:** at that level essentially nothing in native media is i+1 - every sentence has four unknowns. Your cards are _not_ learnable from context, so they degenerate into rote memorization of random words. Your review pile explodes, retention collapses, Anki becomes miserable, and you quit. Meanwhile a pre-made frequency-ordered deck would have taught you the same 1,500 words far faster, with better audio and better examples.
- **The fix:** finish a core deck first - Kaishi 1.5k, ~1,500 words, at 5-10/day. Then mine. The Animecards guide is explicit that the core-deck phase should run 6-12 months and that creating your own mining deck is the _finale_, not the opening move.

## Choosing media you actually like

The single highest-leverage decision in this entire method is _what you watch and read_, because enjoyment is the compliance mechanism. This is not a motivational platitude, it is the load-bearing engineering constraint:

- You need thousands of hours.
- Willpower does not produce thousands of hours.
- Only genuine wanting produces thousands of hours.
- Therefore **media you love is strictly better than "optimal" media you tolerate.**

The "optimal media" argument - start with Non Non Biyori because the vocabulary is simple and the frequency distribution is friendly - is correct on the merits and wrong in practice if you find it boring. Donkuri and saegusa both recommend specific easy starters (Non Non Biyori for anime, Tomo-chan wa Onnanoko for manga, Kuma Kuma Kuma Bear for novels, Tsuyuchiru Letter for VNs) and they are genuinely good recommendations. But the guide's own framing is the right one: "The best one will be the one you don't mind spending lots of time on."

The one structural rule worth enforcing regardless of taste: **pick at least one reading medium and at least one listening medium.** The immersion community historically skews heavily toward reading and produces learners who read novels and cannot follow a conversation. Donkuri flags this explicitly. Do not be that learner.

A useful structure to build a media diet around: a long-running, easy listening backbone for volume (a show with a large episode count and simple, repetitive dialogue is ideal), one short, easy reading source for mining yield, and one harder work worth genuinely loving as emotional fuel - a demanding text like Berserk (archaic, violent vocabulary) or Evangelion (dense technical and religious jargon) can fill that role precisely because it is worth the extra effort. A rough ratio is 70% easy-and-useful to 30% hard-and-loved; the hard-and-loved portion is not inefficiency, it is the reason the other 70% happens at all.

## Common pitfalls

**1. Mining before you have a base.**

- _Why it feels right:_ mining is the advanced technique, so doing it early feels like moving fast.
- _The fix:_ finish a ~1,500-word core deck first. Nothing in native media is i+1 until then.

**2. Letting Anki eat the immersion budget.**

- _Why it feels right:_ Anki is measurable, finite and satisfying. You can _finish_ your reviews. You can never finish immersion.
- _The fix:_ hard-cap Anki. If it exceeds 30 minutes, cut new cards until it comes back down. Anki serves immersion, never the reverse.

**3. Passive immersion as an alibi.**

- _Why it feels right:_ the hour count looks enormous and you did technically do Japanese.
- _The fix:_ count active minutes only. Passive is a free bonus, not a substitute.

**4. "No English" purity in month two.**

- _Why it feels right:_ the original AJATT writing is emphatic about it, and purity feels like commitment.
- _The fix:_ use English grammar explanations for as long as they are the fastest route to understanding. Transition to J-J when J-J is actually easier than J-E, not on a date you picked in advance.

**5. Postponing output until you are "ready".**

- _Why it feels right:_ the MIA-lineage claim that early output damages your Japanese, plus a genuine fear of sounding stupid.
- _The fix:_ output early and get corrected. The cost of a mistake you made and fixed is far lower than the cost of three years of silence. See [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md).

**6. Method-shopping instead of immersing.**

- _Why it feels right:_ researching the optimal approach feels like productive work, and there is always another guide to read.
- _The fix:_ pick one stack and run it for six months without changing it. Donkuri's guide says it best: "Avoid toxic arguments regarding study methods." Also: if you are reading this guide for the third time instead of watching an episode, close the guide.

**7. English subtitles while "listening".**

- _Why it feels right:_ you understand the show, so it feels like comprehension.
- _The fix:_ your brain reads the subtitles and mutes the audio - this is automatic and you cannot override it by trying. Japanese subs or no subs. Donkuri is permissive about occasional glances at English subs when you are totally lost; do not turn that into the default.

**8. Immersing exclusively in one medium.**

- _Why it feels right:_ you found the thing you like, and reading (or listening) is going well.
- _The fix:_ one reading medium plus one listening medium, always. The community's characteristic failure is the learner who reads novels fluently and cannot follow a spoken conversation.

**9. Tracking as procrastination.**

- _Why it feels right:_ graphs, streaks, character counts and heatmaps all look like progress.
- _The fix:_ see the next section. Track a very small number of things, automatically, and never spend more than a minute a day on it.

**10. Optimizing the tooling instead of using it.**

- _Why it feels right:_ the tooling is genuinely interesting, and a better setup is genuinely valuable - especially for anyone technically inclined.
- _The fix:_ name this risk explicitly. One setup session, then a hard freeze. Yak-shaving your mpv config is not Japanese.

## Measuring progress

Vibes are useless here, because comprehension improves too slowly to feel day to day, and your subjective sense of ability swings wildly with how hard the current episode happens to be. You need numbers and you need self-tests.

### What is worth tracking

| Metric                                  | Why it is worth it                                                                                                 | How                                                                               |
| :-------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Active minutes per day**              | The only input that is definitely causal. Everything else is derived.                                              | One number in a note per day, or automatically                                    |
| **Characters read**                     | The community's best available proxy for reading ability; Perdition's whole ladder is built on it                  | [exSTATic](https://github.com/KamWithK/exSTATic), or a subtitle character counter |
| **Cards mined, and retention %**        | A falling mining rate is _good news_ - it means fewer unknowns. Retention below ~80% means your cards are too hard | Anki stats, automatically                                                         |
| **Episodes / volumes / books finished** | The single most motivating number, and it maps to real capability                                                  | A list, one line per item                                                         |

### What is procrastination

- Daily graphs of anything.
- Multi-sheet spreadsheets with weighted composite scores.
- Counting passive hours.
- Vocabulary-size estimators, checked repeatedly.
- Comparing your numbers to other learners' numbers.

If tracking takes more than a minute a day, or is not fully automatic, it has become the hobby.

### Self-tests, not vibes

Real checks you can run, roughly in order of level:

1. **The kana test.** Read a random kana string aloud with no hesitation. (End of Phase 0.)
2. **The sentence-boundary test.** Play an unseen anime episode with no subtitles. Can you tell where sentences begin and end, and catch several words per line? (End of Phase 1.)
3. **The mining-speed test.** Unknown word to finished card in under 30 seconds. (Phase 2.)
4. **The i+1 density test.** Open a page of your current reading material. Count the sentences with exactly one unknown word. Under ~20% of sentences means the material is too hard for efficient mining. Near zero because you know everything means it is too easy - level up.
5. **The completion test.** Have you finished a full season, a full manga volume, a full VN route, a full novel? Finishing matters more than starting ten things.
6. **The no-subtitle test.** Watch a slice-of-life episode with no subtitles at all. Can you follow the plot? Can you follow the jokes? (Phase 3.)
7. **The monolingual test.** Look up an unknown word in a J-J dictionary. Did you understand the definition without a J-E fallback? (Phase 4.)
8. **The conversation test.** 30 minutes with a native speaker on a familiar topic without switching to English. (Phase 4.) **Run this one early and often even if you expect to fail it** - it is the test that exposes the output-neglect failure mode while there is still time to correct it.
9. **The kanji fluency test.** Described in detail in the [Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md) guide - can you recognize a kanji you saw once, days later, among similar ones?

Perdition's character ladder gives you external checkpoints if you want them: 100k characters (平民), 500k (男爵), 1M (子爵), 2M (伯爵), 3.5M (侯爵), 5M (公爵), 7.5M (大公), 10M (王様), 15M (天皇), 25M (地仙), 50M (天仙), 100M (上手). Useful as milestones. Do not mistake the number for the skill - the model that ability is linear in characters consumed is a convenient simplification, and it ignores listening entirely.

## Where to go next

- **Just starting?** [Kana](../Kana/readme.md) first, then [Romaji](../Romaji/readme.md) to understand why you are dropping it, then [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) to get Anki and Yomitan working.
- **Building the base?** [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) for the core deck and card formats, [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md) for sequencing, [Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md) for radicals, readings and kanji fluency.
- **Ready to consume media?** [Listening](../Listening/readme.md) for podcasts and comprehensible-input audio ladders, [Reading](../Reading/readme.md) for graded readers and reading ladders. Those two guides own _what_ to consume; this guide owns _how much, when, and what to do with it_.
- **Worried about output?** [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md) for pitch accent, shadowing and conversation practice. Given the output-neglect critique above, go there sooner than the classic AJATT timeline suggests. [Writing](../Writing/readme.md) for handwriting, stroke order and IME setup.
- **Need a deadline or a credential?** [JLPT](../JLPT/readme.md) for levels, structure, registration and test strategy - useful for visa and hiring purposes even if the test is a poor measure of ability.
- **Planning to actually live there?** [Culture](../Culture/readme.md) for etiquette, work culture, society and history. Language without cultural context gets you politely misunderstood.
