# Romaji

> **TL;DR**
> - Romaji (ローマ字) is not a Japanese script. It is a family of *notations* for writing Japanese in Latin letters, and there are several mutually incompatible ones.
> - There are three systems worth knowing by name - **Hepburn**, **Kunrei-shiki**, **Nihon-shiki** - plus **wapuro romaji**, the informal convention set you actually type into an IME.
> - Romaji has real, permanent jobs: IME input, passports and legal name transliteration, signage for tourists, branding, and linguistic citation. It does *not* have a job in your reading practice.
> - Start dropping it on day 1. Learn kana in your first one to two weeks ([../Kana/readme.md](../Kana/readme.md)), then purge romaji from every piece of study material you own.
> - Recommended: know **revised Hepburn** well enough to read it, type in **wapuro**, and read everything else in kana. Learn to *recognize* Kunrei-shiki so Japanese-authored material does not confuse you.

This guide has no "Daily and weekly routine" section - romaji is a reference topic and a decision, not a daily practice. The daily practice lives in the Kana, Reading, and Speaking guides.

---

## What this is and why it matters

Romaji is the writing of Japanese with the Latin alphabet. 「ありがとう」becomes `arigatou`, or `arigatō`, or `arigatô`, depending on whose rules you follow - and that variation is the whole story of this topic.

Two reasons it matters more than it looks:

1. **You cannot avoid it.** You will type Japanese through a romaji keyboard layout for the rest of your life. You will read station signs in it. Your passport and your residence card will spell your name in it, and your name will be spelled back at you in katakana. Japanese people learn it in elementary school.
2. **It is the single most common thing that quietly caps a beginner's ceiling.** Not because romaji is evil, but because a learner who can read `tabemasu` will postpone learning to read たべます, and that postponement compounds. Everything downstream - kanji, dictionary lookup, grammar, reading speed - is gated on reading kana directly.

So the goal of this guide is narrow and specific: **explain the landscape accurately, then get you out of it fast.**

### Transcription vs transliteration - the distinction that explains everything

Every disagreement between romanization systems comes down to one question:

| | Question it answers | Optimized for |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Transcription** | What does this *sound* like? | A reader who does not know Japanese |
| **Transliteration** | What *kana* was this written with? | A reader who knows Japanese, or a machine |

Hepburn leans transcription. Nihon-shiki leans transliteration. Kunrei-shiki sits between them, closer to transliteration, organized around Japanese *phonemes* rather than surface sounds.

Concretely: the kana た・ち・つ・て・と all begin with the same Japanese consonant phoneme, /t/. It just *comes out* as a different sound before /i/ and /u/. A transliterating system writes `ta ti tu te to` and keeps the pattern visible. A transcribing system writes `ta chi tsu te to` and keeps the pronunciation guessable. Both are correct answers to different questions, and neither can be both.

The local U. Tokyo document ([Recommended System for Romanizing Japanese](<./General Guides/Recommended System for Romanizing Japanese.pdf>), p. 2) makes exactly this argument, and adds the practical counterpoint: an English speaker reading the Kunrei-shiki spelling `tikatetu` (地下鉄) would pronounce all three `t`s as a hard English "t", which is wrong for two of them. Hepburn's `chikatetsu` gets a naive reader much closer.

Hold onto that trade-off. It is also the reason romaji damages your phonology - see [the case against romaji](#the-case-against-romaji) below.

## Where it fits in the journey

**Prerequisites:** none. This is hour zero material.

**What it unlocks:**
- Typing Japanese at all (wapuro romaji) - see [../Writing/readme.md](../Writing/readme.md) for the IME chapter proper.
- Reading the Latin half of Japanese signage, maps, and product names.
- Reading academic and library citations of Japanese sources.
- Writing your own name for Japanese officialdom, and understanding how Japanese names get spelled in Latin letters.

**What it does not unlock:** anything else. Romaji unlocks no reading, no kanji, no dictionary, and no exam. It is a bridge you cross in the first week, not a room you live in.

**Position in the sequence:** Romaji -> [Kana](../Kana/readme.md) (week 1-2) -> everything else. If you are past week two and still reading romaji, this guide is aimed squarely at you.

Bold marks where a system is alone or nearly alone. Note the shape of it: **Nihon-shiki and Kunrei-shiki are identical except where kana distinctions have collapsed in modern pronunciation.** Nihon-shiki keeps the historical spellings; Kunrei-shiki merges them to match how the language is actually said.

Whole words, from the local sources:

| Japanese | Nihon-shiki | Kunrei-shiki | Hepburn | Wapuro (typed) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |

| 富士山 (ふじさん) | Huzisan | Huzisan | Fujisan | `fujisan` |

| 重要 (じゅうよう) | zyûyô | zyûyô | jūyō | `juuyou` |
| any word with a medial ん, e.g. けいひん | - | - | Keihin | `Keihinn` |

(The お茶 / 富士山 / ローマ字 row comes from the [Japan Switch guide](<./General Guides/Ultimate Guide to Japanese Romaji - Japan Switch.html>); the 重要 and ん examples come from the U. Tokyo document, p. 2, which gives `Keihinn` as its example of wapuro spelling leaking into prose.)

Notation for vowel length is the messiest part of every system and varies by publisher: macron (`ō`), circumflex (`ô`), doubled vowel (`oo`), kana-faithful (`ou`), `oh`, or nothing at all. Do not assume a document is using the system you think it is until you see a long vowel.

### Hepburn

ヘボン式. Named for James Curtis Hepburn, who used an early version in the third edition of his Japanese-English dictionary (1887). Sound-oriented: consonants behave roughly like English, vowels roughly like Italian/Spanish/Portuguese.

**Variants you will meet:**
- **Traditional Hepburn** - writes ん as `m` before b, m, p: `sempai` (先輩), `Shimbashi` (新橋). Retains some archaic spellings (`kwa`).
- **Revised / modified Hepburn** - writes ん as `n` everywhere, with `n'` before a vowel or y. This is the modern default in English-language publishing, and the basis of the ALA-LC library romanization used in Western library catalogues. The fine print no longer has to be taken on trust - the Library of Congress table itself is now in this folder at [ALA-LC Japanese Romanization Table](<./Standards - Library and Geographic/ALA-LC Japanese Romanization Table (2022) - Library of Congress.pdf>), so go read the actual rules there rather than any summary. Be warned that it is a scan with no text layer, so you cannot search it.

**Pros**
- A monolingual English reader guesses the pronunciation roughly right. This is a real benefit and the reason it won.
- Dominant outside Japan: textbooks, anime and manga titles, Wikipedia, academic writing in English. The U. Tokyo document recommends a Hepburn-based system and says it is "the system most widely used in English-language publications in Japan and other countries" (p. 1-2).
- Used by the Japanese government on passports and most international communications (U. Tokyo document, p. 1).

**Cons**
- Destroys the kana pattern. `tatsu` -> `tachimasu` looks like an irregular consonant change; つ -> ち is an obvious one-step move within the た row.
- Inconsistent with its own philosophy. Hepburn writes えい as `ei` (`keizai` 経済, `seifu` 政府) even though it is pronounced as a long "e" - a transliteration choice inside a transcription system. The U. Tokyo document prescribes exactly this (p. 4). Meanwhile it merges じ/ぢ and ず/づ, which *are* spelled differently in kana.
- Macrons are fragile. They vanish in email, URLs, ASCII forms, and careless copy-paste, taking vowel length with them. The U. Tokyo document tells you to drop them for well-known place names and naturalized words anyway: `Tokyo`, `Osaka`, "bento", "judo" (p. 5).
- Multiple variants means "Hepburn" alone does not specify a spelling.

**Best for:** anything written in English for readers who do not read Japanese. Your own notes, if you must take notes in Latin letters at all.

### Kunrei-shiki

訓令式, "directive style" - and the name comes from the instrument: it was promulgated as **昭和12年内閣訓令第3号** (Cabinet Directive No. 3 of 1937), titled 「国語ノローマ字綴方統一ノ件」. That directive was then repealed and replaced by **昭和29年内閣告示第1号** (Cabinet Notification No. 1 of 1954) plus its accompanying 内閣訓令第1号 on the same day. So the two dates you see quoted are not a contradiction and not a "revision" - they are two distinct instruments, a 1937 directive and a 1954 notification, the second of which explicitly abolished the first. Both are confirmed from the 文化審議会 report in this folder. Note that the 1954 notification has itself now been repealed - see the status note below. It was the system taught in most Japanese primary schools, and it is codified as **ISO 3602** (U. Tokyo document, p. 1). Organized around Japanese phonemes: one consonant letter per kana row.

**Pros**
- Regular. Near one-to-one correspondence between kana and letters, so verb morphology stays visible: `tatu` -> `tatimasu` -> `tatanai`, all clearly た-row.
- The system Japanese people are actually taught, and therefore the one many Japanese authors and older academic texts use.
- Better for linguistic description of Japanese, precisely because it describes the phonology rather than an English reader's approximation.

**Cons**
- A naive English reader mispronounces it badly. `Huzisan` and `otya` are not close to 富士山 and お茶 for an untrained anglophone.
- Poor at modern katakana extensions - ティ and チ both come out `ti`, creating ambiguity that did not exist in the kana (this point is made on the in-repo [IMABI romanization page](<../Grammar/imabi.org blog offline copy/offline copy/imabi.org/imabi.org/romanization/index.html>), which gives the same ティ / チ example).
- Not used on the signage, passports, or maps you will actually encounter.
- The U. Tokyo document notes it is used only "sometimes" in academic writing and Japanese-language education (p. 1) - officially blessed, practically marginal.

**Best for:** linguistics, phonology, Japanese-authored material, and understanding why Japanese conjugation is more regular than Hepburn makes it look.

> **Status note - important, and it is no longer a hedge. Kunrei-shiki has been repealed.**
>
> The 1954 instruments that made Kunrei-shiki the official standard - 昭和29年内閣告示第1号 and the accompanying 内閣訓令第1号 - were **repealed effective 22 December 2025**. 文化庁 now carries a banner to that effect on every page of the old notification, mirrored locally at [1954 Kunrei-shiki Cabinet Notification](<./Japan Government - Official Notifications/1954 Kunrei-shiki Cabinet Notification (bunka.go.jp)/>):
>
> 「※　この昭和29年内閣告示第1号・同訓令第1号は、令和7年12月22日付けで廃止されています。」
>
> The replacement is **令和7年内閣告示第4号「ローマ字のつづり方」** ([local copy](<./Japan Government - Official Notifications/Romaji no Tsuzurikata - Cabinet Notification No.4 of 2025 (official new rules).pdf>)). The old two-table structure is gone. There is now a single 本表 reading `sa shi su se so / ta chi tsu te to / ha hi fu he ho / za ji zu ze zo / ja ju jo` - which is to say, **Hepburn is now the official standard and Kunrei-shiki is not**. The sequence was: ministerial 諮問 on 2024-05-14, 文化審議会 答申 on 2025-08-20, Cabinet Notification on 2025-12-22.
>
> Two things the new notification is careful about, and they matter more than the headline:
>
> - **Rule 8 does not require established spellings to change.** `judo`, `Tokyo`, `Ohtawara`, `Shimbashi`, `tempura` and `matcha` are all explicitly listed as acceptable in their existing fields. The reform ratifies real-world practice rather than overturning it.
> - **Rule 9 says personal and organisation names should follow the wishes of the person or body concerned.** If someone spells their own name `Itoh` or `Satou`, that is their call, not a standards question.
>
> So everything below about Kunrei-shiki's internal logic still stands as description - it is still what older Japanese schooling taught, it is still what you will meet in linguistics papers, and it is still worth being able to recognise. It is simply no longer the government standard. Read it as history plus recognition skill, not as current officialdom.

### Nihon-shiki

日本式. The oldest of the three, created by the physicist Tanakadate Aikitsu (田中館愛橘) in 1885, originally as part of a movement to *replace* kanji and kana with the Latin alphabet outright. Tanakadate's dating is confirmed by the 文化審議会 report in this folder, which places it in the mid-1880s (明治10年代後半) and notes it originally carried the yotsugana distinctions and the `o`/`wo` split. It is the strictest transliteration of the three.

> **On "ISO 3602 strict":** you will very often see Nihon-shiki equated with a "strict" variant of ISO 3602. No primary source for that equation is available, so treat it as widely repeated rather than verified. What the Japanese government actually says on the record, in the [Romaji Subcommittee minutes of 2025-02-14](<./Japan Government - Official Notifications/Romaji Subcommittee meeting 6 minutes (ISO 3602 discussion, 2025-02-14).pdf>) in this folder, is narrower: 「国際規格、ＩＳＯ3602 というものがあります。こちらは、ぴったり一緒ではないものの現在の内閣告示に近いものになっています。」 - ISO 3602 is "close to, though not exactly the same as," the then-current (Kunrei) notification. That supports ISO 3602 approximating Kunrei-shiki and says nothing at all about a strict variant. The standard itself is paywalled and unread here; the only source asserting the Nihon-shiki equation is a conference presentation. If this distinction matters to your work, buy the standard rather than trusting this guide or any other website.

Differences from Kunrei-shiki, per the in-repo IMABI section:
- The **yotsugana** (四つ仮名) distinctions are preserved: じ `zi` / ぢ `di`, ず `zu` / づ `du`, じゅ `zyu` / ぢゅ `dyu`.
- All わ行 kana keep their `w`: ゐ `wi`, ゑ `we`, を `wo`.
- `kw` and `gw` survive: 関西（くわんさい）`Kwansai`.
- The particles は・へ・を are romanized as written - `ha`, `he`, `wo` - not as pronounced.

**Pros**
- Fully reversible. Given the romaji you can reconstruct the kana exactly, which is what you want for machine processing, historical texts, and precise citation.
- Preserves historical distinctions that matter for etymology and for classical Japanese.

**Cons**
- Most misleading of the three for pronunciation: `wo`, `di`, `du`, `ha` for は as a particle.
- Encodes distinctions that no longer exist in speech, so it looks archaic in modern contexts.

**Best for:** historical linguistics, classical Japanese, reversible machine transliteration. Almost never for learners.

### Wapuro romaji

ワープロローマ字. Not a standard - a convention set. "Wapuro" is from 「ワードプロセッサー」. It is the set of key sequences your IME accepts, and it exists because typing is a different problem from publishing. The U. Tokyo document calls it "the Wapuro System" and notes it "has not been adopted officially by any major publications and it is not suitable for academic writing" (p. 2).

Its defining properties:

| Feature | Wapuro | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Alternate spellings | `shi` and `si` both give し | IMEs accept multiple mappings so both Hepburn- and Kunrei-trained users can type |
| Long vowels | typed kana-faithfully: `toukyou`, `juuyou`, `ookii` | There is no macron key; you type the kana that are there |
| Chouonpu ー | the hyphen key: `ro-maji` -> ローマじ | Direct key mapping |
| Syllabic ん | `nn` (or a bare `n` before a consonant) | A bare `n` before a vowel would be read as な行 |
| Small っ | double the following consonant: `katta`, `kitte` | Mirrors the geminate |
| ぢ / づ | must be typed `di` / `du` | `ji` / `zu` give じ / ず - Hepburn cannot address these kana at all |
| を | `wo` | `o` gives お |

**The consequence that matters:** because you *type* `juuyou` and `nn`, you start *writing* `juuyou` and `Keihinn` in your notes. The U. Tokyo document flags this drift explicitly (p. 2). Wapuro spellings leak out of the keyboard and into prose, where they are wrong in every published system.

**The consequence that helps:** wapuro forces you to know the kana spelling. You cannot type 続く without knowing it is つづく and not つずく. You cannot type 東京 without knowing it is とうきょう. This is genuinely useful discipline, and it is the one place where romaji *teaches* instead of hiding.

**Pros:** it is the only system that matters for input, and it makes kana spelling a daily requirement.
**Cons:** not a publishing standard; inconsistent across IMEs at the edges (obsolete kana, small kana, `kwa`); leaks into writing.
**Best for:** typing. Full stop. See [../Writing/readme.md](../Writing/readme.md) for IME setup and workflow.

### Also worth knowing by name

- **JSL romanization** - from Eleanor Jorden's *Japanese: The Spoken Language*. Kunrei-based, doubles vowels for length (`ookii`, with `ou` reserved for genuinely separate sounds), and - uniquely - **marks pitch accent with diacritics**. A rare example of romaji carrying information kana does not. See [../Speaking/readme.md](../Speaking/readme.md) for pitch accent itself. (Source: in-repo IMABI section.)
- **The early Portuguese Jesuit system** (16th-17th c.) - 日本の言葉 as `Nifon no cotoba`, 平家物語 as `Feiqe no monogatari`. Not usable, but it is direct evidence of how Japanese pronunciation has shifted, and it is why Hepburn's vowels feel Romance rather than English. (Source: Japan Switch guide.)

## Where romaji is legitimately used

Do not overcorrect into believing romaji is always wrong. It has permanent, correct uses:

| Use | System typically used | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| IME input | Wapuro | Universal. You will do this daily forever. |
| Passports, residence cards, legal name transliteration | Hepburn-based | Per the U. Tokyo document, the Japanese government uses Hepburn on passports. The long-vowel handling has its own rules - see caveat below. |
| Station signs, road signs, maps | Hepburn-ish, inconsistently | Good enough to navigate, not good enough to learn from. |
| Product and brand names, logo design | Whatever looks good | Romaji is a design choice in Japan, mixed freely with kana and kanji. |
| Academic and library citation | Revised Hepburn (English-language); Kunrei or Nihon-shiki in linguistics | Different fields, different conventions. Check your venue. |
| Searching for Japanese content in a Latin-script interface | Hepburn | Searching `Fujisan` or an anime title in romaji works. |
| Hours 0-72 of your study, before you know kana | Hepburn | Legitimate. Time-boxed. |

### Japan's own signage is not internally consistent - and that is a data point

A Japanese child is taught Kunrei-shiki in school (`Huzisan`) and then walks past a sign saying `Fujisan`. Passports and road signs have used Hepburn for decades despite cabinet directives pointing elsewhere (in-repo IMABI footnote).

Within Hepburn, signage is still not uniform. Rail signage preserves traditional-Hepburn `m` before b/m/p, so 新橋 (しんばし) is signed **Shimbashi** and 難波 (なんば) is **Namba** - while revised Hepburn and the U. Tokyo recommendation both say write `n` always (`tonbo` not `tombo`, `shinpai` not `shimpai`, p. 5). Long vowels are usually just dropped on signs and passports: 東京 is `Tokyo`, not `Tōkyō`.

> **Passport rules, from the source.** These come from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' own passport romanization table, mirrored locally at [MOFA ヘボン式ローマ字綴方表](<./Passport and Practical/MOFA Hepburn Romanization Table for Passports (ezairyu).html>), so you can act on them - though check the live page before filing anything, because this is the kind of rule that changes.
>
> - **Long vowels are simply dropped.** 「長音：OやUは記入しません。」 伊藤 (イトウ) is `ITO`. 幸太 (コウタ) is `KOTA`. 大野 (オオノ) is `ONO`. 裕貴 (ユウキ) is `YUKI`. No macrons, no doubled letters.
> - **`M` before B, M and P is mandatory, not stylistic.** 難波 is `NAMBA`, 本間 is `HOMMA`, 三瓶 is `SAMPEI`.
> - **`OH` for long o is *not* a freely available option.** It is an exception requiring prior consultation: 「ヘボン式によらないローマ字表記（長音「OH」表記等）を希望するには、予めご相談ください。」 So 大野 as `OHNO` or 伊藤 as `ITOH` is obtainable, but by application, not by ticking a box.
> - **Geminates double the consonant - except before CHI/CHA/CHU/CHO, where a `T` is inserted.** 発地 (ホッチ) is `HOTCHI`, 八丁 (ハッチョウ) is `HATCHO`.
> - **`VA`-style spellings are prohibited.** ヴ becomes `BU`/`BI`/`BA`, not `VU`/`VI`/`VA`.
>
> The U. Tokyo document independently advises respecting individual preference for name spellings like `Satou` or `Itoh` (p. 4), which the 2025 Cabinet Notification has since made explicit policy in its rule 9.

**The lesson:** romaji on a sign is a wayfinding aid produced under a different standard than the one in your textbook. It is not a source of truth about the Japanese language, and you should never infer kana spelling from it.

## The case against romaji

This is the section that actually matters. The argument is not "romaji is cringe." It is that romaji systematically deletes four kinds of information that Japanese encodes, and adds one kind that Japanese does not have.

### 1. It hides mora structure and timing

Japanese timing is **moraic**: each mora takes roughly one beat. Kana are (nearly) a one-character-per-mora script, so the beat count is literally countable on the page. Romaji is read as English syllables.

| Japanese | Morae | Kana beats | Romaji | How an English reader reads it |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| とうきょう (東京) | 4 | と・う・きょ・う | Tokyo | 2 syllables |
| しんかんせん (新幹線) | 6 | し・ん・か・ん・せ・ん | shinkansen | 3 syllables |
| かった | 3 | か・っ・た | katta | 2 syllables |
| かた | 2 | か・た | kata | 2 syllables |

Look at the last two rows. In kana, かった and かた differ by *a whole character* - a visible extra beat. In romaji they differ by a doubled letter, and English spelling has trained you that doubled consonants in "letter" and "better" are *not* extra beats; they are a cue about the preceding vowel. The one piece of information the spelling is carrying is the one your reading habits are primed to discard.

Same for ん. In `shinkansen` the `n`s look like coda consonants attached to the previous syllable. In しんかんせん they are full, independent, one-beat characters. Two of the six beats in the word are ん.

### 2. It smuggles in English phonemes and English vowel values

`fu` is not English /f/ - ふ is bilabial, made with the lips, not the lip-and-teeth. The `r` of ら行 is not English `r`. `tsu` begins with a cluster English does not allow word-initially. And the vowels are Romance, not English, which is why English speakers read `karaoke` as "carry-okee" instead of か・ら・お・け, and `sake` as "SAH-kee".

Kana do not stop you mispronouncing Japanese. But kana are neutral symbols with no prior English baggage attached, so when you learn か you attach the actual sound to it. When you learn `ka` you attach the actual sound to it *plus* every English word you have ever read containing "ka". The details belong to [../Speaking/readme.md](../Speaking/readme.md); the point here is that romaji is where the contamination enters.

### 3. It erases the kana spelling you need

Romaji collapses distinctions the writing system makes:

- **じ / ぢ and ず / づ (the yotsugana).** Hepburn writes both pairs `ji` and `zu`. But 続く is つづく, and you cannot type it from `tsuzuku`. 気づく is きづく, from 付く. The kana spelling carries the morphology; the romaji throws it away.
- **おう / おお.** Both are a long "o". 通り is とおり; 東京 is とうきょう. Hepburn writes `tōri` and `Tōkyō` - identical treatment, and the U. Tokyo document explicitly forbids using `u` or `h` to mark length (p. 4). So the romaji cannot tell you which kana to type, which dictionary entry to find, or why the word is spelled the way it is.
- **えい.** Pronounced as a long "e", written `ei` (`keizai`, `seifu`) by convention. A transcription that is secretly a transliteration - you have to memorize the exception either way, so you have gained nothing.
- **Particles.** こんにちは is written with は because it *is* the topic particle. `konnichiwa` destroys the evidence and leaves you with an arbitrary string.

### 4. It makes morphology invisible

This is the most expensive one, because it costs you months of grammar confusion.

| Plain | Polite | Negative | Pattern in kana | Pattern in romaji |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 立つ たつ | 立ちます たちます | 立たない たたない | つ -> ち -> た, all た行 | `tsu` -> `chi` -> `ta`, three unrelated consonants |
| 話す はなす | 話します はなします | 話さない はなさない | す -> し -> さ, all さ行 | `su` -> `shi` -> `sa`, looks irregular |

Japanese godan conjugation is a **column shift within one kana row**. That is the whole rule. Hepburn makes it look like three arbitrary sound changes you must memorize per verb. Kunrei-shiki would show it (`tatu` / `tatimasu` / `tatanai`) - which is exactly the U. Tokyo document's one-to-one correspondence argument turned into a learning argument.

And okurigana disappears entirely. 食べます is 食べ + ます - stem plus polite auxiliary, visible on the page as a kanji-kana boundary. `tabemasu` is one undifferentiated string with no seam.

That the seam is not recoverable from romaji is provable from the local source: the U. Tokyo document needs an entire **Word Division** section (p. 7) to legislate it - `kakimasu` not `kaki masu`, `omoshirokatta` not `omoshiro katta`, `kakunin shita` not `kakuninshita`, `kangaete iru` not `kangaeteiru`. Japanese does not use spaces. Every space in romaji is an editorial invention, and the rules are arbitrary enough to need a spec.

### 5. It creates a two-hop reading habit

This one is reasoning, not citation, so weigh it accordingly. If you learn words through romaji, reading kana becomes: see か -> retrieve "ka" -> retrieve meaning. Three steps where natives have one. The intermediate step is a learned reflex, and reflexes are much harder to remove than to avoid. Fluent reading requires the kana -> meaning path to be direct, and every hour spent reading romaji strengthens the detour instead.

### 6. It is a dead end for kanji

No romaji-based path scales past beginner material, for a structural reason: romaji cannot represent kanji, and Japanese leans on kanji to disambiguate a very large homophone load. こうしょう maps to a long list of distinct words (交渉, 考証, 高尚, ...). `koushou` tells you nothing that lets you pick. Furigana - the native solution - is *kana*, not romaji.

Practically: dictionaries and pop-up readers (Yomitan and friends, see the General content guide) key on kana and kanji. The JLPT contains no romaji at any level. Japanese textbooks typically drop romaji by upper beginner, at which point a romaji-dependent learner is locked out of the entire intermediate shelf. There is no version of "getting good at Japanese" that goes through romaji.

## Recommended path (opinionated)

**Know revised Hepburn. Type wapuro. Read kana. Recognize Kunrei-shiki on sight.**

The reasoning:
- **Revised Hepburn** because it is what the world outside Japan is printed in, what the Japanese government uses for names, and what the U. Tokyo document recommends for anyone without an existing preference (p. 2). You need to *read* it - book titles, place names, academic citations, your own legal documents. You do not need to *write* much of it.
- **Wapuro** because it is not optional - it is how input works - and because it usefully forces kana-spelling precision.
- **Kana for all study material** because every argument above.
- **Recognize Kunrei-shiki** so that `Huzisan`, `otya`, `zyûyô` in a Japanese-authored paper or an older textbook read as "different system", not "typo". Recognition costs you ten minutes with the table above. Learning to *write* it costs more and buys nothing unless you go into linguistics.

**Who should ignore this:** linguists and anyone working with classical Japanese or historical orthography - go straight to Nihon-shiki (often, though not verifiably, equated with "ISO 3602 strict" - see the caveat above), because reversibility is the whole point of your work. Also see [the counter-argument](#the-counter-argument-fairly-stated).

## Phase-by-phase plan

Framed as "what to do about romaji", since that is the only decision this topic asks of you.

### Phase 0 - hours 0 to 72: use it, deliberately and with a deadline

Say your first phrases out loud from Hepburn. Learn よろしくおねがいします as `yoroshiku onegaishimasu` if that is what gets you talking on day one. **Set the deadline now** - a date, in your calendar, by which romaji is gone. 72 hours is a reasonable target.

Simultaneously: start kana on day one. Not after.

### Phase 1 - week 1 to 2: learn kana, then stop

Go to [../Kana/readme.md](../Kana/readme.md). Both syllabaries, roughly 92 characters, doable in one to two weeks of ordinary effort - faster if you are aggressive about it. This is not the hard part of Japanese; it is the part that *looks* hard from outside.

While you are learning them, keep romaji only as a fallback for characters you have not covered yet, and drop each romaji crutch the moment the corresponding kana sticks.

### Phase 2 - the purge (one sitting, roughly an hour)

Do this as a single deliberate pass, not gradually. Gradual does not happen.

- [ ] **Anki.** Edit your card templates so the front shows kana/kanji and romaji is not on the card at all. Do not merely reorder it - if romaji is on the card you will read it. Delete the field or strip it from the template. (Deck and template specifics: General content guide.)
- [ ] **Dictionary and pop-up reader.** Set reading display to kana, not romaji. Jisho, Yomitan, and most apps have this setting.
- [ ] **Phone and OS input.** Confirm your IME is romaji-input with kana output, and that predictive romaji output is off.
- [ ] **Textbooks.** Switch to kana editions. Where a kana edition exists, use it even if you already own the romaji one.
- [ ] **Music and lyrics.** Replace romaji lyric videos with kana lyrics. Same activity, correct script.
- [ ] **Your own notes.** Stop writing Japanese in Latin letters. If you cannot yet write the kana by hand, write it anyway - see [../Writing/readme.md](../Writing/readme.md).
- [ ] **Flashcard apps and games** with a romaji answer mode - switch the answer mode to kana typing.

### Phase 3 - handling romaji-only material

You will own good books in the wrong format. This repo has a real example: [80-20 Japanese, romaji edition](<../Vocabulary/80-20 Japanese/80-20 japanese book romaji edition.pdf>). The content is a solid, clearly-explained beginner grammar reference. The format is a liability past week two.

Three options, in order of preference:

1. **Find the kana edition.** The file name says "romaji edition", which tells you another edition exists. Do that. Note that this repo's 80-20 cheat sheets *are* the hiragana versions - [sentence structure](<../Vocabulary/80-20 Japanese/cheat sheet/80-20-Japanese_Sentence-Structure-Cheat-Sheet_A4-hiragana.pdf>) and [verb tenses](<../Vocabulary/80-20 Japanese/cheat sheet/80-20-Japanese_Verb-Tenses-Cheat-Sheet_Hiragana.pdf>) - so you can use those directly.
2. **Read the explanations, rewrite the examples.** Take the English prose for what it is good at, and hand-convert every example sentence into kana/kanji yourself. This is slower and it is also excellent practice - you are drilling kana spelling, okurigana, and yotsugana simultaneously. If you cannot convert a sentence, you have found a gap.
3. **Drop it.** If a resource is romaji-only and has no kana path, and you have a kana alternative, the kana alternative wins even if it is slightly worse material. Format beats content here.

Never do the fourth thing: keep reading the romaji edition because it is easier. That is the trap the whole guide is about.

### Phase 4 - permanent state

Romaji appears in your life as: keyboard input, your name on documents, station signs, and book titles in English. It does not appear in your study materials ever again.

## The counter-argument, fairly stated

The recommendation above takes a side, but the other side is not unreasonable. Here is their case, honestly:

**1. Short-horizon, speaking-only goals.** If you have two weeks before a holiday in Japan and you want thirty useful phrases, learning 92 characters first is a bad allocation. Buy a Hepburn phrasebook, learn to say the phrases, have a good trip. Nothing in this guide applies to you.

**2. Some adults genuinely do better with a longer on-ramp.** Learners with dyslexia or visual processing differences, or learners returning to study after decades, sometimes find a wall of unfamiliar glyphs genuinely blocking rather than merely uncomfortable. A slower transition that keeps them studying beats a fast one that makes them quit. The goal is still kana; the timeline is negotiable.

**3. 80-20 Japanese made a deliberate, defensible choice.** Its value proposition is grammar explanation - what to prioritize and why. Publishing a romaji edition lets a day-one reader engage with the grammar *immediately* rather than after two weeks of kana drills. That is a legitimate product decision about sequencing, not laziness, and the fact that a kana edition also exists shows the author understood the trade-off. The Japan Switch guide makes a related argument: romaji plus *audio* can build a decent accent early, because you are matching letters to sounds you are actually hearing rather than guessing.

**4. Romanization is the correct professional tool in several fields.** Library cataloguing, citation, historical linguistics, and machine transliteration all need Latin-script representations. "Drop romaji" is advice for learners, not a claim that romanization is useless.

**When to make an exception:** if you are on day three, the kana wall is what stands between you and quitting, and a week of romaji keeps you in the game - take the week. A mediocre plan you follow beats an optimal plan you abandon. But write the deadline down, and hold it.

## Common pitfalls

- **Treating romaji as a script to master.** It is a notation, not a writing system. There is no level of romaji skill worth acquiring beyond "can read it".
- **Mixing systems in your own notes.** Writing `shi` but `tu`, or `chi` but `sya`. Pick one and be consistent, or better, stop writing romaji.
- **Letting wapuro spellings into your prose.** `Keihinn`, `juuyou`, `konnnichiwa`. Correct as keystrokes, wrong in every published system. The U. Tokyo document flags this drift specifically (p. 2).
- **Assuming Hepburn is universal.** Then being derailed by `Huzisan`, `otya`, `zyûyô` in a Japanese-authored source, or `sempai` / `Shimbashi` in traditional-Hepburn material.
- **Inferring kana from a sign.** 新橋 is signed `Shimbashi` but the kana is しんばし, with ん and ば. Signage is wayfinding, not orthography.
- **Guessing yotsugana in a dictionary.** Searching `tsuzuku` for 続く and finding nothing useful, because the kana is つづく. This will bite you until you stop searching in romaji.
- **Losing macrons silently.** `Tōkyō` pasted into a form becomes `Tokyo` and the vowel length is gone with no error message. If length matters, write kana.
- **Keeping romaji in Anki "just in the reading field".** It will become the thing you actually read. Remove it from the template, not just from the front of the card.
- **Believing `fu` is /f/ and `r` is /r/.** See [../Speaking/readme.md](../Speaking/readme.md).
- **Thinking ん is two things because you type `nn`.** It is one mora, one character, one beat.
- **Overcorrecting into refusing to type romaji.** Kana input (かな入力) exists but is a specialist choice; romaji input is the normal, correct workflow for nearly everyone. Typing romaji and reading kana is not hypocrisy, it is the right architecture.

## Measuring progress

Progress on this topic is measured by absence. You are done when:

- [ ] You can read a kana-only sentence at normal speed **without any Latin letters appearing in your head**. This is the single test that matters.
- [ ] Seeing Japanese written in romaji feels *slower* than kana, and mildly irritating.
- [ ] You can type any word you can hear, choosing づ vs ず and おう vs おお correctly, without trial and error.
- [ ] You can count the morae in しんかんせん and とうきょう instantly (6 and 4).
- [ ] You can read `Huzisan` or `zyûyô` and immediately identify it as Kunrei-shiki rather than assuming an error.
- [ ] You no longer need romaji-annotated lyrics to sing along.
- [ ] You can explain, unprompted, why 立つ -> 立ちます is one rule and not three. If you cannot explain this in your own words, go back to [section 4 of the case against romaji](#4-it-makes-morphology-invisible) - you have read it, not learned it.
- [ ] Your study material contains zero romaji, and you did not have to think about it this week.

**Anti-metric:** "I know all three romanization systems by heart" measures nothing. Recognition is the goal; memorization is a hobby.
