# Reading

![Reading](<../../Assets/Reading-logo.webp>)

> **TL;DR**
>
> - Reading is the highest-leverage skill for most self-taught learners: it is self-paced, infinitely available, lookup-friendly, and it is where vocabulary you "learned" in Anki actually turns into vocabulary you *know*. It is also the skill that quietly lets you skip listening and speaking, which is a trap - keep those running in parallel.
> - There are exactly two modes, and the whole argument is about the ratio between them: **intensive reading** (short text, look up everything, parse every sentence) and **extensive reading / 多読** (large volume of easy text, minimal lookup, read for the story).
> - Start reading **much earlier than feels reasonable** - as soon as you have kana plus a few weeks of grammar. Graded readers exist precisely so that "too early" is not a real category.
> - Recommended path: **run both modes every day, in a ratio that shifts from roughly 70/30 intensive-heavy at the start to 20/80 extensive-heavy by the time you are reading native material.** Climb the [reading ladder](#the-reading-ladder) rung by rung instead of jumping to what you love.
> - The two things nobody resolves for beginners, and which this guide takes seriously: **when to look a word up and when to let it go**, and **how to read a sentence whose every word you already know**.

---

## What this is and why it matters

"Reading" sounds like one skill. It is at least five, stacked, and they fail independently:

1. **Decoding** - turning 猫 into ねこ, turning かった into a word. Owned by [Kana](../Kana/readme.md) and [Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md).
2. **Word recognition** - knowing that 気持ち is a word and that it means something specific. Owned by [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md).
3. **Segmentation** - finding the word boundaries *at all*, in a script with no spaces. Nobody owns this but reading.
4. **Parsing** - assembling those words into a proposition, including nested clauses with no relative pronoun to warn you. Grammar knowledge is the input; the skill of doing it at speed is reading.
5. **Discourse tracking** - holding who is speaking, who the unstated subject is, and what pronoun-free Japanese is referring to across three pages.

Layers 1 and 2 are trainable in isolation with flashcards. Layers 3, 4 and 5 are only trainable by reading. This is the single best argument for reading early: no amount of deck-grinding touches the top of the stack.

**Why reading specifically, over the other skills:**

- **It is self-paced.** Audio moves at the speaker's speed and punishes you for a half-second of hesitation. Text waits. For a beginner this difference is enormous - it is the difference between comprehensible input and noise.
- **It is infinitely available and free.** There is more Japanese text in existence than you could read in a hundred lifetimes, at every difficulty level, and a large fraction of it is one click away.
- **It is lookup-friendly.** You cannot pause a conversation to mouse over a word. You can pause a page. This makes reading the only skill where the unknown-word problem has a cheap solution.
- **It is where vocabulary consolidates.** A word learned in Anki is a fragile association between a form and a gloss. A word met five times in five different sentences in three different books is knowledge. SRS gets words into the building; reading gives them furniture. See [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md).
- **It compounds fastest.** Reading speed and reading volume feed each other. Once you can read 30 pages an hour instead of 5, your daily exposure sextuples at constant effort.

**The honest counterpoint, which you should take seriously.** Reading-heavy learners reliably end up with a specific, embarrassing profile: they can follow a novel and cannot order coffee. The failure modes are concrete, not vague:

- **You learn words in the wrong shape.** If you have only ever seen 雰囲気 on a page, you may not recognise ふんいき in speech, and you will probably mispronounce it. Reading does not install pitch accent or mora timing. See [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md).
- **You never train real-time processing.** Text lets you re-read a clause four times. Speech does not. That re-reading habit is invisible to you and catastrophic in conversation.
- **Written and spoken Japanese are genuinely different registers.** Contractions (ちゃう、じゃん、っつーの), sentence-final particle stacking, dropped particles, and dialect show up in speech and in manga dialogue, and barely at all in prose. See [Listening](../Listening/readme.md).

So: reading is the *engine*, not the whole car. The correct move is not "read less", it is "read a lot and keep a listening habit running the entire time".

## Where it fits in the journey

**Prerequisites - real ones:**

- **Kana, read without decoding letter by letter.** You do not need speed, but if ふ still takes you a beat, fix that first. See [Kana](../Kana/readme.md).
- **Two to six weeks of grammar.** Enough to recognise は、が、を、に、で、の、と、から, the て-form, past tense, plain vs polite, and negation. That is roughly the first third of Tae Kim or Yokubi. Without this, a sentence is a pile of nouns. See [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md).
- **A few hundred words.** Not thousands. A few hundred plus furigana plus pictures is enough for rung 1. See [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md).
- **A lookup tool, installed and working.** Yomitan in a browser, plus a phone dictionary. Installation, dictionary choice and font selection all live in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) - do that before Phase 1, because the friction of "I'll set it up later" is what kills reading habits.
- **Not required:** finishing a kanji deck. Waiting until you "know enough kanji" to start reading is the most expensive mistake in this guide. Furigana material exists specifically so you can read before you can read kanji.

**What reading unlocks:**

- **Sentence mining**, which is the main engine of intermediate vocabulary growth. You cannot mine what you are not consuming. See [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md).
- **The JLPT reading section**, which is a timed reading-comprehension test and rewards exactly the extensive-reading skills below. Test format and strategy live in [JLPT](../JLPT/readme.md).
- **Kanji consolidation through use.** Characters you meet in context stop needing mnemonics. See [Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md).
- **Actual access to the culture without a translation filter** - manga, visual novels, novels, game text, signs, menus, forms, Twitter. This is usually the real goal hiding behind "I want to learn Japanese".

## The schools of thought

### 1. Intensive reading (精読)

Take a short passage. Look up every unknown word. Parse every sentence until you can state exactly what it says and why the grammar produces that meaning. Typical unit: a paragraph, a page, or one manga chapter. Typical pace: 15 to 45 minutes for a page or two.

- **Pros:** builds parsing accuracy, which nothing else builds. Exposes the grammar you only *think* you know. Produces mining material. Gives you unambiguous evidence of comprehension, so you cannot fool yourself. Best possible use of a *hard* text you care about.
- **Cons:** brutally slow. Low volume means low exposure per hour, and volume is what drives automaticity. Exhausting, so it is easy to abandon. Trains a stop-at-every-unknown reflex that later becomes your bottleneck. Done exclusively, you can spend a year on 200 pages.
- **Best for:** the first months, when you genuinely do not have the grammar to guess; any text above your level that you have decided to fight; JLPT preparation; and mining sessions.

### 2. Extensive reading (多読 / tadoku)

Read a large volume of *easy* text, fast, for content. The Japanese tadoku movement formalises this as four rules:

1. **やさしいレベルから読む** - read material that is easy for you now, not aspirational.
2. **辞書は引かない** - do not use a dictionary.
3. **わからないところは飛ばす** - skip what you do not understand.
4. **つまらなければ他の本を読む** - if a book is boring, quit it and pick another.

An honest evaluation of those four rules, because they are usually presented as scripture:

- Rule 1 is **correct and under-applied**. Almost everyone reads too hard. "Easy" should mean you understand the sentence *before* you finish reading it.
- Rule 2 is **correct as a session rule, wrong as a life rule**. Inside an extensive session, zero lookups is right - a dictionary destroys flow and flow is the whole product. But a learner who never looks anything up grows vocabulary very slowly at low coverage levels. The resolution is not "sometimes break rule 2"; it is to run a *separate* intensive session where lookup is the point.
- Rule 3 is **correct and psychologically hard**. Skipping feels like cheating. It is the actual skill: native readers skip constantly. But it fails silently if your coverage is too low, because then you are skipping the load-bearing words and hallucinating a plot. Rule 3 only works when rule 1 is obeyed.
- Rule 4 is **correct and the most-ignored**. Finishing a boring book has zero pedagogical value and a large motivational cost. Abandon freely. The only exception is abandoning a book because it is *hard* rather than boring, when you have nothing easier queued - then you are not being selective, you are quitting.

- **Pros:** builds speed, automaticity, segmentation and tolerance for ambiguity - all the things intensive reading does not. High volume means high exposure. It is enjoyable, so it is sustainable. It is the only way to reach the point where reading stops being work.
- **Cons:** requires material at your level, which is scarce and, early on, boring. Gives weak feedback - you can "read" 100 pages and understand 40% without noticing. Fossilises wrong guesses: if you decide さっぱり means one thing and never check, that error is now permanent. Grows vocabulary slowly below about 95% coverage.
- **Best for:** everyone, every day, from week three onward; the dominant mode from intermediate on; the cure for the mid-intermediate slump.

### 3. Textbook and graded-course reading

Read the 読解 passages in a structured textbook, with pre-taught vocabulary, comprehension questions and an answer key. This is what *Tanoshiku Yomou* in this folder is.

- **Pros:** level-controlled, so rule 1 is handled for you. Comprehension questions give you *external* verification, which neither mode above provides. Teaches non-narrative reading (timetables, ads, pamphlets, letters, graphs) that you will genuinely need in Japan and will never meet in manga. Vocabulary is pre-glossed, so lookup friction is near zero.
- **Cons:** finite, and you will exhaust it. Register is neutral-to-formal, so it does not prepare you for dialogue. Content is chosen for pedagogy, not pleasure. Answer-key reading can become test-taking rather than reading.
- **Best for:** Phase 1 and 2. Also the best available answer to "how do I know I actually understood that?"

### 4. Mining-first reading (read to farm cards)

Read specifically to extract i+1 sentences into Anki. Comprehension of the whole text is secondary; card yield is the metric.

- **Pros:** converts reading into measurable vocabulary growth. Keeps your deck full of words from material you actually care about, which is the single biggest predictor of retention. This is the engine of the immersion method and it works.
- **Cons:** it is not reading. At high mining rates you process 3 pages an hour and build zero fluency. It is easy to end up with a 6,000-card deck and no ability to read a page, because you spent your reading time in the card editor. The pipeline belongs to [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md); treat it as a *tool used during* intensive sessions, not a mode of its own.
- **Best for:** the intensive slot, capped hard (see the routine table).

### 5. Read-only-what-you-love (domain immersion)

Ignore levels. Read the thing you actually want to read, from day one, however painful. Motivation is the scarce resource; protect it at all costs. Narrow to one domain (one author, one genre, one series) so vocabulary repeats.

- **Pros:** the domain argument is genuinely strong - within a single series, vocabulary, register and character voice repeat enormously, so effective coverage rises much faster than general coverage. Motivation stays high because you are reading the real thing. And there is no substitute for wanting to turn the page.
- **Cons:** at low coverage the material is not input, it is decryption. Two hours per page is not reading. The failure mode is specific and common: you attempt your favourite series, hit 40 unknowns a page, conclude you are bad at Japanese, and read nothing for six months.
- **Best for:** intermediate onward, where it becomes close to optimal. And, in a controlled dose, from the very beginning - one page a week of the thing you love, as fuel.

### Side by side

| School | Volume per hour | Builds | Fails at | Right dose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Intensive (精読) | Very low | Parsing, accuracy, mining | Speed, automaticity, endurance | 20-30 min/day, always |
| Extensive (多読) | High | Speed, segmentation, ambiguity tolerance | Accuracy, new vocabulary at low coverage | 20 min/day rising to most of your time |
| Textbook 読解 | Low | Verified comprehension, non-narrative formats | Register, enjoyment, scale | Phases 1-2, then archive |
| Mining-first | Very low | Vocabulary growth, deck relevance | Fluency, reading itself | Capped inside the intensive slot |
| Read-what-you-love | Varies wildly | Motivation, domain coverage | Level control, early comprehension | 1 page/week early, dominant later |

## Recommended path (opinionated)

**Run intensive and extensive reading as two separate daily slots, with different rules, and shift the ratio over time.**

That is the whole recommendation. The reason it is not "pick one" is that the two modes build disjoint skills, and the reason it is not "blend them" is that blending produces the worst of both: a session where you look up half the words is too slow to build speed and too sloppy to build accuracy.

The two slots have *different rules*, and keeping them separate is the point:

| | Intensive slot | Extensive slot |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Material | At or slightly above your level | Clearly below your level |
| Lookup | Yes, freely | No. None. Zero. |
| Mining | Yes, capped at 2-5 cards | Never |
| Goal | Understand this page completely | Finish these pages and enjoy them |
| Timer | Optional | **Mandatory** - it enforces flow |
| Re-reading a sentence | Encouraged | Forbidden |

**The ratio, by stage:**

| Stage | Intensive : Extensive | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Months 0-3 | 70 : 30 | You lack the grammar to guess usefully. Guessing at 70% coverage is hallucinating. |
| Months 3-9 | 50 : 50 | Grammar is broad enough that context does real work. Build the habit of not stopping. |
| Months 9-24 | 30 : 70 | Coverage is high enough that volume is now the binding constraint. |
| Year 2+ | 20 : 80 or less | Intensive becomes a tool you deploy on hard passages, not a daily ritual. |

**Two non-negotiables layered on top:**

1. **Climb the ladder in order.** Skipping rungs is the most common way to stall. See [The reading ladder](#the-reading-ladder).
2. **One page a week of the thing you actually want to read, from day one.** Not as study - as a benchmark and as fuel. Watch it get easier. This is how you keep the ladder from feeling like a detour.

**Ignore this recommendation if:** there is a JLPT date under three months away (go intensive-heavy and read past papers - see [JLPT](../JLPT/readme.md)); the reader is a heritage speaker or comes from a kanji-using language, in which case rungs 1-3 can be skipped almost entirely and the bottleneck is grammar, not decoding; or the goal is conversational and Japan is a month away, in which case reading is not the highest-leverage skill and [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md) is the place to be.

## The lookup problem

This is the practical crux, and most guides either say "look up everything" or "look up nothing" and move on. Both are wrong, and the reason the question is hard is that the right answer changed when Yomitan appeared.

### How Yomitan changed the economics

Looking a word up in a paper dictionary costs 30-60 seconds and requires you to already know the reading, or to do radical lookup. That cost enforced selectivity for free: you only looked up words that mattered.

Yomitan makes a lookup cost roughly half a second and zero cognitive effort. That is an enormous, genuine gain - it is the main reason self-taught reading is viable now in a way it was not in 2005. But removing a cost also removes the discipline that cost was silently providing, and two failure modes appeared:

- **You look up everything, including words you would have correctly guessed.** Guessing from context is a *skill*, and it is trained by being made to do it. Every free lookup is a repetition you did not do.
- **You become tool-dependent.** If every page you have ever read had a hover dictionary attached, you cannot read a physical volume, a sign, a menu, a form, or anything on a phone without the extension. Learners discover this the first time they hold a paper book and find they are helpless.

The fix is not to stop using Yomitan. It is the two-slot split: unlimited lookup in the intensive slot, hard zero in the extensive slot. Setup, dictionary selection and Anki integration all live in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>).

### When to look it up

In the **intensive** slot, the default is yes - that is the point of the slot. But even there, get value out of guessing first: form a hypothesis, *then* check. A guess-then-verify lookup is worth several times a blind one, because the verification either confirms a pattern or corrects an error, and both stick.

The real decision rules are for the ambiguous middle. Look it up if:

- **It appears twice on the same page.** Repetition means it is load-bearing in this text, and you will meet it again in the next chapter.
- **The sentence collapses without it.** If the unknown is the main verb, or the word the whole sentence is *about*, you cannot skip it and still be reading.
- **It is in dialogue you need for the plot.** Losing the exposition is survivable. Losing "he's the one who did it" is not.
- **You have guessed and you want to check.** Cheap and high-value.
- **It looks like grammar, not vocabulary.** An unfamiliar construction (ざるを得ない, てもらえる, わけではない) will recur in every text forever. Stop and take it to [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md).

Let it go if:

- **It is clearly a proper noun.** Names of people, places, shops, techniques, attacks, spells. Japanese name readings are frequently unguessable even for natives (see [the walls](#proper-nouns-and-name-readings)). Assign it a mental placeholder - "the K-place" - and move on.
- **The sentence still works without it.** If it is an adverb, an intensifier, a decorative adjective, or one item in a list of five, you already have the proposition.
- **It is a sound effect or a mimetic word.** See [Manga-specific reading skills](#manga-specific-reading-skills). Many of these are not in dictionaries at all, and their meaning is usually in the drawing.
- **It is in flavour text.** Signage in the background, a newspaper headline in a panel, a menu on the wall. Read it if you want; skip it without guilt.
- **You are in the extensive slot.** Then this whole section does not apply. The answer is no.

### The three-pass method

For a text you care about and cannot yet read comfortably, this works better than either pure mode:

1. **Pass 1 - flow.** Read the chapter with zero lookups. Get the shape: who is here, what happened, what is the mood. You will miss things. Fine.
2. **Pass 2 - intensive.** Re-read the same chapter with lookups. You will find that half your unknowns resolved themselves in pass 1 from context, and that the ones that did not are the important ones. Mine here, capped.
3. **Pass 3 - flow again, later.** Re-read the same chapter a week later, no lookups. This is where the consolidation happens, and it costs almost nothing because you already know the plot.

Pass 3 is the one everyone skips, and it is the cheapest comprehension gain available anywhere in this guide.

## A brief on what makes a text hard

The ladder below tells you *what* to read, in what order. This section is the layer underneath it: **what actually makes a Japanese text hard**, so that you can pick up something nobody has graded, spend ten minutes on it, and predict whether it is for you.

The claim this section exists to install: **difficulty is not a number, and it is not a property of the text alone.** It is a handful of axes that vary independently, measured against one specific reader. Two texts of identical length and identical word count can differ enormously in what they cost you, and the metric everybody reaches for first - how much kanji is on the page - turns out to be close to useless for the decision you are actually making. There is a measured demonstration of that below, computed on the 39 native texts sitting in this folder.

Boundaries, so nothing is duplicated: [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) owns coverage thresholds, frequency data and word strata; [Writing](../Writing/readme.md) owns the orthographic conventions themselves; [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md) owns the constructions. This section keeps the *difficulty consequences* and links out for the mechanics.

### The axes of difficulty

Nine things vary, and they vary **independently**. Naming which one is hurting you is the entire diagnostic skill, because the fix is different for each - and because most of them are invisible to any single difficulty number you will ever be shown.

| # | Axis | The easy end | The hard end | Owned by |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | **Lexical coverage** (既知語率) | You know 98%+ of the running words | Below 90%, where it stops being reading and becomes decryption | [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md#coverage-and-comprehension) |
| 2 | **Furigana support** | 総ルビ - every kanji glossed, so vocabulary is decoupled from kanji | None, so an unknown reading is an unlookupable word the moment you are off a screen | [Furigana conventions](#furigana-conventions) |
| 3 | **Script mix** (漢字含有率) | Kana-heavy, but see the segmentation cost below | Kanji-dense, which is slower to decode and *faster* to segment | This section |
| 4 | **Word stratum and register** | 和語-heavy everyday speech | 漢語-heavy institutional prose, keigo, archaic or military register | [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md#stratum-is-register-and-register-is-your-corpus-problem) |
| 5 | **Sentence length and clause nesting** | One predicate per sentence, one clause per comma | Three levels of pre-nominal modification with no relative pronoun to warn you | [The parsing wall](#i-know-every-word-but-not-the-sentence---the-parsing-wall) |
| 6 | **Dialogue versus narration** | Short exchanges, one speaker per balloon, the situation carried by the art | Long descriptive narration, unmarked speaker changes, no pronouns, unstated subjects | [Manga-specific reading skills](#manga-specific-reading-skills) |
| 7 | **Assumed background knowledge** | A plot you already know, a setting from your own life | Invented magic systems, military hierarchies, Meiji social structure, Buddhist or legal vocabulary | [Culture](../Culture/readme.md) |
| 8 | **Dialect and non-standard forms** | Standard 標準語 | Kansai-ben, Tohoku, period slang, invented character dialects, 旧字旧仮名, 古文 | [Listening](../Listening/readme.md) owns the contraction and dialect inventory |
| 9 | **Presentation** | Large horizontal type, clean digital text your tools can reach | Vertical columns, hand-lettering, display faces, poor scans, text no tool can select | [Digital versus physical](#digital-versus-physical-and-getting-text) |

**How to read that table.**

- **Axis 1 dominates everything.** Below roughly 90% coverage, no amount of favourable scoring on the other eight rescues the text. This is why the honest first question about any book is never "is it hard" but "how many words per page do I not know".
- **Axes 2 and 9 are free.** They are properties of the *edition*, not of the work, and you can shop for them. The same novel in a 総ルビ children's edition and in an adult paperback are different difficulties. A mokuro'd scan and a paper volume are different difficulties. Nobody's difficulty rating knows which one you are holding.
- **Axis 3 is not monotonic**, which is the trap. More kanji means more characters you must know, and *also* clearer word boundaries - a wall of kana is harder to segment, not easier. See [no spaces](#no-spaces-segmentation-as-a-skill). So there is no direction in which this axis is simply "better".
- **Axes 4, 7 and 8 are why genre is a weak proxy.** They are the ones a text-statistics model cannot see at all.
- **Axes 5 and 6 are why prose is a step change over manga** at equal coverage, and why visual-novel narration can be much harder than the dialogue in the same game.

Look at what this predicts for *Berserk*, the guide's own worked example at rung 7: archaic register (4), sparse furigana (2), invented and domain-specific terminology (7), long monologues rather than exchanges (6), set in vertical blocks (9). **Five axes hostile at once** - on a title whose individual sentences are not particularly long. That is why it is a rung-7 text and not a rung-4 text, and no single number was ever going to tell you.

### Difficulty is not one number

The metric every learner reaches for is **漢字含有率**, the share of characters that are kanji, because it is the only one you can compute without knowing anything about the reader. Here is what it is actually worth, measured on this folder's own Aozora selection.

For context, from the Japanese side: 稲積 and 大野, writing in 『漢字・日本語教育研究』第6号 ([PDF](https://www.kanken.or.jp/project/data/investigation_aid_2016_a2.pdf)), set their authoring tool's "kanji balance" bands from the BCCWJ corpora, and report the per-corpus averages behind them.

| BCCWJ corpus | Sentences | Mean 漢字含有率 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| 教科書 school textbooks | 8,736 | 34.2% |
| 新聞 newspapers | 5,412 | 41.4% |
| 白書 government white papers | 32,544 | 47.8% |

Their tool then labels a text 「漢字が少なすぎる」 below 10%, 「漢字が少ない」 below 20%, 「ちょうど良い」 below 45%, 「漢字が多い」 below 50%, and 「漢字が多すぎる」 above that. Note what those bands are for: **judging whether a native writer's prose looks normal.** They are not a learner difficulty scale, and the authors' own closing caveat is that simply kana-ising words to push the kanji rate down, or chopping sentences shorter, is *not sufficient* to make a text easier to understand.

Now the same measure on the 39 texts in [this folder's Aozora selection](<./Aozora Bunko/>):

| Text | 漢字含有率 | Furigana coverage | This folder's honest level |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| [くまのこ](<./Aozora Bunko/02 - Childrens Stories (Niimi Nankichi)/Kuma no Ko.html>) (Niimi) | 0.4% | none needed | The easiest native prose here |
| [浦島太郎](<./Aozora Bunko/01 - Folk Tales (Kusuyama Masao)/Urashima Taro.html>) (folk tale) | 12.3% | **26%** | Easy - but far less supported than its siblings |
| [セロ弾きのゴーシュ](<./Aozora Bunko/03 - Miyazawa Kenji/Cello Hiki no Goshu.html>) (Miyazawa) | **13.6%** | 13% | Rung 4 to 6 |
| [風の又三郎](<./Aozora Bunko/03 - Miyazawa Kenji/Kaze no Matasaburo.html>) (Miyazawa) | 20.3% | **6%** | Dialect-heavy. Hard |
| [一寸法師](<./Aozora Bunko/01 - Folk Tales (Kusuyama Masao)/Issun Boshi.html>) (folk tale) | **22.6%** | **93%** | Genuinely easy |
| [手袋を買いに](<./Aozora Bunko/02 - Childrens Stories (Niimi Nankichi)/Tebukuro o Kai ni.html>) (Niimi) | **24.0%** | 12% | The easy end of the folder |
| [人間失格](<./Aozora Bunko/05 - Dazai Osamu/Ningen Shikkaku.html>) (Dazai) | 28.4% | **3.5%** | ~N1 |
| [こころ](<./Aozora Bunko/06 - Natsume Soseki/Kokoro.html>) (Soseki) | 29.7% | 15% | The hardest thing in this repo |
| [夢十夜](<./Aozora Bunko/06 - Natsume Soseki/Yume Juya.html>) (Soseki) | 33.1% | 22% | Among the hardest here |
| [蜜柑](<./Aozora Bunko/04 - Akutagawa Ryunosuke/Mikan.html>) (Akutagawa) | **35.8%** | 16% | Early rung 8 |

> **Method**, so you can reproduce or distrust it. 漢字含有率 is kanji characters as a share of kanji plus hiragana plus katakana, with punctuation, Latin and digits excluded, computed on the body text only and with the furigana readings themselves stripped out first. "Furigana coverage" is the share of kanji *characters* that carry a ruby gloss. Aozora's ruby reflects whatever the base print edition printed, so this measures the edition in this folder, not the work in the abstract.

Four things fall out, and every one of them is bad news for the metric:

1. **The ordering is wrong where it matters.** セロ弾きのゴーシュ (13.6%) has barely more than half the kanji density of 手袋を買いに (24.0%), and [sources.md](./sources.md) is blunt that the Niimi is beginner-accessible while the Miyazawa is rung-4-to-6 material that is "the most commonly mis-sold author for learners". 一寸法師 at 22.6% is *denser* than 風の又三郎 at 20.3% and is unambiguously the easier read. **On the comparison you actually face - two texts in the same folder, which do I open - kanji rate gets it backwards.**
2. **Furigana coverage is the axis that really separates the easy end here, and nobody quotes it.** Ten of the twelve folk tales carry ruby on 82-93% of their kanji. Everything from Miyazawa onward sits at 3.5-22%. That is a difference in *kind*: at 93% coverage you can read the text without being able to read the kanji, and at 3.5% you cannot. It is also invisible to every tool in the next section.
3. **Dense furigana does not mean graded material.** 夢十夜 carries more ruby per 1,000 characters (49) than any Niimi or Miyazawa text in this folder, and it sits in the Soseki folder at the very top of the ladder. In literary prose, heavy ruby means the words are obscure, not that the publisher was being kind.
4. **The whole folder is 「ちょうど良い」 or below.** Not one of the 39 texts reaches the newspaper average of 41.4%; exactly one (蜜柑) exceeds the textbook average. By kanji rate, こころ is lighter going than a broadsheet. The honest reading of that is not that the metric is broken but that it is measuring something else: **漢字含有率 is a decent proxy for register and a poor proxy for learner difficulty.** It will reliably tell 白書 from a picture book. It will not tell you which of two children's stories to open.

If you want the number anyway it is two lines of script - count the kanji characters, divide by the count of kanji plus hiragana plus katakana, and ignore punctuation, Latin and digits so the answer does not depend on typography. Knowing how cheap that is to compute should also tell you how much it can be worth.

One trap, because it biases the answer in the worst possible direction: **strip the furigana before you count.** Ruby readings are kana, so counting them inflates the denominator, and the inflation is largest precisely on the most heavily glossed material. Counting the ruby drops 一寸法師 from 22.6% to 16.5% but こころ only from 29.7% to 27.4% - so the error makes graded material look kana-heavier than it is and squeezes the gap you were trying to measure.

### What the reader brings

The nine axes are properties of the text. The other half of difficulty is you, and this is why a shared difficulty number can never be more than a shortlist generator.

- **Coverage is a relation, not a text property.** The same page is 99% coverage for one reader and 85% for another. Every number in this section that does not know your vocabulary is measuring the text and calling it difficulty.
- **Your script background flips the sign on axis 3.** For a reader coming from Chinese or from a heritage background, high kanji density is *help*: more of the page is already meaningful and word boundaries are marked. For everyone else it is cost. This is the same fact as the guide's own caveat that such readers can skip rungs 1 to 3 almost entirely - and it means there is no single correct kanji rate, only a correct one for a given reader.
- **Background knowledge is the cheapest difficulty reduction available.** It is precisely why [Project Sugita Genpaku](./sources.md) is in this folder: you already know the plot of *The Time Machine*, and that carries you through language you could not otherwise handle. Re-reading works on the same mechanism, which is why [pass 3](#the-three-pass-method) is so cheap.
- **Format hours are not vocabulary.** Vertical columns, 明朝 at small sizes, hand-lettered effects, a mediocre scan - each costs you speed until you have hours in the format, and no amount of studying fixes it. Learners routinely misread this discomfort as not knowing enough Japanese.
- **Tolerance for ambiguity is trainable and it is the thing the extensive slot trains.** Two readers at identical coverage will report wildly different difficulty for the same book, because one stops at every unknown and the other does not.

### Measuring a text before you commit

There are real tools. None of them can see more than two or three of the nine axes, so the right use is **narrowing a shortlist and rejecting things**, never deciding.

| Method | Cost | What it sees | What it cannot see |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Read three pages and count the unknowns** | 10 minutes | All nine axes, plus the thing no tool can see at all: whether you want to keep turning the page | Nothing. This is the best method and it is free |
| **[jpdb](https://jpdb.io/) per-title data** | Free, needs an honest known-word list | Per-title word counts, unique words, unique kanji, mean sentence length, a difficulty score and - unusually good design - a **peak difficulty** figure for the hardest passages rather than the average. Its real killer feature is the pre-read vocabulary list | Register, dialect, how much of the meaning the artwork is carrying, which edition you hold. The difficulty number is, per its own FAQ, "automatically generated by a machine learning model", so it is a single scalar collapsing all nine axes. Check whether any percentage you are shown is over unique words or over running words before you believe it |
| **[jReadability](https://jreadability.net/)** | Free, paste the text | Scores 0.5 to 6.5 over six bands, where **higher is easier** (5.5-6.5 lower-elementary down to 0.5-1.5 upper-advanced), from mean words per sentence plus the 漢語, 和語, verb and particle proportions. So it does see axes 4 and 5, which nothing else in this table does | It has no idea who you are, and no coefficient for furigana, script mix, dialect or domain. It was fit on textbooks and BCCWJ, so what it really answers is "how much does this resemble a graded textbook passage" |
| **漢字含有率** | Two lines of script | Script mix, and by proxy register | Everything else, as demonstrated above |
| **[Reading Tutor](https://chuta.cegloc.tsukuba.ac.jp/) (リーディング・チュウ太)** | Free | Vocabulary- and kanji-level checkers that band a pasted text's words by difficulty - the closest thing to a per-text 未知語 report that does not need an account | Honest caveat: it moved from Tokyo International University to Tsukuba on its creator's retirement, was last updated around 2021, and has visibly stale links. Useful, not maintained |
| **Community gradings** | Free | Aggregated human opinion, which *does* see register and dialect | Aggregated opinion is informative and systematically noisy. The [repo's own copies](#digital-versus-physical-and-getting-text) and their provenance caveats are covered later in this guide |
| **[JLPT official practice passages](<./JLPT Official Practice Workbook 2018 - Reading/>)** | Free, 20 minutes | Your own level, against the only officially calibrated passages in this repo | A text you are considering. This is a ruler for *you*, not for the book |

**The verdict.** Use the tools to build a shortlist and to throw things out. Then **sample three pages and count the unknowns**, which is what the rest of this guide tells you to do anyway. Any measurement that does not include your own vocabulary is measuring the text, and difficulty is a relation between a text and a reader - so a number that knows only one of the two was never going to answer the question.

One corollary worth stating, because it is the most common way this goes wrong: **a text can be too easy on every axis and still be the right choice.** The extensive slot wants material that scores embarrassingly low, and the reason learners cannot find anything at their level is usually that they are rejecting it for being beneath them rather than because it is not there.

## The reading ladder

This is the core of the guide. The ladder exists because the single strongest predictor of whether you keep reading is whether the material is at your level, and the single most common failure is starting three rungs too high.

**Read this caveat before the ladder, and believe it:** difficulty varies *enormously within each category*. Some manga are harder than some novels. Some light novels are harder than some literary fiction. A slice-of-life seinen title can be gentler than a shounen fantasy aimed at twelve-year-olds, because the fantasy invents vocabulary and the slice-of-life does not. Genre is a weak proxy for difficulty; **domain vocabulary, register, furigana coverage and text density are the real variables.** The rungs below are ordered by the *typical* case, and you should expect exceptions constantly. Sample before you commit: read three pages, count the unknowns, decide.

The vocabulary pointers below are deliberately loose bands, given as orientation only. [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) owns the actual numbers, the coverage thresholds and the frequency data - go there for anything precise. The one figure worth carrying: **around 95% known-word coverage is where reading becomes possible with effort, and around 98% is where it becomes comfortable.**

### Rung 1 - Graded readers and tadoku material

**Purpose-written, level-controlled text.** Vocabulary and grammar are deliberately restricted to a known set, illustrations carry meaning, and the whole point is that you can read it *now*.

- **Entry:** kana, two to six weeks of grammar, a few hundred words.
- **What:** the [Tadoku](https://tadoku.org/) free graded-reader library (their level-graded books are the best starting material in existence; the catalogue and the free/paid split change, so check the site). Locally: [*Tanoshiku Yomou I*](<./Tanoshiku Yomou Read Japanese/Tanoshiku Yomou I.pdf>), which puts furigana on **every** kanji and pre-glosses all new vocabulary in English. Also locally, and a better fit for the very first weeks than *Tanoshiku Yomou*: [23 Tadoku free readers](<./Tadoku Free Graded Readers/>) spanning Start to Level 4, and [7 KC よむよむ readers](<./KC Yomuyomu (Japan Foundation Kansai)/>) at CEFR A1-A2/B1.
- **Roughly:** your first few hundred to ~1,000 words.
- **Ready to move up when:** you can read a level-appropriate graded reader start to finish at a steady pace, without stopping, understanding the story rather than reconstructing it.

This is the best possible starting point and **it is the rung almost every self-taught learner skips.** They skip it because graded readers feel babyish, and the alternative they choose is usually "nothing at all for six months". If you read one thing from this guide, read this rung.

### Rung 2 - Furigana-heavy manga for young readers

**Shounen and shoujo titles with full or near-full furigana.** Serialisations aimed at elementary and middle-school readers generally furigana everything; the young-reader magazines are your friend here.

Two barriers disappear at once, which is why this rung works so well:

- **Visual context.** The picture tells you what is happening. You are not deriving meaning from text alone, you are *confirming* it. That is a much easier task and it is exactly how children acquire reading.
- **Furigana lets you read words you cannot yet read in kanji.** This is a genuine accelerator and it is underrated. With furigana you can learn 冒険 as ぼうけん from context, in a sentence, before you have ever studied either character. You are decoupling vocabulary acquisition from kanji acquisition, which means both can proceed in parallel instead of in series.

- **Entry:** rung 1 cleared; you can read a graded reader fluidly.
- **Roughly:** ~1,000-2,000 words.
- **Ready to move up when:** you can read a chapter with fewer than about 5 lookups per page and follow the plot without the English release.

### Rung 3 - NHK News Web Easy

[NHK News Web Easy](https://www3.nhk.or.jp/news/easy/) publishes simplified versions of real news: full furigana, shortened sentences, restricted vocabulary, difficult terms glossed inline, articles of a few hundred characters, and an audio reading of most articles.

- **Pros:** short (one article is a complete, finishable unit, which is motivationally excellent), free, updated daily, and the audio gives you a listening rep on text you have just understood - one of the highest-value five-minute exercises available.
- **Cons:** **narrow register.** News Japanese is formal, noun-heavy, passive-heavy, and full of institutional vocabulary (政府、発表、対策、影響). It does not transfer to dialogue at all. Treat it as a checkpoint and a daily five-minute habit, not as a diet.
- **Entry:** rung 2 in progress. Works fine in parallel with rungs 2 and 4.
- **Roughly:** ~2,000-3,000 words.
- **Ready to move up when:** you can read an article with 5 or fewer lookups and summarise it out loud.

### Rung 4 - Slice-of-life and romance manga

The gentlest large category of native material, for structural reasons worth understanding:

- **Domain vocabulary is bounded.** School, home, food, feelings, part-time jobs. No magic systems, no military hierarchies, no invented terminology.
- **Register is everyday casual** - the same register as the anime you are already hearing, so reading and listening reinforce each other.
- **Visual support is high** and text density per page is low.
- **Dialogue-driven beats description-driven.** This is the important distinction inside the rung: a talky slice-of-life title is dramatically easier than an action or fantasy title of the same nominal age rating, because action panels carry SFX and shouted fragments while fantasy carries invented nouns.

*The Guy She Was Interested In Wasn't a Guy At All* (気になってる人が男じゃなかった) is a genuinely gentle example: short exchanges, contemporary casual register, low text density, and a lot of the storytelling done in the art. Much of the emotional content arrives visually, which means you can miss a line and stay oriented. Titles built on 4-koma or short-scene structures (*Onimai* is in this family) behave similarly - each unit is small and self-contained, so failure is cheap.

- **Entry:** rung 2 cleared.
- **Roughly:** ~3,000-5,000 words.
- **Ready to move up when:** you can finish a volume in under a week, at under 2 lookups per page, and enjoy it rather than study it.

### Rung 5 - Visual novels

Visual novels are an unusually good learner format, and the argument is strong enough to state properly rather than as a preference:

- **They are fully text-based.** Unlike an action game, essentially all content is delivered as text. Nothing is gated behind reflexes or listening.
- **They are usually voiced.** A voiced line is simultaneous reading and listening practice, with the text as a safety net - the single most efficient input format available for a learner. (Note the exception below.)
- **They are self-paced.** Text advances on your click. No pause button needed, no rewind, no lost audio. This is the VN's decisive advantage over anime for a learner.
- **They are text-hookable.** A text hooker such as [Textractor](https://github.com/Artikash/Textractor) extracts the current line to your clipboard, where a texthooker page plus Yomitan gives you instant hover lookup on game text. Setup lives in [General content](<../General content/readme.md>). This turns a closed application into something as lookup-friendly as a web page.
- **Character voice repeats.** A single character's speech patterns, vocabulary and verbal tics recur across tens of thousands of lines. That is domain immersion by construction, and it produces exactly the repetition that makes words stick.
- **They are long enough to build momentum.** A single VN can be dozens of hours of text. You spend that whole time inside one vocabulary domain, which is why people often report a step change in reading ability after finishing their first one.

**Caveats, honestly:** narration in VNs can be considerably harder than the dialogue - descriptive prose, literary flourishes, unusual vocabulary - and the narration is often the unvoiced part. Some works have heavy dialect. And not everything is voiced: *Higurashi*, for example, is in its original release a text-only work rather than a voiced one (voice patches exist for some editions - check what you have). It is also very long, split into arcs, and its first-person narration is dense in places. It is still a good learner target because it is self-paced, hookable, and enormously repetitive within each arc; just do not expect the free listening rep that a voiced VN gives you.

- **Entry:** rung 4 cleared, plus a working text hooker.
- **Roughly:** ~5,000+ words, and it varies hugely by title.
- **Ready to move up when:** you can read a VN route at a rate that feels like playing rather than translating, and finish one.

### Rung 6 - Light novels

The traditional "first real prose" target, and a reasonable one: prose is simple, sentences are short, the books are mostly dialogue with connective narration, and furigana appears on harder words (coverage varies by publisher and by series, so check before buying).

This is also the rung where a real change happens: **you lose the pictures.** Prose has no visual context. Everything you were getting for free from the art, you now have to get from the text. Expect a jolt even if your vocabulary is fine.

*Bunny Girl Senpai* (青春ブタ野郎 series) is a natural target if it is already a favourite - contemporary setting, dialogue-heavy, bounded vocabulary.

- **Entry:** rung 4 or 5 cleared, and specifically: you have tried five pages of prose and survived.
- **Roughly:** ~6,000-10,000 words.
- **Ready to move up when:** you finish a volume and start the next one voluntarily.

### Rung 7 - Seinen and dense manga

Adult-audience manga drops most of the scaffolding: **furigana is sparse or absent**, vocabulary is wider, registers multiply, and panels carry more text.

**Berserk** is the honest worked example here, and it is worth being blunt about because it is a fan favourite, which is exactly what makes it dangerous:

- **Archaic and pseudo-medieval register** - old-fashioned pronouns and copulas, elevated or military speech, formal constructions you will not have met in a textbook.
- **Sparse furigana**, as is normal for seinen serialisation. You need to *read* the kanji, not be told them.
- **Dense, wide vocabulary** - warfare, religion, anatomy, politics, plus invented and archaic terminology.
- **Long, heavy monologues** in blocks of vertical text, which is a different reading task from exchanged dialogue.

This is a qualitative assessment, not a measured ranking - but the direction is not controversial. **Do not make it your first manga.** The specific failure is that you love it, you attempt it at rung 2 level, you hit 40 unknowns a page, and you conclude the problem is you. Put it at rung 7, read one page a week as a benchmark, and watch it become possible.

- **Entry:** rung 6, or rung 5 plus a large kanji base.
- **Roughly:** ~10,000+ words, plus real comfort with unfurigana'd kanji.
- **Ready to move up when:** you can read a dense chapter in one sitting without the lookup tool doing all the work.

### Rung 8 - General native prose

Contemporary novels, non-fiction, essays, long-form journalism, blogs. No furigana, no pictures, full adult vocabulary, author-specific style. The ceiling above this: literary fiction with idiosyncratic prose; pre-war texts with historical orthography (旧字体, historical kana usage - see [Writing](../Writing/readme.md)); classical Japanese (古文); and academic or legal writing, each of which is effectively a separate skill acquired later.

Public-domain prose at [Aozora Bunko](https://www.aozora.gr.jp/) is the standard free source, but note that a large share of its catalogue is pre-war and uses older orthography, so it is not automatically an easy on-ramp. This folder holds [a curated 39-work selection](<./Aozora Bunko/>) chosen to dodge exactly that problem: every text filtered to 新字新仮名, modern kanji forms *and* modern kana usage. Read [sources.md](./sources.md) first for which of them are genuinely accessible and which are merely famous - the short Niimi Nankichi pieces are usable far earlier than this rung, while the Soseki novels sit at the very top of it.

- **Entry:** rung 6 or 7 cleared.
- **Ready to move up when:** there is no up. You are reading.

## Phase-by-phase plan

The phases below map onto the ladder but are organised around *what you do daily*, not what you read.

### Phase 0 - Set up so that reading has no friction

- **Goal:** remove every excuse. One evening of setup.
- **Time:** one session, 1-2 hours.
- **Daily routine:** none yet.
- **Materials:** [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) for Yomitan, dictionaries and a Japanese font that renders kanji correctly (a wrong font makes characters subtly wrong-shaped and is worth fixing early). Download [*Tanoshiku Yomou I*](<./Tanoshiku Yomou Read Japanese/Tanoshiku Yomou I.pdf>). Bookmark [Tadoku](https://tadoku.org/) and [NHK News Web Easy](https://www3.nhk.or.jp/news/easy/). Pick a **benchmark page** now: one page of the hardest thing you eventually want to read, saved somewhere permanent. A page of *Berserk* is a common choice.
- **Done when:** you can hover a Japanese word in your browser and see a definition; you have a graded reader open; and your benchmark page is saved with today's unknown-word count written next to it.

### Phase 1 - First text, with all the training wheels

- **Goal:** read a complete Japanese text to the end. Not comprehend perfectly - *finish*.
- **Time:** weeks 3-10 of study.
- **Daily routine:** 15 min intensive (one *Tanoshiku Yomou I* unit, or one graded reader) + 10 min extensive (re-read yesterday's text, no lookups). Mine at most 2 cards.
- **Materials:** [*Tanoshiku Yomou I*](<./Tanoshiku Yomou Read Japanese/Tanoshiku Yomou I.pdf>) with [its answer key](<./Tanoshiku Yomou Read Japanese/Tanoshiku Yomou I - Kaitou.pdf>); Tadoku level-0 and level-1 books; [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md) running in parallel.
- **Done when:** you have finished 5 graded readers or 5 *Tanoshiku Yomou I* units, checked your answers against the key, and scored most of the comprehension questions correctly on the first attempt.

### Phase 2 - The two-slot habit, and manga reading mechanics

- **Goal:** install the intensive/extensive split as a habit, and learn to physically read a manga page.
- **Time:** months 2-5.
- **Daily routine:** 20 min intensive (3-5 pages of furigana manga, full parse, 2-3 cards) + 15 min extensive (graded readers or easy manga, timer on, zero lookups) + one NHK Easy article with audio, 3x/week.
- **Materials:** a furigana-heavy manga volume; [*Japanese the Manga Way*](<../Grammar/Books/Japanese The Manga Way/Japanese the Manga Way.pdf>) - grammar taught through annotated real manga panels, including panels marked with where to start reading and how furigana sits, which is exactly the mechanical skill this phase is about; the [Manga tab](<../AJATT/saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations/Manga.png>) of the community recommendation spreadsheet for picking titles by difficulty.
- **Done when:** you can read a furigana manga chapter at under 5 lookups per page; you never consciously think about panel order; and you have completed 30 consecutive days with both slots done.

### Phase 3 - Volume, and the first native-level book

- **Goal:** finish a native manga volume, and make extensive reading the larger slot.
- **Time:** months 5-12.
- **Daily routine:** 20 min intensive (mine 3-5 cards) + 25-30 min extensive. Ratio crosses 50/50 during this phase.
- **Materials:** slice-of-life and romance manga (*The Guy She Was Interested In Wasn't a Guy At All* and similar); [*Tanoshiku Yomou II*](<./Tanoshiku Yomou Read Japanese/Tanoshiku Yomou II.pdf>) with [its key](<./Tanoshiku Yomou Read Japanese/Tanoshiku Yomou II - Kaitou.pdf>) - furigana now only on new words, plain-form prose, longer passages, which is the right step up; [jpdb](https://jpdb.io/) for a pre-reading vocabulary list of the specific volume.
- **Done when:** you have finished one complete manga volume in Japanese and could summarise the plot in English without checking anything. Re-count your benchmark page.

### Phase 4 - Long-form: the visual novel or the light novel

- **Goal:** sustain a single long text for tens of hours. This is where reading stops being an exercise.
- **Time:** year 1-2.
- **Daily routine:** 45-60 min in one long-form work, mining 2-5 cards; intensive is now a mode you enter on hard passages rather than a scheduled slot. Keep one short daily extensive read (a manga chapter) so speed keeps growing.
- **Materials:** a VN with [Textractor](https://github.com/Artikash/Textractor) plus a texthooker (setup: [General content](<../General content/readme.md>)), or a light novel in a browser-based ebook reader so Yomitan applies; the [Visual Novels](<../AJATT/saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations/Visual Novels.png>) and [Novels](<../AJATT/saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations/Novels.png>) tabs for difficulty-graded picks.
- **Done when:** you have finished one VN route or one light novel volume, and you started the next thing without being told to.

### Phase 5 - The favourite text, and unassisted reading

- **Goal:** read the thing you started all this for, and read without tools at all.
- **Time:** year 2+.
- **Daily routine:** mostly extensive, in whatever you want. One weekly **unplugged session**: 30 minutes, physical book or lookup tool disabled, no exceptions. One weekly hard-text session on the dense material.
- **Materials:** *Berserk* and whatever else was on the list; [Aozora Bunko](https://www.aozora.gr.jp/) if you go toward prose; the [DJT reading list](<../General content/djtguide.neocities.org/reading list.html>) for further progression ideas.
- **Done when:** your benchmark page reads as a page rather than a puzzle, and you can read a physical volume on a train with no dictionary and stay in the story.

## Manga-specific reading skills

Nobody teaches these, and they are a real part of why a first manga feels impossible even when your vocabulary is adequate.

### Reading order

Japanese manga reads **right to left** at every level of nesting, and you have to internalise all three:

- **Pages:** the spine is on the right. The first page of a volume is at what an English reader calls the back. Page order runs right to left.
- **Panels within a page:** start at the **top right**, move left, then down to the next tier and again from the right.
- **Speech balloons within a panel:** same rule - rightmost first, then leftward. When two balloons are stacked, the upper one usually comes first; when they are diagonal, right-and-up beats left-and-down. This is convention, not law, and artists break it deliberately for effect.
- **Text within a balloon:** vertical, top to bottom, **rightmost column first**. See the next section.

[*Japanese the Manga Way*](<../Grammar/Books/Japanese The Manga Way/Japanese the Manga Way.pdf>) is the best resource in this repo for this, because it teaches grammar on real annotated panels and explicitly labels where to start reading and how the scripts and furigana sit on the page. It also states the fact worth internalising: virtually all manga still use the traditional vertical format, even though modern horizontal text is everywhere else.

Diagnostic: if you ever find yourself confused about who said what, it is usually balloon order, not vocabulary.

### Furigana conventions

- **In vertical text, furigana sits to the right of the character column.** In horizontal text it sits above. Same information, different position - and it takes a while before your eye stops treating it as clutter.
- **Coverage varies by target audience,** and this is the single most useful thing to check before buying a volume. Young-reader serialisations tend to furigana everything; adult titles furigana little or nothing. Flip to a random page in a shop or a preview and look.
- **Furigana is a reading aid you should use and then wean off.** Early on, read it. Later, try to read the kanji first and use the furigana as a check. Covering the furigana with a finger on a re-read is a genuinely effective drill.
- **Furigana is also used for effect.** Manga frequently puts a *different word* in the furigana than the kanji spell - 本気 glossed as マジ, an English word glossed onto a kanji compound, a character's name glossed with a nickname. When furigana and kanji disagree, that is intentional: the kanji give the meaning and the furigana give what is actually said. This confuses learners badly the first few times.

### Sound effects: 擬音語 and 擬態語

This is a genuine wall and it is rarely named.

- **擬音語 (giongo)** imitate sounds: バタン (a door slamming), ゴゴゴ (rumbling), ザーザー (heavy rain), ドキドキ (a pounding heart).
- **擬態語 (gitaigo)** imitate *states*, which English mostly cannot do: キラキラ (sparkling), ぼーっと (spaced out, vacant), ずきずき (throbbing), しんみり (quietly moved). These are not "sound effects" at all; they are a productive part of ordinary Japanese and they appear in speech and prose too, not just in panels.
- There are even conventional effects for the *absence* of sound - シーン for total silence - which tells you how far the system extends beyond literal noise.

Why they are a wall:

- **Many are not in dictionaries**, or are in some dictionaries and not others. They are semi-productive: authors coin new ones, stretch them, and respell them. Yomitan will simply fail on a good fraction of them.
- **They are drawn, not typeset.** Hand-lettered effects are part of the artwork, in stylised or distorted forms, sometimes in unusual scripts. OCR tooling like mokuro is built around speech balloons, so hand-drawn marginal text and effects are largely out of its reach. Cross-reference: [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) for what mokuro does and does not do.
- **The stylisation destroys your character recognition.** A バ stretched across half a page in a jagged display face looks nothing like the バ in your kana chart.

How to handle them: **mostly let them go.** The meaning is almost always in the drawing - that is the entire point of a sound effect. Look one up only when it recurs across a series, and expect to fail sometimes. Over a few volumes you will accumulate the common ones passively, which is the correct acquisition path for this category. Do not mine them into Anki; a deck of onomatopoeia is a bad use of reviews.

### Other manga-specific hazards

- **Handwritten and stylised marginal text.** Small asides scrawled outside balloons, author notes in the gutter, signs and labels drawn into the scene. Often in casual handwriting that shares little with printed kana. Skip freely; they are usually jokes.
- **Heavy contraction and dialect in dialogue.** Manga dialogue is *spoken* Japanese written down: ～てる for ～ている, ～ちゃう for ～てしまう, ～なきゃ, じゃん, ～っつーの, dropped particles, and Kansai-ben or invented character dialects. This is why manga can feel harder than a textbook passage with identical vocabulary. [Listening](../Listening/readme.md) owns the contraction inventory - it is the same set of transformations, so learning them once pays in both skills.
- **Character-voice markers.** Archaic pronouns (わし、おれ様), gendered sentence-final particles, rough or elevated copulas, and role-language stereotypes. These carry characterisation, and once you notice them they become one of the pleasures of reading in Japanese rather than an obstacle.
- **Panel-fragment grammar.** Dialogue in manga is frequently incomplete: trailing-off with ..., subject-only utterances, verbless exclamations. Do not try to parse a fragment as a sentence. [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md) covers what is being elided.

## Vertical text as a reading skill (縦書き)

Manga, most novels, and a great deal of print use **vertical writing: characters run top to bottom, and columns run right to left.** [Writing](../Writing/readme.md) owns the conventions themselves - when vertical is used, punctuation placement, how it interacts with orthography. What belongs here is vertical text as a *reading* skill, because it is one, and it takes hours to acquire.

**The mechanics you need:**

- **Start at the top right of the block.** Read down. When you reach the bottom, move **left** one column and return to the top. Your English-trained eye wants to go right; it will take weeks to stop.
- **Furigana sits to the right of its column.** So a column of kanji has a thin ribbon of kana running alongside it on the right - which means, awkwardly, that the furigana for column N sits in the gap between column N and column N+1, on the side you just came from.
- **On a full page, the reading block starts top-right and ends bottom-left.** Same diagonal as manga panels, which is not a coincidence.
- **Small kana, ー and っ rotate or shift.** The long vowel mark ー becomes a vertical stroke, and small kana sit in the upper-right of their cell rather than the lower-left. Numbers and Latin text either rotate 90 degrees or get set as upright groups.
- **Line breaks land in different places than you expect,** which means a word can be split across a column boundary and your segmentation has to survive it.

**It genuinely feels alien until you have put hours in, and then it stops mattering entirely.** That is the honest description. The first vertical novel page is disorienting in a way that has nothing to do with your Japanese ability, and learners often mistake the discomfort for incomprehension. It is not - it is eye-movement training, and it resolves with volume, not study.

**How to train it deliberately:** read vertically on purpose rather than seeking out horizontal text. Manga is the gentlest vertical trainer because the balloons are short - three or four columns of five characters - so you practise the column-to-column jump hundreds of times per volume with tiny units. Vertical novel pages are the hard version. Do not avoid vertical because it is uncomfortable; the discomfort *is* the exercise.

## The specific walls and how to get through them

### "I know every word but not the sentence" - the parsing wall

This is the defining intermediate experience. Every word is in your deck and the sentence means nothing. The cause is structural: Japanese builds long sentences by nesting modifiers *before* the thing they modify, and it has **no relative pronoun** - no "that", "which" or "who" to mark where a clause starts. The only signal that a clause is modifying a noun is that a plain-form predicate sits immediately in front of that noun.

Compare:

```
私は昨日本を買った。           I bought a book yesterday.        (sentence)
私が昨日買った本               the book I bought yesterday       (modifier + noun)
```

Nothing but word order and the missing final punctuation distinguishes them. Now scale that up three levels of nesting and you have the wall.

**The decomposition algorithm.** Do this explicitly, on paper, until it becomes automatic:

1. **Find the final predicate.** Japanese puts the main verb at the end. That is the sentence's actual claim. Read it first.
2. **Work backwards, not forwards.** Find what that verb's object and subject are, then expand each one.
3. **For every noun, look immediately to its left.** If a plain-form verb or adjective sits directly before it, that is a relative clause modifying it. Bracket it.
4. **Treat こと / の / ところ / わけ / はず as clause packagers.** They turn everything before them into a noun. Bracket the whole thing.
5. **Chop at commas and at て-forms** - these are your clause boundaries.
6. **Rebuild inside-out** and only then translate.

**Worked example.** Take:

```
去年の夏に初めて会ったときに彼女が貸してくれた本を、まだ読み終わっていないことを思い出した。
```

Step 1 - **final predicate**: 思い出した, "remembered". Whatever else happens, the sentence is "I remembered X".

Step 2 - **what is X?** The ことを immediately before it packages a clause as the object. So X = 「まだ読み終わっていないこと」= "the fact that (I) still haven't finished reading (it)".

Step 3 - **finished reading what?** 本を is the object of 読み終わっていない. So: "haven't finished reading the book".

Step 4 - **which book?** Look left of 本. 貸してくれた is a plain-form verb sitting directly in front of a noun: relative clause. 「彼女が貸してくれた」本 = "the book she lent me". (The くれた tells you the direction of the favour - toward the speaker - which is also how you know the unstated subject of 思い出した is "I".)

Step 5 - **lent me when?** 「...会ったときに」. And ときに itself has a relative clause in front of it: 「去年の夏に初めて会った」とき = "the time when we first met, in the summer of last year".

Rebuild:

```
[[去年の夏に初めて会った] ときに [彼女が貸してくれた]] 本を
  まだ読み終わっていない ことを
    思い出した。

"I remembered that I still haven't finished the book she lent me
 when we first met last summer."
```

Three levels of nesting, two relative clauses, one nominalised clause, and not a single relative pronoun to warn you. Once you can run this procedure in your head in two seconds, the wall is gone.

**Aids:** [ichi.moe](https://ichi.moe/) takes a pasted sentence and splits it into glossed words - the right tool for exactly this situation, where you know the words and cannot assemble them. [massif.la](https://massif.la/ja) searches real published Japanese for a pattern, so when you do not believe a construction works the way a guide says, read twenty native examples. [*Making Sense of Japanese*](<../Grammar/Books/Making Sense of Japanese What the Textbooks Don't Tell You/Making Sense of Japanese What the Textbooks Don't Tell You.pdf>) by Jay Rubin is the best single treatment of the specific problems that cause this wall - the "subjectless sentence", は versus が as answers to unasked questions, and the invisible-subject problem - and there is a [greppable text version](<../Grammar/Books/Making Sense of Japanese What the Textbooks Don't Tell You/Making Sense of Japanese - text version.txt>) alongside it. The [80-20 Japanese sentence-structure cheat sheet](<../Vocabulary/80-20 Japanese/cheat sheet/80-20-Japanese_Sentence-Structure-Cheat-Sheet_A4-hiragana.pdf>) is a one-page scaffold for step 1. Conjugation and particle detail: [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md).

### Proper nouns and name readings

Japanese personal and place names have **genuinely unpredictable readings**. The same characters can be read several ways, parents choose readings freely, and place names preserve archaic or local pronunciations. Natives routinely ask how a name is read; business cards exist partly to solve this problem.

**This is not a failure of your study.** Being unable to read a character's surname is not a vocabulary gap. Practical handling:

- Accept a placeholder. "The 神-something girl." Consistency matters more than accuracy - you need to track *who*, not pronounce them.
- Most manga and novels furigana a character's name on first appearance. Note it then; it will not be repeated.
- Do not mine names.
- Do not let an unreadable name stop you. It is the one category where skipping is unambiguously correct.

### No spaces: segmentation as a skill

Japanese does not put spaces between words. Finding the boundaries is a skill you build, not a thing you know. The classic demonstration:

```
ここではきものをぬいでください
```

This parses two ways:

```
ここで  はきもの を ぬいでください   -> "Please take off your footwear here."
ここでは きもの  を ぬいでください   -> "Here, please take off your kimono."
```

Written with kanji, the ambiguity vanishes: 履物 versus 着物. Which is the point - **kanji are the word-boundary markers.** A wall of kana is harder to read than mixed script, not easier, and this is why "I wish they'd just use kana" is a beginner's wish.

How segmentation actually develops: by reading volume, not by study. What you can do deliberately is (a) learn to recognise where okurigana ends and a particle begins, which is grammar; (b) read kana-heavy text (children's books, some VN narration) on purpose, since that is the hard case; and (c) when stuck, use [ichi.moe](https://ichi.moe/) to see the split and then notice what you missed.

### The mid-intermediate slump

Somewhere around the point where you have a few thousand words and real grammar, everything becomes **"almost readable"**. You understand most of every page and finish nothing. It is the most common quitting point.

Why it happens: you are on the wrong side of the coverage gap. At 90-95% coverage you can follow a page with effort but cannot enjoy one, and closing the last few points costs thousands of low-frequency words - see [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) for why that gap is so expensive. Meanwhile every word you learn now buys a rounding error of coverage, so the felt return on study collapses while the effort stays constant.

What actually works:

- **Drop a rung deliberately.** Read something clearly too easy for a week. It rebuilds the feeling of fluency, which is the resource you are short of.
- **Narrow your domain.** Stop reading one volume each of ten series. Read ten volumes of one series, or one long VN. Within-domain coverage rises far faster than general coverage, and this is the single most effective intervention available.
- **Switch the metric from volume to finishing.** Finish something small. A short manga. One VN route. Completion is what restores motivation, not page counts.
- **Re-read.** Something you struggled with three months ago will now feel easy, and that is the only direct evidence of progress you can get.

## Digital versus physical, and getting text

For a learner this is not a matter of taste. It is a large, decisive practical difference.

**Digital text is strictly better for study:**

- **Yomitan works on it.** Hover lookup turns a 30-second dictionary trip into half a second. This is the difference between reading a page and giving up on it.
- **It is searchable.** "Where did this character say that earlier" is answerable.
- **It feeds the mining pipeline directly.** Text and audio go into a card in two clicks. See [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md).
- **Font, size and layout are yours.** You can enlarge a kanji you cannot resolve instead of squinting.

**Physical text is better for building independence:**

- **It forces real reading.** No hover means you must guess, use context, or do radical lookup. That is the skill you need for signs, menus, forms and paper books in Japan.
- **It kills lookup speed**, which is exactly why it is useful in controlled doses. A physical volume is the best available unplugged test.
- **Fewer distractions**, and a volume is a satisfying finishable object.

**A reasonable split is roughly 80% digital, 20% physical, deliberately.** The physical portion is not nostalgia - it is the only thing that prevents total tool dependence, and a learner who has never read a page without Yomitan is in for an unpleasant surprise in a Japanese bookshop.

**Getting text into a readable form.** [General content](<../General content/readme.md>) owns all the setup; here is what each thing buys you as a *reader*:

| Format | Tool | What it gives you |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Scanned manga (images) | [mokuro](https://github.com/kha-white/mokuro) (built on [manga-ocr](https://github.com/kha-white/manga-ocr)) | Produces an HTML page with an invisible, selectable text layer over the artwork, so Yomitan works on manga. The single biggest quality-of-life gain for a manga reader. Balloon text only - hand-drawn effects stay unreadable. |
| Ebooks | A browser-based reader (ttu-reader and similar) | Yomitan applies to the page. Conversion notes are in the DJT [resource guide](<../General content/djtguide.neocities.org/resource guide.html>). |
| Visual novels and games | [Textractor](https://github.com/Artikash/Textractor) + a texthooker page | Extracts the current line so Yomitan can reach it. This is what makes rung 5 viable. |
| Anime subtitles (for the listening side) | [kitsunekko](https://kitsunekko.net/), [jimaku](https://jimaku.cc/) | Japanese subtitle files. Reading subtitles is a hybrid skill; [Listening](../Listening/readme.md) owns it. |

**Choosing what to read next.** Two classes of tool, both useful, both to be taken with salt:

- **[jpdb](https://jpdb.io/)** builds per-work vocabulary and kanji lists and assigns works a difficulty ranking. Its most valuable feature for a reader is the **pre-read vocabulary list**: before starting a volume, study its top unknown words, and you walk in with materially higher coverage. Treat the difficulty numbers as a rough ordering, not a measurement - they are derived from text statistics and cannot see register, dialect, or how much of the meaning the artwork is carrying. Coverage estimates depend entirely on how honestly you have marked words known.
- **[Learn Natively](https://learnnatively.com/)** is a community site with user-contributed difficulty gradings and reading logs across manga, novels and other media. Community difficulty grades are opinions aggregated, which is genuinely informative and also systematically noisy. Check the site for its current feature set.
- **The repo's own copy** of the community recommendation spreadsheet is a good offline substitute: the [Manga](<../AJATT/saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations/Manga.png>), [Novels](<../AJATT/saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations/Novels.png>) and [Visual Novels](<../AJATT/saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations/Visual Novels.png>) tabs are colour-banded by difficulty with a review for each entry. Its [front page](<../AJATT/saegusa resources/Japanese Media Recommendations/Front Page.png>) is explicit that the difficulty ratings are reviewers' opinions, sometimes adjusted by the maintainer for consistency - so read them as informed opinion.

Use all three the same way: to generate a shortlist, then **sample three pages yourself and count the unknowns.** Your own count beats anyone's rating.

## Reading speed

Speed is not a vanity metric. It is the difference between finishing books and abandoning them.

Consider a 180-page manga volume. At 3 minutes a page that is 9 hours - three weeks of daily reading for one volume, which is slow enough that you will lose the plot and quit. At 1 minute a page it is 3 hours and you finish it in a week. Same comprehension, completely different outcome. **Speed determines what you are able to read at all**, because long works have a minimum viable pace below which you cannot hold the story in your head.

**Where speed comes from:**

- **Volume, mostly.** Automaticity is a function of exposure. There is no shortcut and no drill that substitutes.
- **Not sub-vocalising every compound.** Beginners internally pronounce every kanji compound before accepting its meaning, and if the reading does not come, they stall. But you do not need the reading to get the meaning - 心配 means worry whether or not しんぱい surfaces. Learn to accept the meaning and keep going; flag the reading for later. (Do not take this too far: if you *never* attach readings, your listening and speaking suffer badly, and you build a silent private version of the language. See [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md).)
- **Not re-reading.** Re-reading a sentence is sometimes necessary and usually a reflex. The extensive slot forbids it for exactly this reason.
- **High coverage.** Every unknown word is a stall. Speed rises sharply as coverage crosses the high-90s, which is another reason the extensive slot must use easy material.
- **Font and layout familiarity.** Vertical text, unfamiliar typefaces and handwriting-style fonts all cost you speed until you have hours in them.

**How to train it:** the timer in the extensive slot is the whole method. Set 15 minutes, read as far as you get, note the page count. Watch the number rise over months. Do not chase it daily - it is noisy - but the monthly trend is real.

## Common pitfalls

**Starting with something far too hard because you love it (the Berserk trap).**
*The mistake:* your first real reading attempt is your favourite dense series.
*Why it feels right:* motivation is the scarce resource, and reading what you love is genuinely good advice - at the right level. Graded readers are boring and this is not.
*The fix:* put the favourite on the ladder at its honest rung and read one page a week of it as a benchmark. Read at your level the rest of the time. You are not giving it up, you are scheduling it.

**Looking up every single word, forever.**
*The mistake:* every unknown gets a lookup, in every session, indefinitely.
*Why it feels right:* Yomitan makes it nearly free, and skipping a word feels like accepting failure.
*The fix:* the two-slot split, with an absolute zero-lookup rule in the extensive slot. Guessing is a trainable skill and lookups are how you avoid training it.

**Reading only with a lookup tool, so you can never read offline.**
*The mistake:* 100% of your reading has a hover dictionary attached.
*Why it feels right:* it is faster and more comfortable, and it feels like pure efficiency.
*The fix:* one weekly unplugged session - physical book or extension disabled, 15-30 minutes. Start it early. It is much less painful at month three than at year two.

**Never re-reading.**
*The mistake:* every session is new material.
*Why it feels right:* re-reading feels like not progressing, and new material feels like progress.
*The fix:* one re-read session a week, of something 1-3 weeks old. It is the cheapest fluency gain in this guide, and it is the only direct measurement of improvement you get.

**Abandoning graded readers as babyish - and then reading nothing for six months.**
*The mistake:* quitting level-controlled material because it is beneath you, with no replacement at your level.
*Why it feels right:* you are an adult with adult taste, and reading about a cat going to the shop is humiliating.
*The fix:* notice that the alternative you actually chose was zero reading, not harder reading. Six weeks of graded readers buys you access to manga. Also: the material in this folder is not childish - *Tanoshiku Yomou* has you reading timetables, travel pamphlets and articles on Japanese marriage.

**Counting pages instead of tracking comprehension.**
*The mistake:* the metric is volume, so 50 pages at 30% understood counts as a good day.
*Why it feels right:* pages are easy to count and comprehension is not, and the community's character-count ladders reinforce it.
*The fix:* keep the volume metric, but add the benchmark-page unknown-word count and a 1-5 comprehension self-rating per session. Volume without comprehension is page-turning.

**Treating manga as your only format, then finding prose impossible.**
*The mistake:* 100% manga for a year, then a light novel that is unreadable despite "good" vocabulary.
*Why it feels right:* manga is enjoyable and it *is* real Japanese, so it feels like it must generalise.
*The fix:* understand what manga was giving you for free - visual context, dialogue scaffolding, short bursts of text, no descriptive prose, no narration - and that prose removes all four at once. Start mixing in prose (light novels, NHK Easy, VN narration) well before you think you need to. Five pages a week is enough to keep the channel open.

**Mining every unknown word until reading becomes card-making.**
*The mistake:* the session's output is 20 cards and 3 pages.
*Why it feels right:* each card is a real word you did not know, and mining is what the immersion method tells you to do.
*The fix:* a hard cap of 2-5 cards per session, and if you exceed it the text is too hard. See [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md) for the pipeline; the cap is a reading rule, not a mining rule.

**Reading without ever listening.**
*The mistake:* text-only study, because text is comfortable and self-paced.
*Why it feels right:* it produces visible progress fast, and reading is genuinely the highest-leverage skill.
*The fix:* passive audio during commutes and chores from day one, plus the NHK Easy audio trick - read the article, then listen to it. See [Listening](../Listening/readme.md).

## Measuring progress

Vibes are useless here, because comprehension feels binary from the inside and is not. Use these, in order of value.

**1. Unknown words per page on a fixed benchmark text, monthly.**
The single best metric. Pick one page of something above your level - one *Berserk* page, one novel page - and never change it. Once a month, read it and count the words you do not know. Write the number and the date in a file. That number falling is the only unambiguous evidence that you are improving, and it is immune to the "am I imagining it" problem.

**2. The unplugged test.**
Read a page with no lookup tool, then rate 1-5 how much you followed. Track it. This measures the thing you actually want - reading ability - rather than reading-with-a-dictionary ability, and it is the metric that reveals tool dependence.

**3. Pages or panels per hour, in the extensive slot.**
Noisy daily, meaningful monthly. Watch the trend. If comprehension is stable and speed is rising, you are doing it right; if speed is rising and comprehension is falling, you are skimming.

**4. Can you finish a volume?**
The most motivating metric and the one that maps best to real capability. A learner who finishes one volume a month is a reader. A learner who is 40 pages into six different volumes is not. Count completions.

**5. Comprehension self-rating on a re-read.**
Re-read something from three months ago and rate it. Easy now? You moved. Still hard? Your material has been too hard the whole time - drop a rung.

**6. Characters read, as a proxy.**
The immersion community tracks total characters read, with [exSTATic](https://github.com/KamWithK/exSTATic) automating the count. It is a useful, motivating number and the milestone ladders built on it live in [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md). Treat it as a proxy: the model that ability is linear in characters consumed is a convenient simplification, and it cannot tell reading from skimming.

**7. Verified comprehension, occasionally.**
The one thing self-directed reading cannot give you is confirmation that you were right. Use the comprehension questions and answer keys in [*Tanoshiku Yomou*](<./Tanoshiku Yomou Read Japanese/Tanoshiku Yomou I.pdf>), or JLPT past papers from [JLPT](../JLPT/readme.md), a few times a year. It is uncomfortable and worth it - most learners overestimate their comprehension, and the only way to find out is to be graded.

**What not to measure:** hours spent, cards added, kanji known, or how many books you own.
