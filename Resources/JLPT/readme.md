# JLPT

> **TL;DR**
> - The JLPT (Japanese-Language Proficiency Test) is a five-level, multiple-choice reading and listening exam. It has **no speaking and no writing section at all** - it measures passive recognition only, and every honest critique of it starts there.
> - It matters if you need a document: visa points, employer screening, school admission. If you need none of those, the certificate buys you close to nothing.
> - Levels run N5 (easiest) to N1 (hardest). N2 is the practical floor for Japanese-language employment; N1 is the level most professional filters ask for.
> - Do not start "JLPT study" at day one. Acquire the language, then sit the exam once you are already comfortably past the level. Prep is a 10-12 week tuning cycle, not a curriculum.
> - Every number on this page about hours, kanji counts and word counts is a **rough community estimate**. The organisers stopped publishing official vocabulary and kanji lists after the 2010 revision. For anything numeric that actually binds you - fees, dates, deadlines, pass marks - check <https://www.jlpt.jp/e/>.

---

## What this is and why it matters

The **Japanese-Language Proficiency Test** (日本語能力試験, *Nihongo Nouryoku Shiken*, "JLPT") is the most widely recognised standardised test of Japanese for non-native speakers. It is administered by the **Japan Foundation** overseas and by **Japan Educational Exchanges and Services (JEES)** inside Japan.

It is offered **twice a year, in July and December**. Both sittings run in Japan. Overseas it varies by city: many locations offer both, but a significant number offer **only the December sitting**, and some sites offer only a subset of levels at a given sitting. If you are studying outside Japan, check your local host institution early - you may effectively have **one shot per year**, which changes your planning completely.

There are five levels, from **N5** (easiest) to **N1** (hardest). There is no level above N1.

### The 2010 revision

This matters because it explains why so much JLPT information online is subtly wrong.

Before 2010 the test had four levels, numbered 4級 (easiest) to 1級 (hardest), and the organisers **published official vocabulary and kanji lists** for each level. In 2010 the test was rebuilt:

- Four levels became five: **N1, N2, N3, N4, N5**. N3 was inserted to bridge the notoriously wide gap between the old level 3 and level 2.
- Scoring moved to a **scaled ("equated") score** rather than a raw percentage, so that scores are comparable across sittings of differing difficulty.
- **Sectional minimum scores** were introduced - see [Scoring](#scoring-the-part-that-actually-trips-people-up).
- The official vocabulary and kanji lists were **discontinued and never replaced.**

That last point is the single most important thing to internalise before you read any other JLPT guide. **There is no official JLPT vocabulary list. There is no official JLPT kanji list.** Every "N2 kanji list" and "N3 vocabulary list" you will ever see - including the ones in this repo - is either derived from the pre-2010 official lists, or reverse-engineered from past papers and commercial textbooks, or straight-up guessed. They are useful as rough gap-checking tools. They are not a syllabus, and treating them as one is the most common way learners waste months.

### Why the test matters anyway

Despite all that, the JLPT is the de facto currency of Japanese proficiency, for a boring reason: it is cheap, standardised, held worldwide, and machine-marked. Nothing else has that combination. So:

- Japan's **points-based Highly Skilled Professional visa** awards points for Japanese language ability, with N1 worth more than N2.
- Job postings at Japanese companies routinely state a JLPT level as a hard requirement.
- Japanese universities and vocational schools commonly use it for admissions.

If you are in this repo because you want to move to Japan and work there, the JLPT is not optional theatre - it is a form you have to fill in. If you are here because you want to read manga, it is optional theatre.

## Where it fits in the journey

**Prerequisites before the exam is even a sensible topic:**

1. Both kana read fluently, no hesitation - see [Kana](../Kana/readme.md).
2. A first pass through core grammar - see [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md). You do not need to have "finished" grammar; you need to not be confused by basic conjugation and particles.
3. Kanji recognition under way as a habit - see [Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md).
4. A vocabulary SRS running - see [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md).

**What each level unlocks** is covered in detail below, but the short version: N5 and N4 unlock nothing external. N3 unlocks some service-sector roles and is a useful psychological checkpoint. **N2 is where the certificate starts doing real work.** N1 is where most professional and immigration doors open.

**What the JLPT does not unlock, ever:** the ability to speak. There is no output on this exam. If your goal involves talking to Japanese people - a job interview, a team standup, a landlord - the JLPT will not prepare you for it and will not measure it. Build that separately via [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md), and expect the gap to be larger than your certificate suggests.

## What the test does and does not measure

This deserves its own section because it is the fulcrum of every strategic decision on this page.

**The JLPT is 100% multiple choice.** Every question has a small set of numbered options and you mark a bubble. There is:

| | On the exam? |
| :--- | :--- |
| Vocabulary recognition | Yes |
| Kanji reading recognition | Yes |
| Grammar recognition | Yes |
| Reading comprehension | Yes |
| Listening comprehension | Yes |
| **Speaking** | **No** |
| **Writing / composition** | **No** |
| **Handwriting kanji** | **No** |
| **Producing a single Japanese word from memory** | **No** |

You never write a Japanese character. You never say a word. You never translate. You never form a sentence. You **select**.

The consequences:

- **It is a recognition test, so recognition training optimises it.** SRS flashcards with a Japanese front and a meaning back are an almost perfect match for the JLPT's demands. This is why immersion learners with big Anki decks often overperform on the JLPT relative to how well they think they know Japanese.
- **It systematically over-reports your ability.** A passing score means "I can pick the right answer from four options under time pressure," which is a strictly weaker claim than "I know this."
- **"N1 but can't hold a conversation" is not a meme, it is the expected outcome** of optimising for this exam in isolation. There is no mechanism in the test that would catch it. Employers know this, which is partly why many of them interview in Japanese regardless of your certificate.
- **Conversely, it is genuinely hard to fake.** The reading passages at N2 and N1 are long, dense and brutally timed. You cannot bluff your way through them with test tricks. The test is a narrow measure, but within its narrow lane it is fairly honest.

## The exam structure per level

Structurally, the test measures three things, which the organisers group as:

1. **Language Knowledge** - 言語知識 - vocabulary, kanji readings, and grammar
2. **Reading** - 読解
3. **Listening** - 聴解

How those are packaged on test day, and how they are packaged for scoring, are two different questions - and mixing them up is where most online guides go wrong.

### Scoring sections

This part is stable and worth learning precisely:

| Level | Scoring sections | Range per section | Total |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **N1** | 3: Language Knowledge (vocab/grammar) / Reading / Listening | 0-60 each | 0-180 |
| **N2** | 3: Language Knowledge (vocab/grammar) / Reading / Listening | 0-60 each | 0-180 |
| **N3** | 3: Language Knowledge (vocab/grammar) / Reading / Listening | 0-60 each | 0-180 |
| **N4** | 2: Language Knowledge (vocab/grammar) **and** Reading combined / Listening | 0-120 and 0-60 | 0-180 |
| **N5** | 2: Language Knowledge (vocab/grammar) **and** Reading combined / Listening | 0-120 and 0-60 | 0-180 |

**Total score is out of 180 at every level.** That is the same across N5 to N1 - what changes is the difficulty of the questions and the pass mark.

The N4/N5 difference is the one to remember: at those levels, language knowledge and reading are **merged into a single reported score**, so a weak reading performance can be carried by strong vocabulary. From **N3 upward, reading is scored on its own** - and that is the section that fails people.

### Papers on the day

On test day the sections are administered as separate timed papers, and **the exact grouping and the exact minute counts have been revised over the years** (notably for the lower levels). Minute-by-minute timings are not restated here, because a stale timing table is worse than no timing table when you are planning a test day.

What is safe to plan around:

- **Listening is always the last paper of the day.** It is played from a single audio track for the whole room.
- Total time in the exam hall scales sharply with level - roughly **an hour and a half at N5 up to around three hours at N1**, before you add check-in, briefing and breaks. Budget most of a morning or afternoon regardless of level.
- There are short breaks between papers, and you cannot move between papers - once the vocabulary paper is collected, it is gone.

**Get the current, official section list and timings for your level from <https://www.jlpt.jp/e/> when you register.** They are published per level, they are authoritative, and they take two minutes to read.

### Question types, in broad strokes

You do not need a taxonomy, but knowing the shape helps:

- **Kanji and vocabulary:** pick the reading of an underlined word; pick the kanji for a word written in kana; pick the word that fits a blank; pick the near-synonym; pick the sentence that uses a word correctly.
- **Grammar:** pick the grammatical form that fits a blank; **sentence ordering** (re-arrange scrambled chunks and report which chunk lands in the starred slot - people lose easy points here purely from misreading the instructions); pick the form that fits a gap in a longer passage.
- **Reading:** short passages, medium passages, long passages, comparison passages (two texts on one topic, at the upper levels), and **information retrieval** (scan an advert, notice or timetable for a specific fact). The information-retrieval questions are usually the fastest points on the paper and they are usually last - which is why running out of time is so expensive.
- **Listening:** task-based comprehension (listen, pick the right action or answer), point comprehension, summary comprehension, and at the upper levels **immediate-response items** where you hear one line and pick the natural reply. Everything is **played exactly once**.

## Scoring: the part that actually trips people up

Two rules decide whether you pass. You need **both**.

**Rule 1 - the overall total.** Your scaled total out of 180 must reach the pass mark for your level.

**Rule 2 - the sectional minimum.** You must **independently** reach a minimum score in **every** scoring section. Clearing the overall total is not enough.

This second rule is the one that catches people, and it catches a very specific person: **the immersion learner with enormous reading ability and neglected listening**, or the classroom learner with textbook grammar and no ear. You can score beautifully on two sections, blow past the overall pass mark, **bomb one section, and fail the exam.** There is no averaging out, no compensation, no appeal.

### The numbers, with appropriate caution

These have been stable since the 2010 revision, but **verify them on <https://www.jlpt.jp/e/> before you plan around them** - they are reported here as commonly published figures, not as something guaranteed current:

| Level | Approximate overall pass mark (of 180) |
| :--- | :--- |
| N1 | around 100 |
| N2 | around 90 |
| N3 | around 95 |
| N4 | around 90 |
| N5 | around 80 |

Yes, **N3's pass mark is commonly listed as higher than N2's**. That is not a typo in this table, and it is not as strange as it looks - the scores are scaled per level, so a "95" at N3 and a "90" at N2 are not measuring the same thing. Do not read across the column and conclude N3 is harder than N2.

Sectional minimums are commonly published as roughly **19 out of 60** for each of the three sections at N1/N2/N3, and roughly **38 out of 120** for the combined language-knowledge-and-reading section plus roughly **19 out of 60** for listening at N4/N5. Again - **confirm on the official site.**

Note what those minimums imply: the sectional bar is *low* in absolute terms. It is not asking you to be good at everything. It is asking you to be **not catastrophically bad** at anything. If you are failing a sectional minimum, you do not have a tuning problem, you have a hole.

### Practical consequences of scaled scoring

- **You cannot compute your score from a mock exam percentage.** Scaling means raw-correct does not map linearly to the reported score, and the mapping is not published. Mock results tell you *direction and margin*, not your score.
- **Because of that, aim well above the bar.** A mock that lands you right at the pass mark is a coin flip, not a pass. Do not sit a level until mocks are landing comfortably clear of it with time left on the clock.
- **Guessing is not penalised.** There is no negative marking. See [Test-taking strategy](#test-taking-strategy).
- **You get a sectional breakdown in your results**, which is genuinely useful feedback even on a fail - it tells you exactly which skill to attack. Results come out online some weeks after the sitting, before the paper certificate arrives; check the official site for the current schedule.

## What each level actually means in practice

The official descriptors are vague on purpose. Here is the practical version. **All kanji and vocabulary figures below are rough community estimates, not official figures** - the organisers publish no such lists, as explained above. Treat them as order-of-magnitude, not targets.

Study-hour figures are **even rougher**. They vary enormously with study quality, immersion volume, and especially with whether you already read Chinese characters - learners with a Chinese background routinely reach the upper levels in a fraction of the hours. Wide ranges below are deliberate.

### N5

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Rough kanji | ~100 |
| Rough vocabulary | ~800 words |
| Rough hours (community estimate) | ~250-450 |
| Roughly equivalent to | finishing a first-semester textbook |

**What you can actually do:** read kana fluently, recognise a small set of kanji, understand short set-phrase conversations spoken slowly and clearly, read simple sentences written largely in kana.

**What becomes accessible:** essentially nothing native. Graded readers at the very bottom rung. Children's material with furigana, with heavy dictionary support.

**Honest assessment:** N5 is a participation certificate. It is worth nothing to an employer and nothing for immigration. Its only real value is as a deadline that forces a beginner to actually study. If you need that, fine. If you do not, skip it.

### N4

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Rough kanji | ~300 |
| Rough vocabulary | ~1,500 words |
| Rough hours (community estimate) | ~500-900 |
| Roughly equivalent to | finishing a two-volume beginner textbook series |

**What you can actually do:** hold simple conversations on familiar everyday topics at slow, clear speed. Read short passages about everyday life. Handle basic polite and plain forms, past/negative/te-form, basic conditionals.

**What becomes accessible:** easy graded readers comfortably. Slice-of-life manga with furigana, slowly, with a dictionary. Children's anime with Japanese subtitles, partially.

**Honest assessment:** this is the end of "textbook Japanese." It is still not externally useful, but it is a meaningful competence threshold - it is roughly where native material stops being a wall and starts being merely difficult. If you want one cheap logistics rehearsal before a level that counts, N4 is a reasonable place to spend it.

### N3

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Rough kanji | ~650 |
| Rough vocabulary | ~3,700 words |
| Rough hours (community estimate) | ~900-1,500 |

**What you can actually do:** follow everyday conversation at close to natural speed when the topic is concrete. Read and roughly understand writing about everyday topics. Get the gist of newspaper headlines. Handle the bulk of practical grammar, including keigo basics and the common compound structures.

**What becomes accessible:** slice-of-life anime becomes genuinely followable with Japanese subtitles. Manga in your favourite genre becomes readable at a slow but real pace. Visual novels with a text hooker become viable. This is the level where **immersion stops being an act of faith and starts being entertainment.**

**Honest assessment:** N3 is the great bridge level and it is where most learners quit. Some service and retail roles list N3. It is not enough for a Japanese-language office job. Its real value is internal: at N3 your input finally outpaces your study, and the whole learning curve changes character.

### N2

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Rough kanji | ~1,000 |
| Rough vocabulary | ~6,000 words |
| Rough hours (community estimate) | ~1,600-2,800 |

**What you can actually do:** read newspapers and general-interest articles and follow the argument. Follow news broadcasts and ordinary TV at natural speed. Understand writing on a broad range of topics, including some abstraction.

**What becomes accessible:** most native media aimed at a general adult audience, with effort. Light novels. Technical documentation in your own field, often quite comfortably, because you already know the concepts.

**Honest assessment:** **this is the level that starts paying.** N2 is very commonly the stated floor for Japanese-language workplaces and a common admissions bar for Japanese institutions. If you are optimising for employability per hour of study, N2 is the highest-value single target on the ladder. It is also, importantly, *achievable* - the jump from N3 to N2 is large but it is not the monster the next one is.

### N1

| | |
| :--- | :--- |
| Rough kanji | ~2,000 - close to, but not the same as, the 2,136-character 常用漢字 set |
| Rough vocabulary | ~10,000 words |
| Rough hours (community estimate) | ~3,000-4,800 |

**What you can actually do:** read logically complex and abstract writing - editorials, criticism, essays, argumentative prose - and follow the structure of the argument, not just the topic. Follow lectures, news and conversation across a wide range of subjects, including some at speed and with less contextual support.

**What becomes accessible:** most adult non-fiction. Serious journalism. Business and technical Japanese in most fields.

#### The N2 to N1 gap, honestly

This is the biggest single jump in the ladder, and it is worth being blunt about why:

- **The vocabulary requirement roughly doubles**, and the new vocabulary is much lower frequency - which means SRS gets less efficient and volume of reading gets more important.
- **The reading changes in kind, not just difficulty.** N1 passages are dense argumentative prose where the questions test whether you followed the writer's *reasoning* - what they are conceding, what they are rejecting, where the turn is. Knowing every word in the passage is necessary and not sufficient.
- **Time pressure becomes the dominant constraint.** At N1, plenty of people who understand the passages still fail because they do not finish.
- **The grammar becomes a long tail** of formal, literary and set expressions that are rare in speech and common in writing. This is the one part of N1 that genuinely does reward test-specific drilling, because you will not meet these often enough in anime to acquire them naturally.

#### N1 is not native-level

Worth stating plainly, because N1 is sometimes described as "fluency" and it is not:

N1 is roughly "can handle general adult non-fiction with effort." It is **not** the ceiling of Japanese and it is not close to educated-native reading ability. Beyond N1 you will still struggle with literature and classical influence, legal and medical text, heavy dialect, and - the one that surprises people most - **casual speech, slang and youth language**, which the JLPT barely tests because it tests standard 標準語 in relatively formal registers. And of course you can hold N1 and still be unable to speak, because the test does not ask you to.

If you want a benchmark beyond N1, the **BJT (Business Japanese Proficiency Test)** is scored on a continuous scale with no ceiling and is used by some employers, and the native-oriented **Kanji Kentei (漢検)** goes far past N1 on kanji specifically. Neither is a JLPT replacement for visa purposes - confirm what any given employer or authority accepts.

## The schools of thought

There are four coherent positions on the JLPT. All four are held by serious people.

### 1. JLPT as the goal

Structure your whole study around the next level. Use level-graded textbooks, work through them in order, sit the exam, move up.

**Pros**
- Removes the hardest problem for a self-directed learner: *what do I do today?*
- External deadline, external validation, real accountability. Enormously effective for people who need a scoreboard.
- Forces **breadth**. You cannot skip the grammar you find boring, and you cannot skip listening. The sectional minimum enforces this.
- Very efficient for producing the specific document you may need.

**Cons**
- You will optimise for the test, because that is what the incentive says. Level-graded textbook Japanese is a register that nobody speaks.
- Rewards passive recognition exclusively. Two years of this produces a person who can pass N2 and cannot order food.
- Level lists are **fabrications** - remember, no official lists exist. You are grinding somebody's guess at a syllabus.
- Creates the "I can't read that yet, it's above my level" reflex, which is corrosive. Native material does not have levels.

**Best for:** learners who need a certificate on a deadline; learners in a formal class; learners who have genuinely tried self-direction and bounced off it.

### 2. JLPT as a byproduct

Acquire the language through massive comprehensible input and sentence mining. Ignore JLPT levels while studying. When you are already comfortably past a level, register for it and pass it with light prep.

**Pros**
- You learn the actual language, in the registers it is actually used in, from material you actually enjoy.
- Output and comprehension develop from the same source, so the "N1 and mute" failure mode is much less likely.
- Prep collapses to a short tuning cycle - familiarisation and timing, not learning.
- Robust to the test changing, because you were never studying the test.

**Cons**
- Requires you to tolerate long periods without measurable progress. Many people cannot, and they quit.
- Immersion produces **lopsided** ability shaped by your media diet. If you read manga and watch subtitled anime, your listening will lag - and the sectional minimum will find it.
- It is slower to a *certificate*, even where it is faster to actual ability. If you need N2 by a specific date, "it'll come" is not a plan.
- The formal and literary grammar at N1 is genuinely under-represented in entertainment media. You will have a hole there.

**Best for:** learners with time and no hard deadline; anyone with a strong media connection to Japanese; anyone who has been burned by textbook boredom.

See [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md) for how to actually run this.

### 3. Ignore the JLPT entirely

Never register. Measure progress by what you can read and understand.

**Pros**
- Zero fees, zero prep time, zero cramming, zero test anxiety. All of it goes into learning instead.
- No incentive distortion at all.
- Honest: your real benchmark becomes "can I read this book," which is a better question than "did I pass."

**Cons**
- If you ever *do* need the document - a job, a visa, a school - you need it on their timeline, and the prep cycle is not instant.
- You lose a genuinely useful external calibration. Self-assessment in language learning is notoriously bad in both directions.
- Nothing forces you to fix your weak skill.

**Best for:** hobbyists with no plausible relocation or employment angle. If you are learning Japanese to enjoy Japanese things and you live somewhere else, this is a completely defensible position and you should not let anyone guilt you out of it.

### 4. JLPT purely instrumentally

You need N2 or N1 on a CV or for immigration points. So you grind it deliberately and cynically - including test-specific tricks you would otherwise consider a waste of time - while keeping your real learning separate.

**Pros**
- Honest about what it is: acquiring a document, not a skill. That clarity prevents self-deception.
- Fastest route to the certificate. Test-specific prep is very high yield *for the test*.
- Keeps your real study uncontaminated, because you never confused the two.

**Cons**
- Requires you to actually *maintain* the separate real study, under deadline pressure. Most people don't - the grind eats everything.
- Grinding something you find pointless is miserable and burnout is a real risk.
- If you grind too far ahead of your ability you can pass a level you cannot back up, then get dismantled in a Japanese-language interview.

**Best for:** people with a concrete, dated external requirement. This is a tool, not a philosophy.

## Recommended path (opinionated)

**Position 2, with a scheduled switch to position 4 at the top of the ladder.**

Concretely:

1. **Do not study for the JLPT at all for your first year or more.** Build kana, grammar, kanji and vocabulary through the normal path in this repo, with heavy immersion. Let level lists be gap-check tools, never syllabi.
2. **Skip N5 entirely.** It costs money and a test day and buys nothing.
3. **Sit N3 once as a rehearsal**, when you are already fairly comfortable at that level. Not for the certificate - for the *logistics*: the registration process, the hall, the answer sheet, the single-play listening, the real feeling of the clock. Do this cheap rehearsal while the stakes are zero, so that the sitting that matters is not also your first.
4. **N2 is the first sitting that counts.** Prepare it properly with a full 10-12 week cycle. This is the highest-value single certificate on the ladder.
5. **For N1, switch to instrumental mode.** Deliberately grind the formal and literary grammar that immersion under-supplies, drill reading under the clock, and accept that a chunk of this is test-specific work with limited real-world payoff. Do it anyway, because N1 is what the professional filters ask for.
6. **Throughout, defend listening.** Reading-heavy immersion plus reading-heavy SRS is the default drift, and the sectional minimum punishes exactly that.

**Ignore this recommendation if:**

- **You have a hard deadline inside 18 months** - visa application, admissions cycle, job offer contingent on a level. Go to position 1 or 4 immediately. Immersion-first is the better road and it is not the faster one, and a road that arrives after the deadline is worth nothing.
- **You have no relocation or employment angle at all.** Go to position 3. You are considering paying money to be told something you could find out by opening a book.
- **You genuinely cannot self-direct.** If you have tried immersion twice and stalled twice, position 1's external structure is worth more than its distortions. A distorted study habit beats no study habit by an enormous margin.
- **You already read Chinese.** Your hours-per-level and your optimal strategy are both different enough that most of the sequencing advice here does not apply to you.

**A worked example of the arithmetic, worth running for any target timeline:** at 10 new cards a day across a typical vocabulary/kanji deck split, a learner adds roughly 3,600 items a year. N1 sits around 10,000 words on the community estimates - so a 10-card-a-day pace does not reach N1 vocabulary in three years from SRS alone, no matter how consistent the reviews are. Closing that gap has to come from immersion volume and a ramped-up mining habit, with the SRS deck as a floor rather than the engine, not from an indefinitely rising new-card count. Anyone planning a specific level-by-date target should run this arithmetic against their own new-card rate before assuming SRS alone will get them there.

## Is it worth it, concretely

The honest answer depends entirely on whether you need a document.

### Immigration

Japan operates a **points-based system for the Highly Skilled Professional visa**, which totals points across categories including academic background, professional experience, annual salary, age, and **Japanese language ability**. Japanese ability contributes points, and **N1 is worth more than N2**.

The point values, the thresholds, and the list of qualifying tests are deliberately not reproduced here. That table is set by immigration policy, it has been revised, and a stale copy in a GitHub readme is exactly how people end up planning around numbers that no longer apply. **Confirm the current table with the Immigration Services Agency of Japan (<https://www.isa.go.jp/en/>) or a qualified immigration adviser.**

The structural points that are stable and worth knowing:

- Language ability is one input among several. It is not a substitute for the salary, experience and education points, and for many engineers those dominate. **You may well qualify without any JLPT at all** - do the arithmetic before you assume N1 is on the critical path.
- Where language points *do* matter is at the margin, and the difference between N2 and N1 can be the difference that crosses a threshold.
- Separate from the points system, some visa categories and some routes have their own Japanese-language evidence requirements, and some accept tests other than the JLPT - the **JFT-Basic** (Japan Foundation Test for Basic Japanese) is used for certain worker categories, for example. Which test is accepted for which purpose is a matter of current regulation, not general knowledge. Check.

Broader immigration, residency and life-in-Japan questions belong to [Culture](../Culture/readme.md) - this section is only the narrow slice of how the certificate interacts with the paperwork.

### Employment

Softer, but more consistently reported:

- **N2 is commonly the practical floor** for roles that expect you to work in Japanese. It appears constantly in job postings as a stated minimum.
- **N1 is asked for by many employers**, particularly for client-facing roles, larger traditional companies, and anything involving Japanese-language documentation or meetings.
- **The English-language tech bubble is real but small.** A subset of international and startup-flavoured companies in Japan operate in English and will hire you with little or no Japanese. It is a genuine option and it is a much narrower market with more competition per role.
- **The certificate gets you past the screen, not the interview.** Recruiters filter on the line in your CV. Then a human talks to you in Japanese. Since the JLPT tested neither speaking nor listening-under-pressure-while-nervous, the screen and the interview are measuring different things, and the gap between them is where people get hurt.

### The honest critique

The "N1 and can't hold a conversation" outcome is common, predictable, and structurally guaranteed by the test design. It is not evidence that the holder is a fraud or that the test is worthless. It is evidence that **a passive recognition test measures passive recognition**, and that people optimise for what is measured.

The correct response is not to dismiss the JLPT. It is to treat the certificate as one axis of a two-axis problem, and to build the other axis - output, spontaneous listening, conversational register - on purpose, because nothing in the JLPT will ever make you do it.

### Timing, if the certificate has a job to do

A practical planning note that is easy to miss: **results take weeks.** If you need a certificate for an application cycle, count backwards from the deadline through the results date to the sitting date to the registration window. For an autumn application cycle the **July sitting** is usually the right one; December results land in the new year. And if your overseas test site only runs December, you have one attempt per year and a failed sitting costs you twelve months. Plan accordingly.

## Phase-by-phase plan

Each phase assumes you are **already at or near the level before you begin prep**. Prep is tuning, not learning. If a diagnostic mock says you are 40 points short, you do not have a prep problem, you have a study problem - go back to [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md), [Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md), [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md) and immersion, and re-enter this plan next cycle.

### Phase 0 - Before any JLPT material (roughly your first year)

**Goal:** acquire enough Japanese that a level becomes a plausible target. No JLPT-specific study whatsoever.

**Time:** however long it takes. For 60-90 minutes a day plus real immersion volume, expect N4-N3 territory somewhere in the region of 12-18 months - and treat that as a very soft estimate.

**Daily routine:** whatever the sibling guides say. SRS reviews, new cards at a sustainable rate, grammar reading, and as much immersion as your day allows.

**Materials:** [Grammar](../Grammar/readme.md), [Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md), [Vocabulary](../Vocabulary/readme.md), [Listening](../Listening/readme.md), [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md).

**Done when:** you can read a page of manga in a familiar genre and follow it without looking up more than a few words per page, **and** you can follow the gist of slice-of-life anime with Japanese subtitles. Not before.

### Phase 1 - N5 and N4

**Goal:** for most readers, **skip these.** They cost a fee and a test day and return nothing external.

**Take N4 only if** one of these is true: you want one cheap logistics rehearsal at minimum stakes; you need documented evidence of *some* Japanese for a school or employer that asks for any level; or you know, honestly, that you need an external deadline to study at all.

**Time:** if you are already at the level, 3-4 weeks of familiarisation is plenty.

**Daily routine:** normal study, plus one timed past-paper section every few days purely to learn the question formats and the answer sheet.

**Materials:** official sample questions on <https://www.jlpt.jp/e/>, plus one practice workbook for the level. Do not buy a series.

**Done when:** you can complete a full mock at the level with time to spare and no sectional score near the minimum.

### Phase 2 - N3, the rehearsal sitting

**Goal:** a real sitting under real conditions, treated as a dress rehearsal. Pass it, but the point is the experience.

**Time:** 6-8 weeks of light prep on top of normal study.

**Daily routine:**
- Keep 100% of your normal SRS and immersion. This phase does **not** replace your study.
- Add roughly 20-30 minutes, 4-5 days a week, of JLPT-specific work: one timed section, then review every wrong answer until you can say *why* it was wrong.
- Once a week, a full timed listening section. This is the section most likely to be your weak point and it is the one you can least afford to discover on test day.

**Materials:**
- Official sample questions and one official practice workbook for N3, from <https://www.jlpt.jp/e/>.
- One "gentle review" series volume for grammar - the So-matome style books are organised into small daily chunks and are pleasant rather than punishing, which is right for a rehearsal level.
- [Coto JLPT Grammar and Vocab Mega List](<../AJATT/Reference Spreadsheets/Coto JLPT Grammar and Vocab Mega List.xlsx>) - filter to N3, scan for grammar points you do not recognise at all, and look those up in [Tae Kim](<../Grammar/Tae Kim Guide to Learning Japanese>) or the [Dictionary of Japanese Grammar](<../Grammar/DoJG grammer guide>).

**Done when:** you have actually sat the exam. Secondary check: your mock totals were comfortably clear of the pass mark with no section within 10 points of its minimum.

### Phase 3 - N2, the sitting that counts

**Goal:** pass with margin. This is the certificate that starts doing work.

**Time:** a full 10-12 week cycle, on top of continued immersion. See [the 12-week cycle](#the-12-week-prep-cycle) below.

**Daily routine:**
- Normal SRS and immersion continue, non-negotiable.
- 30-45 minutes of JLPT work, 5-6 days a week.
- Shift your immersion diet deliberately toward **non-fiction prose** - news, articles, blog posts, essays. N2 reading is nothing like anime dialogue, and anime immersion alone will leave you unprepared for the register even when you know all the words.
- Keep a dedicated Anki deck for grammar points you miss on mocks. Mined from your own errors, this is the single highest-yield deck in a prep cycle.

**Materials:**
- **Official practice workbook** for N2 - the closest thing to a real paper that the organisers publish.
- **A full series** for N2. Pick one and finish it rather than sampling three. Shin Kanzen Master is the dense, demanding option with separate volumes per skill - grammar, reading, listening, vocabulary, kanji - and it is the standard recommendation for people who want to be *over*prepared. The So-matome family is the gentler, chunked-review option, better if you are already near the level and want structured revision rather than instruction. Try! is grammar-centred and more textbook-like, with audio, and works well as a first pass for someone who finds the Kanzen Master books grim. **Note:** "Kanzen Master" without the *Shin* ("new") is the older pre-revision edition - make sure you are buying the current one.
- [Coto JLPT Grammar and Vocab Mega List](<../AJATT/Reference Spreadsheets/Coto JLPT Grammar and Vocab Mega List.xlsx>), filtered to N2, as a gap-check.
- Past papers for volume - see the [Resources](#resources) table.

**Done when:** three consecutive full timed mocks land clear of the pass mark, **with every section clear of its minimum by a real margin**, and you finished the reading section without rushing the last passage. If you pass on total but scrape a section, you are not ready - fix the hole.

### Phase 4 - N1, instrumental mode

**Goal:** the certificate. Accept that a meaningful share of this work is test-specific.

**Time:** a 12-week cycle minimum, and realistically two cycles - many people sit N1 twice. Budget for that emotionally in advance.

**Daily routine:**
- Immersion continues, now heavily weighted toward **dense argumentative and expository prose**. Opinion columns, essays, criticism, long-form journalism. If your reading diet is fiction and manga, N1 reading will hurt regardless of your vocabulary.
- 45-60 minutes of JLPT work, 6 days a week.
- **Formal and literary grammar as an explicit deck.** This is the honest concession to instrumentalism: the N1 grammar tail is rare in speech, common in formal writing, and under-supplied by entertainment immersion. Drill it deliberately.
- **Timed reading, every single day.** At N1 the binding constraint is speed. Practise reading passages against a clock from week one, not week ten.
- Listening at natural speed with no subtitles, from varied sources - not just the anime voices your ear is calibrated to.

**Materials:**
- Official practice workbook for N1.
- Shin Kanzen Master N1, particularly the reading and grammar volumes. At this level the demanding option is the correct option.
- [JLPT N1 Vocabulary Anki deck](<../Vocabulary/JLPT N1 Vocabulary/JLPT_N1_Vocabulary.apkg>) - a pre-made N1 word list. Use it as a **late-stage gap-filler**, not as your main vocabulary source. Pre-made level decks are low-context and mediocre for acquisition; they are decent for sweeping up the low-frequency tail you will not meet often enough in the wild.
- [Dictionary of Japanese Grammar](<../Grammar/DoJG grammer guide>) for the intermediate and advanced entries - this is the right reference for the formal structures N1 loves.
- Every past paper you can get.

**Done when:** mocks land clear of the pass mark with all sections safe, **and** you are consistently finishing the reading section with a few minutes spare. The second condition is the one that fails N1 candidates. Treat it as the real gate.

## The 12-week prep cycle

A single reusable structure. It scales to any level by changing the materials, not the shape.

| Weeks | Focus | What you actually do |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1** | **Diagnostic** | Sit one **full, untimed-but-tracked** mock at your target level. Mark it. Record your per-section performance. Do not study for this - the point is a clean baseline. |
| **2** | **Triage** | Analyse the diagnostic properly. Which section is weakest in *relative* terms? Within it, which question types? Write the answer down. This document is your syllabus for the next eight weeks. |
| **3-6** | **Targeted gap work** | Attack the weakest areas only. Grammar points you did not recognise, vocabulary holes, the specific question formats you fumbled. **Untimed** - you are learning, not racing. Mine every error into a personal deck. |
| **7-9** | **Timed sections** | Single sections against the clock, one at a time. Learn your per-question pace. This is where you build the internal timer that stops you over-investing in one hard passage. Continue gap work on whatever the timed sections expose. |
| **10-11** | **Full mocks** | Two or three complete mocks under real conditions: full length, correct section order, listening played once, no pausing, no dictionary. Review each one thoroughly before the next. |
| **12** | **Taper** | No new material. Light review of your error deck, one final listening session, sleep properly. **Do not cram the week before** - you are trading alertness for a handful of low-frequency words, and alertness is worth more on a heavily timed exam. |

**Rules for the cycle:**

1. **Immersion does not stop.** The cycle is additive. If you drop immersion for twelve weeks of test prep you will pass the test and go backwards in the language.
2. **Mocks are scarce - do not waste them.** There are only so many real papers. Burning them all in week three leaves you nothing for week ten. Use workbook exercises for practice and save full papers for full mocks.
3. **Review is where the value is.** An unmarked, unanalysed mock is worth almost nothing. Budget as long for the review as for the paper itself, and for each wrong answer write down *why* the right answer is right - "I guessed and got lucky" counts as wrong.
4. **If the week-1 diagnostic is a long way short, abort the cycle.** Do not spend twelve weeks failing. Go back to acquisition and re-enter next sitting. Registering for a level you cannot reach is how people waste a year.

## Daily and weekly routine

A concrete copy-able schedule for a prep cycle, sized for someone with **60-90 minutes a day of active study** plus passive immersion while working. Adjust the split, keep the shape.

### Daily

| Block | Time | What |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Morning | 15-25 min | SRS reviews - sentence bank, vocabulary, kanji. Reviews first, always. Never skip reviews to do prep. |
| Morning | 5-10 min | New cards at your sustainable rate, plus any error-deck cards mined from yesterday's mock review. |
| Working day | passive | Passive immersion - audio in the background. Costs nothing, compounds. See [AJATT](../AJATT/readme.md). |
| Evening | 25-40 min | **The JLPT block.** Content varies by cycle week per the table above: gap work in weeks 3-6, timed sections in weeks 7-9, review in weeks 10-12. |
| Evening | 15-30 min | Active immersion in the register your target level tests. Anime and manga for N3. **News, articles and essays for N2 and N1.** |

### Weekly

| Day | Addition |
| :--- | :--- |
| Mon-Thu | Standard daily routine. |
| Fri | **Timed listening section.** Full length, played once, no pausing. Defend this slot - listening is the section most likely to sink a reading-heavy learner. |
| Sat | **Long block, 90-120 min.** Full timed section or, in weeks 10-11, a complete mock. |
| Sun | **Review and planning, 45-60 min.** Mark the week's work, mine errors into the error deck, and update your triage notes. Then stop. Take the rest of the day off. |

**The two non-negotiables:** reviews happen every day even when nothing else does, and the weekly review block actually happens. A prep cycle where you do the papers and never analyse them is a prep cycle where you learn almost nothing.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Studying "for the JLPT" from day one.** You will learn a graded dialect of Japanese that exists only in textbooks, build a "that's above my level" reflex about native material, and arrive at N2 unable to speak. Acquire the language; sit the exam as a checkpoint.
2. **Treating unofficial level lists as a syllabus.** There are no official lists. You would be memorising someone's reverse-engineered guess, in frequency order that has nothing to do with what you will actually read. Use lists to *find holes*, never to *define scope*.
3. **Ignoring the sectional minimum until it kills you.** The classic: brilliant reading, neglected listening, comfortable total, failed exam. Check your weakest section against its minimum in week 1 of every cycle, not week 10.
4. **Registering for a level you cannot reach.** Hope is not a diagnostic. Sit a mock *before* you register. If you are far short, register for the level below or skip the sitting - especially if your site only runs one sitting a year.
5. **Running out of time in reading.** The most common way to fail N2 and N1 while actually understanding the material. Practising reading untimed builds a habit that the exam punishes. Get a clock involved early.
6. **Leaving blanks.** There is no negative marking. A blank is a guaranteed zero; a guess is free expected value. Leaving a single blank is a pure, unforced error.
7. **Over-investing in one hard reading passage.** Every question is worth roughly its share; the hard passage is not worth three easy ones. Learn your per-question budget in weeks 7-9 and obey it on the day.
8. **Assuming listening will replay.** It will not. If you miss an item, drop it instantly and lock on to the next one. Ruminating on a lost question costs you the following question too - this is how one miss becomes four.
9. **Cramming the final week.** Trading sleep and alertness for a handful of low-frequency words, on an exam where the binding constraint is speed under pressure. Bad trade. Taper.
10. **Stopping immersion during prep.** Twelve weeks of workbooks with no contact with real Japanese will cost you more fluency than the certificate is worth.
11. **Believing your own certificate.** N1 is not fluency and N2 is not professional competence. Both are passive-recognition benchmarks. Check your actual ability against actual Japanese people and actual Japanese material, regularly.
12. **Mistaking "sat the paper" for "prepared."** An unreviewed mock teaches you almost nothing. The learning is in the error analysis.
13. **Buying three series and finishing none.** Pick one series per level and complete it. Breadth of materials is not a strategy.
14. **Forgetting how long results take.** If the certificate has a job to do by a date, count backwards through the results release. People miss application cycles over this.

## Measuring progress

Vibes are useless here, and the JLPT's scaled scoring means even your mock percentages are only loosely informative. Use these instead.

### Full mock exams

The best signal available, and the only one that tests the thing that actually fails people: **all sections, in order, under time, in one sitting.**

Sources, best to worst:

1. **Official practice workbooks** (公式問題集) - published by the organisers. Not a full paper's worth of questions per volume, but the calibration is authoritative. A couple of volumes have been released per level over the years.
2. **Official sample questions** on <https://www.jlpt.jp/e/> - free, short, and the definitive reference for current question formats. Read these before your first sitting at any level, regardless.
3. **Real past papers.** The organisers **do not routinely publish full past exams** - this is a genuine and often-misunderstood limitation of JLPT prep, and it is why the official workbooks exist. Real papers from past sittings do circulate unofficially; see the archive linked in the [Resources](#resources) table.
4. **Commercial mock books** - varying quality. Useful for volume in weeks 7-9. Do not calibrate your readiness off them; some are noticeably easier or harder than the real thing.

**Rules:** full length, correct order, listening played exactly once, no pausing, no dictionary, no phone. A mock you paused is a practice exercise, not a measurement.

### Scoring your mocks

You cannot compute your scaled score, so do not pretend to. Track instead:

- **Raw correct per section**, as a fraction. Watch the trend across mocks, not the absolute number.
- **Your weakest section's margin.** The number that matters most, because it is the one that can fail you outright.
- **Time remaining at the end of reading.** Track this explicitly, every mock. Negative or zero is a red flag even if your score looks fine - it means you got lucky on question distribution.
- **Guess rate.** How many answers were real versus coin flips? A "pass" built on 30% guessing is not a pass, it is variance.

Because scaling is opaque, **aim for a comfortable margin, not the bar.** Landing exactly on the published pass mark in mocks is a coin flip on the day.

### Non-exam self-checks

Worth more than mock scores for knowing whether you actually have the level:

- **Read a native text cold**, no dictionary, at the register your target level tests - a news article for N2, an opinion column for N1. Can you state the author's argument afterwards? Not the topic - the *argument*.
- **Watch unsubtitled content at natural speed** on an unfamiliar topic. Not your comfort anime. A news segment, a documentary, a podcast between two people who are not enunciating for you.
- **The kanji fluency check** described in [Kanji](../Kanji/readme.md) - can you distinguish and recall characters you have seen only once?
- **The output check the JLPT will never give you:** have a real conversation, or write a few paragraphs unaided. This will not move your JLPT score and it is the most honest information you will get about your actual Japanese. Do it anyway. See [Speaking](../Speaking/readme.md) and [Writing](../Writing/readme.md).

## Test-taking strategy

The JLPT is a **timed recognition exam**, and it rewards exam skill more than most people expect. Worth a few hours of deliberate attention.

### Time is the real enemy

Especially reading, especially at N2 and N1. Plenty of people who understand every passage still fail because they do not finish.

- **Work out your per-question budget before the day** - total section time divided by question count, roughly. Internalise it in weeks 7-9 of your cycle so you do not have to compute it under stress.
- **Consider doing the fast questions first.** Information-retrieval items (scan an advert or notice for one fact) are usually among the quickest points on the paper and are usually positioned late. If you grind through the long passages in printed order and run out of clock, you lose the *easiest* marks on the paper. That is the worst possible way to lose points.
- **Cap your time per hard passage and enforce it.** Mark it, guess, move on, come back if there is time. There usually isn't, and that is fine - one hard passage is not worth three easy ones.
- **Do not re-read passages for certainty.** Re-reading is the single biggest time sink. First careful pass, answer, move.

### Guessing

**There is no negative marking.** A wrong answer scores the same as a blank: zero. Therefore:

- **Never leave a blank. Not one.** Not on a question you did not read. If the clock is dying, spend the last thirty seconds filling every remaining bubble with anything at all.
- Eliminate obviously wrong options first - even going from four options to two is a real improvement in expected value.
- **Mark your guesses** with a tiny tick on the question paper so you know where to return and so your post-mock review is honest about what you actually knew.

### Listening

- **It is played once.** For the whole room, from one track. There is no pause, no rewind, no individual control.
- **Read the options during the pause before the audio**, where the format allows it. Knowing what you are listening *for* transforms the item.
- **Take minimal notes.** Writing prose costs you the next sentence. Jot single kanji, numbers, arrows.
- **If you lose an item, abandon it instantly.** The most expensive listening mistake is not missing one question - it is ruminating on the one you missed while the next two play. Drop it. Move on. Breathe.
- **Mark answers on the sheet as you go**, not at the end. There is often little or no time at the end of listening, and "I'll transfer them after" has cost people the section.

### Section order and the day itself

- **You cannot revisit a collected paper.** Finish each paper's answer sheet before it goes.
- **Listening is last.** Which means you are tired for the section with no replay. Factor this into your mocks - always practise listening *after* the reading section, never fresh, or you will be calibrating against a version of yourself that will not be there on the day.
- **Use the breaks.** Water, bathroom, stand up, stop thinking about the last paper. Ruminating between papers is pure cost.

### The answer sheet

Mundane and occasionally fatal:

- **Mark the bubbles properly** - filled in, dark, a soft pencil. Bring more than one pencil and an eraser.
- **Check your row alignment periodically.** A one-row offset that you notice with two minutes left is a catastrophe. Check at the end of each question block, not at the end of the paper.
- **Fill in your candidate details carefully** at the start. Every sitting has someone who does not.
- **Sentence-ordering questions ask which chunk goes in the starred slot**, not for the whole sequence. Read the instruction once, properly, in advance - free points are lost here every sitting.

## Logistics

Structural only. **Fees, dates, deadlines and eligibility change every year, so this guide deliberately does not restate them** - get them from <https://www.jlpt.jp/e/>, which links to the in-Japan registrar (JEES) and to the overseas host institution for each city.

**Registration**

- **Windows open months ahead of the sitting and close months ahead of it.** Missing the deadline means waiting for the next sitting - which, if your site only runs December, means a full year.
- **Popular overseas sites fill up.** Capacity is finite and some cities sell out quickly. Register on the day the window opens, not on the day you feel ready.
- **Overseas availability varies by city**: both sittings or December only, and sometimes only a subset of levels. Confirm your local site's actual offering before you build a plan around a July sitting.
- **You choose your own level with no prerequisite.** You do not need N5 to sit N1. You can also skip levels entirely.
- **You register for exactly one level per sitting.** You cannot hedge across two.
- There is no penalty for failing, and **you can retake as often as you like**.

**On the day**

- **Photo ID and your test voucher are required.** Requirements are specified by the host institution - read what they send you rather than assuming.
- **Bring:** the voucher, ID, several pencils (soft, suitable for the answer sheet), an eraser, and a simple analogue watch if permitted - phones are put away and the hall clock may not be where you can see it. Confirm what is and is not allowed with your site.
- **Arrive early.** Doors close, and late arrival at some sites means no entry.
- **Phones and electronics are put away** for the duration.

**After**

- **Results are published online some weeks after the sitting**, before the paper certificate arrives by post.
- You get a **per-section score breakdown**, not just pass or fail - keep it. Even on a fail it tells you precisely which skill to attack in the next cycle, which is the most useful piece of study advice you will get all year.
- JLPT certificates are not issued with an expiry date, but **individual employers, schools and authorities may apply their own recency expectations** - do not assume a ten-year-old N2 carries the same weight as a recent one.

### On prep material series

Categories rather than a shopping list, since availability and editions vary:

- **Official practice workbooks** (公式問題集) - from the organisers. The most authoritative calibration you can buy, but each volume is less than a full paper's worth of questions. Buy these first at any level you are serious about.
- **Shin Kanzen Master** (新完全マスター) - dense, demanding, one volume per skill per level (grammar, reading, listening, vocabulary, kanji). The standard choice for grinding and for being overprepared. Note that **"Kanzen Master" without *Shin*** is the older pre-2010-revision edition - check which one you are buying.
- **So-matome family** (総まとめ style) - organised into small daily chunks over a fixed number of weeks. Gentler, review-oriented, good when you are already near the level and want structure rather than instruction. Not enough on its own if you have real gaps.
- **Try!** - grammar-centred and textbook-like, with audio. A reasonable first pass for someone who finds the Kanzen Master books joyless, and a weaker choice as your only material at N1.
- **Commercial mock exam books** - variable quality and variable difficulty calibration. Useful for volume in the timed-sections phase; do not judge your readiness by them.

Pick one main series per level and **finish it**. Owning three and finishing none is the most common materials mistake in JLPT prep.
