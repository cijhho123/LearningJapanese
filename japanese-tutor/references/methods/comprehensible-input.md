# Method: Comprehensible input

**Premise (Krashen-derived):** acquisition happens by understanding messages
slightly above your current level. Low anxiety, high volume, minimal explicit
study.

## What this biases toward

- `graded-passage` above everything else - generated fresh, constrained to the
  learner's known-word set plus 3-5 new items
- Free retell in English rather than comprehension questions
- Listening and reading volume; explanation only when comprehension actually breaks
- Audio resources from the active set

## Teaching stance

**Mostly do not ask.** This is the one profile where questioning works against the method: the premise is understanding messages with low friction, and stopping to quiz every unfamiliar word breaks exactly the flow that does the work. Let them read or listen; explain when comprehension genuinely breaks, not before.

## Sequencing rule

Keep the passage at roughly 95% known words. Below ~90% it stops being
acquisition and becomes decoding; above ~98% nothing new is happening. (Same
thresholds `mine` reports - they are conventions, not cliffs.)

New items enter through the passage, not from a list.

## Why this method belongs in an AI tutor at all

Every static graded reader is written for an average learner who does not exist.
You can generate text constrained to *this* learner's exact known-word set, in a
domain they care about, at any length. That is a genuine capability advantage,
not a repackaging.

## What it deliberately does not do

- No grammar drilling
- No production demands - this method says forced output is counterproductive
- No SRS grinding

## Honest limits - be straight about these

- **Kanji does not come free from listening.** Reading has to be trained
  separately whatever the input purists say.
- **Pure input produces people who understand a lot and cannot speak.** The
  evidence for the strong form of the input hypothesis is weak, and the
  interaction/feedback side of acquisition matters.
- **Incidental acquisition is slow, and worse than the usual figure suggests.**
  Roughly 5-15% of unknown words are picked up per encounter on an immediate
  test, and retention decays hard: Waring & Takaki (2003) found about 1 of 25
  target words still known after three months, and essentially nothing for words
  met fewer than eight times. Volume and repeated encounters are the whole
  mechanism - fine as a supplement, slow as a sole engine.

Treat this as the low-friction, high-volume layer, not the whole programme.
