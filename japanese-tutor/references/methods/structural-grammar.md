# Method: Structural grammar walkthrough

**Premise:** most English-language Japanese teaching distorts the language by
forcing it into English categories. Teach the actual structure and a lot of
"exceptions" stop being exceptions.

## The structural lens

Useful framings, drawn from the Cure Dolly / organic-Japanese tradition and from
standard Japanese linguistics:

- **が marks the grammatical subject**, and every sentence has one - often
  invisible (the zero pronoun). 象は鼻が長い: 鼻 is the subject, 象 is the topic.
- **は is not a subject marker.** It is a topic marker sitting on top of, and
  hiding, whatever case-marked element it replaced.
- **い-adjectives** conjugate; they are verb-like, not "adjective + to be". な-adjectives are the opposite - nominal, and they need the copula (静かだ / 静かではない / 静かだった). Never merge the two, or the learner produces `静かくない`.
- A relative clause is just a sentence used as an adjective, with a zero pronoun
  in the gap.
- Most "irregular" conjugation is regular once you see the real rule.

## Where this lens is wrong - do not skip this

The strong claim that が always marks the *actor* does not hold. With stative
predicates the が-argument is the thing known, wanted or liked, not the one
doing it: 水が飲みたい, 日本語が分かる, ピアノができる, 猫が好き. Whether you
call that an object (Kuno) or the subject of an intransitive predicate is an
analytical choice - don't teach either label as settled. What matters practically
is that the が-argument is not the agent.

And producing 私が where 私は is natural sounds immediately foreign.

So: use it to **explain** why a sentence parses. Never use it to **generate**
target output. Show the underlying structure, then immediately show the natural
surface form.

## Teaching stance

**Ask hard.** The learner parses before being told, every time. This is the method - handing over the analysis defeats the entire point of it.

## What this biases toward

- `grammar-parse`, `grammar-judgment`, `grammar-transformation`
- Working linearly through an active grammar resource at its stored position
- Explaining *why*, not just *what*

## Sequencing rule

Follow the active grammar resource's own order, but never introduce a point
whose prerequisites are shaky. Verb class before て-form; て-form before ている,
てある, ておく, てしまう, てあげる/くれる/もらう.

## What it deliberately does not do

- Does not chase vocabulary volume
- Does not assume exposure alone will deliver the pattern

## Failure mode

Explanation is addictive for both of you. It feels like progress and produces
none on its own. If a session has become all explanation and no retrieval, stop
and make them produce something.
